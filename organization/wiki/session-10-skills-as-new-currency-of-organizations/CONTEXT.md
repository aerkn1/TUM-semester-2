# CONTEXT: Session 10 Skills As The New Currency Of Organizations

Source note: `organization/wiki/session-10-skills-as-new-currency-of-organizations/session-10-skills-as-new-currency-of-organizations.md`

Purpose: standalone terminology, decision language, formula-free mental models, and exam traps for the guest lecture on skills-powered organizations.

## Job And Skill Architecture Terms

| Term | Definition | Aliases to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| **Skills-powered organization** | Organization that uses explicit skill data and proficiency evidence to guide work design, hiring, pay, development, career mobility, and talent deployment. | "Skills database" when the design affects decisions; "HR tool" if the organizational-design role matters |
| **Job architecture** | Structured classification of roles, job families, levels, grades, career streams, and role relationships. It makes work comparable across the organization. | Organization chart; job list |
| **Skill architecture** | Structured classification of competencies, skills, skill categories, and proficiency levels required to perform work. | Training catalog; competency buzzword list |
| **Job function** | Highest-level category of related work types, such as Engineering, HR, Legal, or Sales. | Department if the classification is capability-based rather than reporting-line-based |
| **Job family** | Group of roles requiring related capabilities and career logic, such as backend engineering or HR business partnering. | Random title cluster |
| **Job level** | Hierarchical tier of responsibility, complexity, autonomy, and influence. | Job grade |
| **Job grade** | Pay/value classification that indicates a role's relative compensation range or value band. | Job level |
| **Career stream** | Progression path type, such as expert, management, project, support, or executive. | Promotion ladder only |
| **Role profile** | One-page summary of purpose, responsibilities, accountabilities, and expected competencies for a role. | Job ad |
| **Skill profile** | Structured list of core, functional/job-family, and role-specific skills plus expected proficiency levels. | CV; personal trait list |

## Competency And Proficiency Terms

| Term | Definition | Aliases to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| **Skill** | Specific practical ability or knowledge that can be learned, demonstrated, and applied, such as API design or data analysis. | Talent; personality |
| **Competency** | Broader ability to perform a role by integrating skills, knowledge, behavior, and judgment. | Skill when the concept is broader |
| **Core competency** | Company-wide expected behavior or capability relevant for all employees. Use few, memorable items. | Generic value if it does not guide behavior |
| **Functional competency** | Capability required for a specific function, such as finance, HR, IT, or sales. | Core competency |
| **Job-family competency** | Capability shared by roles in a job family regardless of department. | Role-specific skill |
| **Role-specific skill** | Concrete skill that distinguishes one role from adjacent roles. | Core skill |
| **Proficiency level** | Observable behavioral depth of a skill, from basic guided work to expert standard-setting. | Seniority label without behavior |
| **Dreyfus model** | Skill-acquisition model moving from novice rule-following to expert pattern recognition and field-shaping judgment. | Years-of-experience scale |
| **Observable behavior** | Concrete action that shows skill depth in context. | "Understands X"; "has knowledge of X" |

## People And Governance Terms

| Term | Definition | Aliases to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| **Role identity** | Part of self-concept built around a professional role, such as "I am a software engineer." Skill-based language can threaten or enrich it. | Job title only |
| **Autonomy** | Motivational need to experience meaningful choice and agency. Skill transparency supports it only if employees can act on the information. | Independence without support |
| **Competence** | Motivational need to feel capable and growing. Skill gaps must be paired with learning support. | Performance score |
| **Relatedness** | Motivational need for belonging and trust. Skill rankings can weaken it if they create internal competition. | Team spirit only |
| **Employability** | Employee's ability to show and use capabilities across roles, projects, and organizations. | Job security |
| **Skill transparency** | Visible criteria for what skills matter and how they are assessed. It can enable fairness or surveillance. | Objectivity by default |
| **Algorithmic skill matching** | AI-supported matching of people, roles, projects, and career paths based on skill data. | Neutral allocation |
| **Contestability** | Ability for employees to challenge, correct, or contextualize skill data and algorithmic decisions. | Feedback optionality |

## Regulatory Anchors For The Lecture Context

These are not Business Law exam rules. Use them only to explain why transparent criteria matter for organizational design.

| Anchor | Verified function | Organization exam use |
|---|---|---|
| **EU Pay Transparency Directive (EU) 2023/970** | Strengthens equal-pay application through pay transparency and enforcement; employers may need objective, gender-neutral, bias-free criteria for pay differences. Verified from EUR-Lex, 2026-07-11: `https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2023/970/oj`. | Explains why role value, pay criteria, and skill evidence need to be transparent and defensible. |
| **CSRD / Directive (EU) 2022/2464** | Requires sustainability reporting and common standards; social factors include training and skills development disclosures under the reporting-standards logic. Verified from EUR-Lex, 2026-07-11: `https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2022/2464/oj`. | Explains why workforce skill data can become a compliance and management asset. |
| **EU Gender Balance Directive** | The slide names gender-balance pressure as a driver for transparent criteria. Treat this as source-deck context unless a legal exam asks for detail. | Connects skills criteria to fairness, representation, and bias-aware governance. |

## Relationships Between Canonical Terms

- **Business strategy** should drive **job architecture** and **skill architecture**, not the other way around.
- **Job architecture** classifies work and value; **skill architecture** classifies capabilities and depth.
- **Proficiency levels** turn a **skill profile** into evidence for learning, pay, performance, and career decisions.
- **Skill transparency** supports **employability** when paired with learning investment and appealable criteria.
- **Algorithmic skill matching** requires **contestability** because skill data can be incomplete, biased, or context-poor.

## Mental Model

```text
Strategy -> work design -> job architecture + skill architecture
         -> HR decisions -> employee consequences
         -> governance question: empowerment or surveillance?
```

## Example Dialogue

Manager: "We need a skill profile for Backend Engineer."

HR lead: "Then we should separate layers. Core competencies apply to everyone, engineering skills define the job family, and API/payment reliability skills distinguish this role."

Employee representative: "What happens if the profile says I am weak in one skill?"

HR lead: "Then the framework must trigger development support and allow correction of context. If it only affects pay without appeal, it becomes surveillance."

## Exam Traps And Correction Rules

| Trap | Correction rule |
|---|---|
| Treating job architecture as an org chart | Say it classifies work, level, value, and career progression; reporting lines are only one input. |
| Confusing job level and job grade | Level = responsibility/complexity; grade = pay/value band. |
| Defining skill levels as "knows / understands / expert" | Use behavior: "does X independently in context Y with outcome Z." |
| Calling skill systems objective | Ask who chose the criteria, whether invisible work is valued, and whether employees can contest data. |
| Ignoring identity | Explain that roles are part of self-concept; skill language needs change management. |

## Compact Cheat-Sheet

```text
Job architecture: What roles exist, how they relate, and how they are valued.
Skill architecture: What capabilities are needed and how deeply they must be performed.
Proficiency: Observable behavior, not vague knowledge.
Value: clearer career paths, fairer pay, better talent deployment.
Risk: identity threat, surveillance, biased criteria, development burden.
Exam stance: skills empower only when governance and learning support are built in.
```
