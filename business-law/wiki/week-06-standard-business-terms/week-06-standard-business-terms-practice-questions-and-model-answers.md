## Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 1

What are Standard Business Terms (SBT), and what is the statutory test for their existence?

**Model Answer:**

1. Standard Business Terms are pre-formulated contract terms that one party (the user) presents to the other party upon conclusion of a contract, intended for repeated use.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 305 I BGB.
3. Requirements: (1) a contract term shaping rights or duties, written or spoken; (2) pre-formulated before contract conclusion; (3) intended for repeated use, with a practical threshold of at least three contracts (though Section 310 III No. 2 BGB pulls in even first-use clauses against a consumer); (4) presented unilaterally by the user without real influence from the other party.
4. Example: a webshop's standard delivery-and-returns policy presented on every checkout page.
5. Consequence/trap: the classification question is separate from the fairness question — a clause can be SBT and still be perfectly valid, or perfectly ordinary-looking and still fail content control. Do not skip straight to "is this unfair."

### Question 2

Compare a surprising clause under Section 305c I BGB with a clause that fails content control under Sections 307-309 BGB.

**Model Answer:**

A surprising clause triggers when the clause is so unusual in placement or content, given the circumstances, that the other party could not reasonably expect it — anchored in Section 305c I BGB — and the legal consequence is that the clause never becomes part of the contract at all (non-incorporation). Content control triggers when a clause is clear and was properly incorporated, but its substance unreasonably disadvantages the other party or deviates too far from statutory defaults — anchored in Sections 307-309 BGB — and the legal consequence is that the clause is ineffective (invalid), with the rest of the contract surviving under Section 306 BGB. Contrast example: a liability waiver hidden inside an unrelated "delivery logistics" paragraph is a surprise problem; the same waiver clearly labeled under a "Liability" heading, but excluding liability even for gross negligence, is a content-control problem. Exam trap: do not label a clearly worded but harsh clause as "surprising" — reserve that word for placement/content shock, and route harsh-but-clear clauses to 307-309 instead.

### Question 3

List the requirements for effective incorporation of SBT into a contract under Section 305 II BGB.

**Model Answer:**

The requirements are:
1. The user points the other party to the SBT (or, where explicit reference is impractical, gives notice by other adequate means, e.g. a clearly visible sign).
2. The user gives the other party a reasonable opportunity to take notice of the content.
3. The other party agrees to the SBT's application (agreement can be implied by conduct, e.g. proceeding with the transaction).

If any one of these three is missing, the SBT never becomes part of the contract. The reason this rule exists is that a party who never had a fair chance to know or object to hidden terms should not be bound by them — actual reading is not required, only a genuine opportunity to read.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 4 (Case)

**Facts:** Online furniture retailer F sells a dining table to consumer C through its website. At checkout, a "Terms of Sale" document is pre-formulated by F, used for every online order, and presented via a mandatory checkbox that C must tick before paying. The document is F's first time using this exact wording, since F just redesigned its checkout flow last week. Clause 9 states: "F excludes any and all liability for damage arising from delivery, including liability for gross negligence, except where caused by intent." The table arrives with a deep gouge caused by F's driver carelessly dragging it across gravel — a case of gross negligence by F's own staff.

**Question:** Can F rely on Clause 9 to exclude liability for the gouge damage?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: F might have used effective Standard Business Terms excluding liability for the gouge damage under Sections 305 et seq. BGB, unless the clause fails content control.

II. Requirements:
III. Existence of SBT
IV. Incorporation
V. Content control
VI. Legal consequence

III. Existence of SBT: Questionable is whether the Terms of Sale qualify as SBT despite being used for the first time. This is supported by Section 310 III No. 2 BGB, which treats even a first-use, pre-formulated clause as SBT when presented to a consumer. Against this, one could argue novelty suggests individual drafting rather than repeated use. However, the document was drafted in advance for the entire class of online orders, not negotiated with C specifically. Consequently, the Terms of Sale are SBT.

IV. Incorporation: Questionable is whether Clause 9 became part of the contract. This is supported by F pointing C to the document, giving C the opportunity to read it before the mandatory checkbox, and C's active agreement by ticking the box. Against this, one could argue the clause is buried in a long document, but nothing in the facts suggests unusual placement or unexpected content for a "liability" provision in sale terms. However, a liability exclusion clause in a delivery-goods contract is a standard, expected term, not a surprise under Section 305c I. Consequently, Clause 9 is incorporated.

V. Content control: Questionable is whether Clause 9 survives scrutiny given it excludes liability even for gross negligence. This is supported by Section 309 No. 7(b) BGB, which directly and absolutely prohibits clauses excluding liability for damage caused by gross negligence in consumer (B2C) contracts. Against this, one could argue the clause carves out intent, suggesting a good-faith attempt at balance. However, carving out only intent while still excluding gross negligence is exactly what Section 309 No. 7(b) forbids — no valuation of the circumstances is even needed given C is a consumer. Consequently, Clause 9 fails content control and is ineffective for the gross-negligence exclusion.

VI. Legal consequence: the invalid clause falls out under Section 306 I BGB; the rest of the sale contract remains valid; statutory liability rules under Sections 280 I, 241 II BGB apply in place of the failed clause.

Final answer: As a result, F cannot rely on Clause 9 to exclude liability, and C can pursue statutory damages for the gouge caused by F's grossly negligent delivery.

### Question 5 (Case)

**Facts:** Car-rental company R has a standard rental agreement used for every booking. Deep inside a section titled "Vehicle Return Procedure," between paragraphs about fuel levels and cleaning, sits one sentence: "Any late return, even by one minute, triggers a flat EUR 500 penalty regardless of cause." Customer K, renting for a weekend city trip, signs the agreement without reading past the first page and returns the car eight minutes late due to unexpected traffic.

**Question:** Can R charge K the EUR 500 penalty?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: R might have a claim against K for the EUR 500 late-return penalty under the rental agreement's clause, if that clause validly became part of the contract.

II. Requirements:
III. Existence of SBT
IV. Incorporation, including the surprising-clause filter
V. Legal consequence if not incorporated

III. Existence of SBT: the penalty clause is a pre-formulated contract term used for every rental, presented unilaterally by R — it satisfies Section 305 I BGB. No facts suggest individual negotiation.

IV. Incorporation: Questionable is whether the clause became part of the contract despite K not reading it. This is supported by the general rule that actual reading is not required, only a reasonable opportunity to take notice — K had the document available and signed it. Against this, one could argue the clause's placement defeats that opportunity: a severe flat penalty clause is buried under an unrelated heading about fuel and cleaning, not under any "Fees," "Penalties," or "Late Return Charges" heading a reasonable customer would scan for financial consequences. However, Section 305c I BGB excludes from the contract any clause so unusual in placement that the other party need not expect it there, and a EUR 500 flat penalty hidden inside routine procedural text is exactly this kind of placement shock — it is disproportionate to the surrounding content and not signaled by any heading. Consequently, the clause is a surprising clause under Section 305c I BGB.

V. Legal consequence: a surprising clause does not become part of the contract at all — it is excluded, not merely reinterpreted or reduced. The rest of the rental agreement remains valid under Section 306 I BGB, and R would need a different (unexcluded) legal basis, such as ordinary default-related damages under Sections 280 I, 286 BGB, to claim anything for the short delay — which would require proof of actual loss, not an automatic flat fee.

Final answer: As a result, R cannot charge K the EUR 500 flat penalty under the clause, because the clause was never incorporated into the contract; any claim for the eight-minute delay would need to rest on ordinary default rules and proof of actual damage.

### Question 6 (Case)

**Facts:** Manufacturer M sells industrial shelving to buyer B. M's order confirmation includes M's SBT, which state: "Title to the goods remains with M until full payment is received." B's purchase order, sent in reply, includes B's own SBT stating: "Buyer rejects any retention-of-title arrangement; title passes on delivery." Neither party objects to the other's document; M ships the shelving and B installs it in its warehouse. M later demands the shelving back after B misses a payment, relying on its retention-of-title clause.

**Question:** Can M reclaim the shelving under its retention-of-title clause?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: M might have a right to reclaim the shelving under Section 985 BGB (ownership-based recovery) if M validly retained title through its SBT clause.

II. Requirements:
III. Contract formation despite conflicting SBT
IV. Which SBT terms actually apply
V. Legal consequence for the retention-of-title dispute

III. Contract formation: Both parties acted as if a contract existed — M shipped, B accepted and installed the goods — so a contract was concluded despite the conflicting paperwork; the "battle of forms" issue concerns which terms govern, not whether a contract exists at all.

IV. Which terms apply: Questionable is whether M's retention-of-title clause prevails because M's SBT accompanied the shipment last, or whether B's rejection wins because B's purchase order was the most recent document before shipment. This is supported by the intuitive "last shot" theory, under which whichever party's terms were sent last and unopposed would apply. Against this, the statutory-provisions approach rejects the last-shot theory: it does not reflect real agreement, only who happened to communicate last, and would create an arbitrary advantage from persistence rather than consent. However, applying the statutory-provisions approach, matching terms between the two SBT sets apply, but the retention-of-title clauses directly conflict (M asserts it, B rejects it) and therefore do not agree — a conflicting clause falls out entirely, on both sides, rather than one side's version winning. Consequently, no retention-of-title term governs the contract at all.

V. Legal consequence: with the conflicting clauses cancelled out, Section 306 BGB fills the gap with statutory law. Under German sale-of-goods law, retention of title is not a statutory default — it must be affirmatively agreed under Section 449 I BGB. Since no valid agreement on retention of title exists here, ownership passed to B on delivery under the ordinary Section 929 route.

Final answer: As a result, M cannot reclaim the shelving under a retention-of-title clause, because the conflicting SBT cancel each other out on that point and no retention-of-title arrangement was validly agreed; M's remedy for the missed payment lies in ordinary payment-default claims, not in reclaiming ownership.

### Question 7 (Case)

**Facts:** Logistics company L's SBT contain a single sentence: "L's liability for any negligence, whether slight or gross, in the performance of transport services is capped at EUR 1,000 per shipment." A shipment is destroyed due to L's grossly negligent failure to secure cargo, causing EUR 40,000 in damage. L invokes the cap.

**Question:** Can the clause be partially saved to cap L's liability for ordinary (slight) negligence, even if the gross-negligence portion fails?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: L might rely on its liability-cap clause to limit damages to EUR 1,000, unless the clause fails content control and cannot be salvaged for its slight-negligence portion.

II. Requirements:
III. Content control of the cap as applied to gross negligence
IV. Whether the blue-pencil test allows partial survival

III. Content control: a clause capping liability even for gross negligence is invalid — this contradicts the basic legal principle that liability for gross fault should not be pre-excluded or drastically capped, a value reflected in Section 309 No. 7(b) BGB and, via Section 307, in B2B dealings as well. The cap clause as applied to gross negligence therefore fails content control.

IV. Blue-pencil test: Questionable is whether the slight-negligence portion of the cap can survive even though the gross-negligence portion cannot. This is supported by the general blue-pencil principle: mechanical, clean severance of an invalid part can save an independent, grammatically separable remaining part. Against this, one could argue any partial rescue effectively rewrites the clause into something fairer than what L actually wrote. However, the clause here is a single, undivided sentence applying one identical cap to "any negligence, whether slight or gross" — there is no grammatically separate sub-clause for slight negligence that could be deleted while leaving a self-standing valid remainder; splitting it would require inventing new wording, not deleting existing wording. Consequently, the blue-pencil test does not apply, and the entire clause fails.

Final answer: As a result, the clause cannot be partially saved — the whole liability cap is ineffective, and L faces unlimited statutory liability (subject to ordinary damages rules) for the EUR 40,000 loss, illustrating why liability caps should be drafted as separate sentences for gross and slight negligence rather than combined into one.

### Question 8 (Case — integrates Week 06 + Week 09)

**Facts:** Machine parts wholesaler W (a merchant) sells a batch of custom-milled components to manufacturing company P (also a merchant) for use in P's production line. W's SBT, presented and incorporated without dispute, state: "W excludes any liability for damages of any kind arising from defects in the delivered goods." The components turn out to have a material defect: they are milled to the wrong tolerance. Installing them causes a production-line jam that damages an unrelated piece of P's assembly equipment, costing EUR 15,000 to repair. P also wants the EUR 8,000 purchase price back for the defective components themselves.

**Question:** Can P recover the EUR 15,000 equipment-damage loss and the EUR 8,000 purchase price, or does W's SBT clause block these claims?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: P might have claims against W for damages under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I BGB, unless W's blanket exclusion clause validly bars them.

II. Requirements:
III. SBT content control of the exclusion clause (B2B route)
IV. Consequence for the clause
V. Damages route: in addition to vs. instead of performance (Week 09 analysis)
VI. Final allocation of both loss items

III. Content control: Questionable is whether W's blanket "any liability for damages of any kind" exclusion survives content control given both parties are merchants. This is supported by Section 310 I BGB, which blocks the direct application of Sections 309 and most of Section 308 in B2B transactions, making Section 307 the central test. Against this, one could argue B2B scrutiny is looser, so a broad exclusion might be more tolerable between merchants than against a consumer. However, Section 307 still catches clauses that unreasonably disadvantage the other party or restrict essential rights so strongly that the contract's purpose is endangered, and the values underlying Section 309 No. 7(b) (no exclusion of liability for significant breaches or, by extension, for the core defect-liability regime of a sale) inform that assessment even in B2B — a total, unqualified exclusion of all defect-related damages liability, with no carve-out even for the seller's own culpable breach, unreasonably guts the buyer's core warranty protection. Consequently, the clause fails Section 307 BGB content control.

IV. Consequence: the exclusion clause is ineffective under Section 306 I BGB; the rest of the sale contract remains valid; statutory warranty and damages rules apply as if the clause were never there.

V. Damages route (Week 09 hypothetical-performance test): for the EUR 15,000 equipment damage, ask whether this loss would still exist even if W now delivered conforming components — yes, the damage to the separate assembly equipment already occurred and correct later performance would not undo it, so this is damages in addition to performance under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I BGB, requiring only a breach of duty (the defect), causation, and responsibility (presumed against W under Section 280 I sentence 2, and nothing in the facts rebuts it) — no additional period (Nachfrist) is needed for this branch. For the EUR 8,000 purchase price, P is effectively seeking to give up the defective performance and recover what was paid, which is the profile of big damages or revocation rather than an "in addition" loss; since the facts do not show P set a Nachfrist for cure or that cure was dispensable, P would need to first pursue cure or establish a dispensing ground before claiming the price back as big damages under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB, or alternatively revoke the contract under Sections 437 No. 2, 323, 346 BGB (no fault required for revocation, but a Nachfrist is still required unless dispensable).

VI. Final answer: As a result, P can recover the EUR 15,000 equipment-damage loss immediately as damages in addition to performance, because W's exclusion clause is ineffective under Section 307 BGB and the loss survives regardless of later cure. Recovery of the EUR 8,000 purchase price is not yet available on these facts alone — P must first set a reasonable additional period for cure (or show one is dispensable) before claiming big damages or revoking the contract, since W's exclusion clause being void does not skip the ordinary procedural prerequisites of the warranty gateway.

## Additional Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 9

Explain the Section 310 I BGB switch for B2B content control and state which sections it excludes from direct application.

**Model Answer:**

1. Section 310 I BGB is the switch that limits direct application of certain consumer-protective content-control provisions when both parties act commercially.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 310 I BGB.
3. Requirements/features: (1) both contracting parties must be entrepreneurs under Section 14 BGB acting for their commercial or independent professional purpose, classified transaction-by-transaction rather than by fixed identity; (2) if so, Section 309 BGB and most of Section 308 BGB do not apply directly; (3) Section 307 BGB remains the central, applicable route in B2B; (4) the values underlying the excluded 308/309 prohibitions still inform the Section 307 reasonableness assessment indirectly — they are not simply irrelevant.
4. Example: a liability-cap clause that would automatically fail Section 309 No. 7(b) in a B2C sale must instead be tested under the more flexible Section 307 BGB reasonableness standard when both the seller and buyer are merchants.
5. Trap: B2B does not mean "no content control." Concluding that a B2B clause escapes scrutiny entirely because 309/308 don't apply directly is wrong — Section 307 is still a live, and often outcome-determinative, gate.

### Question 10

Explain the contra proferentem rule and its trigger condition, distinguishing it from ordinary content control.

**Model Answer:**

1. Contra proferentem is the interpretation rule that resolves genuinely ambiguous SBT wording against the party who drafted and presented it.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 305c II BGB.
3. Requirements/features: (1) the clause's wording must actually be unclear or open to more than one reasonable reading — clear-but-harsh wording does not qualify; (2) where multiple readings genuinely exist, the reading most unfavorable to the SBT user is applied; (3) this operates at the interpretation stage, logically before content control is even reached, because content control needs a settled meaning to test against Sections 307-309.
4. Example: a freight-insurance clause promising "appropriate compensation" without defining a method or reference value is ambiguous; a court applying contra proferentem adopts the reading most favorable to the policyholder and least favorable to the insurer who drafted it.
5. Trap: do not reach for contra proferentem when a clause is perfectly clear but simply harsh — that is a content-control problem under Sections 307-309, not an ambiguity problem, and the two produce different legal-consequence language (interpretation adjustment vs. clause ineffectiveness).

### Question 11

Compare Section 309 BGB and Section 308 BGB as stages of content control.

**Model Answer (Compare Schema):**

Section 309 BGB lists clauses that are prohibited outright with no possibility of case-by-case valuation — anchored directly in Section 309 BGB — so a clause matching one of its enumerated categories (e.g., excluding liability for gross negligence) fails automatically once the facts match, regardless of surrounding circumstances. Section 308 BGB lists clauses that are typically unfair but leave room for a valuation of the individual circumstances before they are struck down — anchored in Section 308 BGB — so a clause matching one of its categories (e.g., an unreasonably vague additional-performance period reserved for the user) can still be saved if the specific facts show it was not actually unreasonable. Contrast example: a blanket "no liability for gross negligence, ever" clause is caught by Section 309 No. 7(b) with no room for debate, while a "the seller may take a reasonable additional period to remedy defects" clause with a vaguely long window falls under Section 308 and requires an examination of what "reasonable" actually meant on the facts before condemning it. Exam trap: do not skip straight to Section 307 for a clause that squarely matches a Section 309 category — the correct scrutiny order is 309 first (no valuation), then 308 (valuation possible), then 307 (general catch-all); and 309/308 do not apply directly in B2B under Section 310 I, where 307 becomes the frontline test instead.

### Question 12

List the requirements for a clause to count as "individually negotiated" under Section 305 I sentence 3 BGB and thereby escape SBT control entirely.

**Model Answer (List-Requirements Schema):**

The requirements are:
1. The user must seriously put the specific clause up for discussion, not merely present it as a take-it-or-leave-it package.
2. The other party must have a real, genuine opportunity to influence the substantive content of the clause, not just discuss it.
3. The final wording must reflect more than a cosmetic acceptance of the user's original template — it must show an actual change resulting from that influence, or at minimum a demonstrated genuine chance to change it.

If any one of these three is missing, the clause remains subject to the full SBT regime (existence, incorporation, content control) rather than escaping to ordinary private-autonomy freedom of contract. The reason this rule is drawn so narrowly is that a party who merely clicked "accept," discussed the clause without any real power to change it, or negotiated some other term while leaving this one untouched still faces exactly the bargaining-power imbalance that SBT law is designed to correct — an easy "we talked about it" escape hatch would gut the entire protective regime.

### Question 13

Explain why content control does not ordinarily review a contract's main price or main performance, and where the boundary of that immunity lies.

**Model Answer:**

1. Content control under Sections 307-309 BGB is designed to police departures from statutory default rules in ancillary clauses, not to let courts second-guess the core bargain the parties struck.
2. Statutory anchor: Sections 307-309 BGB (content control), Sections 134, 138 BGB (outer boundary for extreme cases).
3. Requirements/features: (1) the immunity covers only the main price and the main performance obligation, i.e. the core price-performance relationship freely set by private autonomy; (2) it does not cover ancillary clauses that happen to have a financial effect (fees, penalties, caps) — those remain fully subject to 307-309; (3) an extreme main-price term can still fail outside content control altogether, through Section 138 BGB (usury: a strong imbalance plus exploitation of a weaker party) or Section 134 BGB (violation of a separate statutory prohibition), rather than through 307-309.
4. Example: a car dealer's SBT stating a sale price of EUR 25,000 cannot be attacked as "unfair" under Section 307; but the same EUR 25,000 price for a car objectively worth EUR 5,000, extracted from a buyer in a position of clear weakness or distress, could instead be attacked as usurious under Section 138 II BGB.
5. Trap: do not run a Section 307-309 content-control analysis on the headline price itself — if a price genuinely looks exploitative, reroute the analysis to Section 138 (or 134), because 307-309 structurally does not reach the main price/performance term.

## Additional Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 14 (Case)

**Facts:** Platform provider Q offers project-management software to business customers under a subscription contract. Q's SBT, properly incorporated, include: "Q may unilaterally modify the scope of core platform features at any time, at its sole discretion, without prior notice." Business customer Z relies heavily on a specific reporting feature central to its workflow. Q removes that feature overnight without warning, disrupting Z's operations.

**Question:** Is the unilateral-change clause valid, and can Q rely on it to justify removing the core feature?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: Q might be entitled to unilaterally remove the reporting feature under its SBT clause, unless the clause fails content control despite the more permissive B2B setting.

II. Requirements:
III. Content control of the unilateral-change clause under Section 307 BGB (B2B route)
IV. Legal consequence

III. Content control: Questionable is whether an unlimited, sole-discretion, no-notice right to alter core features unreasonably disadvantages Z under Section 307 I, II BGB, notwithstanding Section 310 I channeling B2B scrutiny through Section 307. This is supported by Section 307 II No. 1 BGB's value that a clause deviating from the essential basic idea of the statutory provision it modifies is presumptively unreasonable, together with the deck's principle that clauses restricting essential rights so strongly that the contract's purpose is endangered fail content control — removing a feature the customer specifically relies on, without notice, undermines the very performance Z is paying for. Against this, one could argue platforms need flexibility to evolve software, and B2B counterparties are expected to tolerate more risk-shifting than consumers under the relaxed B2B route. However, "sole discretion" combined with "no prior notice" and no carve-out for core/essential features goes beyond reasonable flexibility — it is unlimited and eliminates any predictability of the bargained performance, echoing the value behind Section 308 No. 4 BGB (a reserved right to change performance is only tolerable if reasonable, considering the other party's interests), which informs Section 307 even though Section 308 itself does not apply directly in B2B. Consequently, the unrestricted, no-notice, core-feature-inclusive change clause fails Section 307 BGB content control even under the more permissive B2B standard.

IV. Legal consequence: the clause is ineffective; the rest of the subscription contract survives under Section 306 I BGB; Q remains bound to perform the originally agreed feature scope unless it separately obtains Z's actual consent to a change or the clause had been properly limited (advance notice, reasonable regard for the customer's interest, exclusion of core features).

Final answer: As a result, Q cannot rely on the clause to justify removing the reporting feature; Q's overnight removal breaches its contractual duty, and Z can pursue remedies for the resulting non-performance.

### Question 15 (Case)

**Facts:** Freight insurer N's SBT state: "In case of cargo damage, the policyholder receives appropriate compensation." Policyholder P's cargo, worth EUR 20,000, is damaged in transit. N offers EUR 4,000, arguing "appropriate" leaves N broad discretion tied to its internal risk assessment. P disputes this and demands compensation closer to the actual loss.

**Question:** How should the ambiguous term "appropriate compensation" be resolved, and can N limit payment to EUR 4,000?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: P might be entitled to compensation beyond N's offered EUR 4,000, depending on how the ambiguous "appropriate compensation" clause in N's SBT is interpreted.

II. Requirements:
III. Whether the clause is genuinely ambiguous under Section 305c II BGB
IV. Which interpretation governs
V. Consequence for the amount owed

III. Ambiguity: Questionable is whether "appropriate compensation" is genuinely unclear. This is supported by the absence of any defined method, percentage, or cap in the wording, leaving at least two reasonable readings open — full market-value indemnification versus an insurer-discretion-limited amount. Against this, N argues "appropriate" is an ordinary word carrying an implicit "reasonable under the circumstances" meaning tied to actuarial practice. However, the clause supplies no objective anchor (no reference to market value, replacement cost, or a defined formula) by which "appropriate" could be pinned down, and multiple genuinely reasonable readings remain open on the wording alone. Consequently, the clause is ambiguous within Section 305c II BGB.

IV. Which interpretation governs: contra proferentem directs that ambiguity in SBT wording is interpreted against the user who drafted and presented it — here, N. This is supported by the policy rationale that N, as drafter, could have specified a method and bears the risk of failing to do so. Against this, N might argue its restrictive reading is also one of the reasonable candidates and should not automatically lose. However, contra proferentem specifically selects the reading least favorable to the drafter among the reasonable candidates, so the policyholder-favorable reading — compensation reflecting the full extent of the loss, absent any drafted limitation — prevails. Consequently, the clause must be read against N's narrow interpretation.

V. Consequence: N cannot rely on its restrictive EUR 4,000 reading; N owes compensation assessed on the policyholder-favorable interpretation, subject to ordinary proof-of-loss rules for the EUR 20,000 claimed.

Final answer: As a result, the ambiguous "appropriate compensation" clause is interpreted against N under Section 305c II BGB, and N cannot limit payment to its self-serving EUR 4,000 figure.

### Question 16 (Case — integrates Week 06 + Week 03)

**Facts:** Wholesaler G's website catalog lists industrial pumps with prices, without mentioning any SBT. Buyer H emails G: "I order Pump Model X at the listed price of EUR 3,000, per your catalog." G replies by email, "Order confirmed, thank you," matching every term of H's email exactly — item, quantity, and price unchanged. Attached to that same confirmation email, for the very first time, is a PDF titled "General Terms" never previously mentioned in the exchange, containing the clause: "Buyer bears all transport risk from dispatch." H does not open the PDF and sends no further reply. G later ships the pump, which is damaged in transit through no fault of either party. G invokes the transport-risk clause to deny any responsibility for the damage.

**Question:** Is a contract concluded, and did the transport-risk clause in G's General Terms become part of it?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: A contract for the pump may have been concluded on H's own terms, but G's General Terms, including the transport-risk clause, may never have become part of that contract.

II. Requirements:
III. Contract formation under Sections 145, 147, 150 II BGB
IV. Incorporation of the General Terms under Section 305 II BGB

III. Contract formation: G's catalog listing is an invitatio ad offerendum, not a binding offer, since G cannot reasonably intend to be bound toward every possible buyer for limited stock. H's email, stating the specific pump, quantity, and the exact listed price, is the offer under Section 145 BGB. Questionable is whether G's reply is a straight acceptance or, per Section 150 II BGB, a modified acceptance that would instead count as a rejection plus new offer. This is supported by the fact that a reply attaching new material terms is normally treated as modification. Against this, the substantive content of G's reply — item, quantity, price — is identical to H's offer in every respect; only an attachment, not a change to the offer's own terms, was added. However, Section 150 II targets changes to the terms of the offer itself, and the core essentialia negotii here are untouched, so G's reply is a plain, unmodified acceptance. Consequently, a contract for the pump at EUR 3,000 was concluded on H's own terms the moment G's acceptance was received under Section 130 BGB.

IV. Incorporation of the General Terms: Questionable is whether the transport-risk clause, bundled into the acceptance email but never mentioned in the prior exchange, became part of this already-concluding contract under Section 305 II BGB, which requires the user (G) to point to the SBT, give a reasonable opportunity to review it, and obtain the other party's agreement. This is supported by G attaching the document and nominally giving H the opportunity to open it. Against this, one could argue that H's silence after receiving the attachment, combined with G's own confirmation already matching and closing H's offer, means no separate, additional act of agreement to the newly bundled document exists on these facts. However, silence is not acceptance under ordinary contract-formation doctrine (Section 145 et seq. BGB reasoning; no facts trigger the narrow Section 362 HGB commercial-confirmation exception, which in any event would run in the opposite direction here since it is G, the sender, not H, who would need to object to a letter it received), and Section 305 II's "agreement" element demands more than passive non-objection to an unsolicited attachment appearing for the first time at the confirmation stage. Consequently, the General Terms, including the transport-risk clause, were never incorporated.

Final answer: As a result, a contract for the pump at EUR 3,000 was validly concluded through H's offer and G's matching acceptance, but the transport-risk clause never became part of that contract; G cannot invoke it, and responsibility for the transit damage must instead be worked out under the statutory default rules that apply absent any effective contractual risk-allocation clause.

### Question 17 (Case)

**Facts:** Wholesaler R sells a packaging machine to buyer S; both are merchants. R's SBT state: "Delivery within 4-6 weeks; payment due 30 days after invoice." S's own SBT, attached to its purchase order, state: "Delivery within 4-6 weeks; payment due 14 days after invoice." Neither side objects to the other's document. R manufactures and ships within 5 weeks; S accepts and installs the machine, then pays 25 days after invoice. R demands default interest, arguing S breached the 14-day term found in S's own SBT, which R claims should prevail as the more specific document.

**Question:** Which payment-term clause, if any, governs, and did S breach it?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: R might be entitled to default interest if S breached an applicable 30-day or 14-day payment term, but this depends on which (if either) party's conflicting SBT governs payment timing.

II. Requirements:
III. Contract formation despite conflicting SBT
IV. Which SBT terms actually apply — matching vs. conflicting clauses
V. Consequence for the payment-timing dispute

III. Contract formation: both parties acted as if a contract existed — R manufactured and shipped, S accepted and installed the machine — so a contract was concluded despite the conflicting paperwork, consistent with the "battle of forms" doctrine that formation and content-of-terms are separate questions.

IV. Which terms apply: Questionable is whether R's 30-day term or S's 14-day term governs, given the two SBT sets conflict on payment timing while matching exactly on delivery timing (both state 4-6 weeks). This is supported by the statutory-provisions approach: matching terms between the two SBT sets apply because both parties actually agree on them, so the 4-6 week delivery term is incorporated without dispute. Against this, R argues a "last shot" logic — that whichever party's document was sent or received last should control the conflicting payment term. However, the statutory-provisions approach expressly rejects last-shot reasoning as an arbitrary reward for persistence rather than genuine agreement; conflicting terms fall out entirely on both sides rather than one side's version winning by timing. Consequently, neither the 30-day nor the 14-day clause governs payment timing at all.

V. Consequence: with the conflicting payment clauses cancelled out, Section 306 II BGB fills the gap with statutory law rather than either party's drafted figure — the applicable default is Section 271 I BGB, under which payment falls due promptly absent an agreed date, not on any 30-day or 14-day schedule invented by either SBT set. Any interest claim R raises must therefore rest on that statutory due date and the ordinary default-of-payment requirements under Section 286 BGB, not on breach of "the 14-day clause," which never bound S in the first place.

Final answer: As a result, neither R's nor S's payment-timing clause governs; the conflicting clauses cancel out under the statutory-provisions approach while the matching delivery term stands, and any interest claim must be built on the statutory due-date default and ordinary default rules rather than on S's rejected 14-day figure.

### Question 18 (Case — integrates Week 06 + Week 10)

**Facts:** Steel supplier S sells and delivers a batch of steel beams to construction firm B. S's SBT, properly incorporated and undisputed, state: "Ownership of the delivered goods remains with S until B has paid the full purchase price." B has not yet paid. Before paying, B resells and hands over some of the beams to subcontractor T, who has no knowledge of the retention-of-title arrangement and pays B in full, reasonably believing B to be the outright owner.

**Question:** Does T acquire ownership of the beams from B?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: T might have acquired ownership of the beams from B under Section 929 sentence 1 BGB, unless B's own lack of ownership — caused by S's still-pending retention of title — blocks a straightforward transfer and instead requires good-faith acquisition.

II. Requirements:
III. Effect of S's retention-of-title clause on ownership as between S and B
IV. B-to-T transfer under Section 929 sentence 1 BGB (authorization)
V. Good-faith acquisition under Sections 929 sentence 1, 932 BGB if authorization fails
VI. Section 935 BGB lost/stolen block

III. Retention of title (S-B layer): the clause is validly incorporated per the facts and functions as a suspensive-condition disposition agreement under Section 158 I BGB, read together with Section 449 I BGB — S and B agreed to transfer ownership only on condition of full payment. Since B has not yet paid, the condition has not occurred, so ownership never passed from S to B; S remains owner and B holds only possession plus a conditional expectancy.

IV. B-to-T transfer: T's acquisition would ordinarily require agreement, delivery, agreement still existing at delivery, and authorization to dispose under Section 929 sentence 1 BGB. Questionable is whether B, as a non-owner, had authorization to dispose of the beams. This is supported by nothing in the facts — B never obtained S's consent to resell before paying in full, and no facts show S authorized onward resale (e.g., no extended-retention-of-title processing clause is mentioned). Against this, one could argue construction-supply trade custom might imply a right to resell. However, absent any such authorization wording in S's SBT, B lacked the power to dispose of goods it did not yet own. Consequently, authorization is missing, and a straightforward Section 929 sentence 1 transfer fails.

V. Good-faith acquisition: Questionable is whether T can nonetheless acquire ownership under Sections 929 sentence 1, 932 BGB despite B's missing authorization. This is supported by T's good faith — T had no knowledge of the retention-of-title arrangement and reasonably relied on B's possession as an indicator of ownership under the Section 1006 BGB presumption. Against this, one could argue B was never genuinely in a position to sell since S's SBT clearly reserved title from the outset. However, good-faith acquisition exists precisely to override a missing-authorization gap where the true owner voluntarily entrusted possession to the non-owner, and S itself voluntarily delivered the beams to B as part of the sale — the legal-appearance risk S created by handing over possession is exactly what good-faith acquisition places on S rather than on the innocent third party T. Consequently, the requirements of good-faith acquisition are met.

VI. Section 935 check: the lost/stolen block only applies where the true owner lost possession involuntarily. Here S voluntarily delivered the beams to B as part of the sale transaction, so Section 935 BGB does not apply and does not bar T's good-faith acquisition.

Final answer: As a result, T acquires ownership of the beams from B via good-faith acquisition under Sections 929 sentence 1, 932 BGB, even though B itself never became owner because S's validly incorporated retention-of-title clause suspended the ownership transfer until full payment. S loses ownership to T and is left only with claims against B for breach of the retention-of-title arrangement, not recovery of the beams themselves — illustrating how an SBT clause valid at the incorporation and content-control level can still fail to protect the drafting party once property-law good-faith acquisition rules take over downstream.

### Question 19 (Case)

**Facts:** Furniture retailer F's SBT, properly incorporated into its consumer sales, state: "If a delivered item is defective, F reserves a reasonable additional period to remedy the defect before the customer may pursue further remedies." Consumer C buys a dining chair with a wobbly leg — a minor, easily-fixed cosmetic-mechanical defect. C notifies F. F takes three months to send a repair technician, offering no interim explanation despite several follow-up emails from C. F argues the clause entitles it to however long it judges "reasonable."

**Question:** Is F's additional-performance-period clause valid, and can F rely on it to justify the three-month delay?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: F might be entitled to rely on its additional-performance-period clause to justify the delay, unless the clause fails content control under Section 308 BGB as applied to these facts.

II. Requirements:
III. Classification of the clause under Section 308 BGB (valuation-possible prohibition), not Section 309 BGB
IV. Valuation of the circumstances: was three months reasonable for this defect
V. Legal consequence

III. Classification: Questionable is whether this clause is caught by Section 309 BGB's automatic, no-valuation prohibitions or by Section 308 BGB's valuation-possible category. This is supported by the clause's own wording, which reserves an "additional reasonable period" without a fixed number of days or months — it does not flatly deny the customer any remedy timeline the way a Section 309 hard-prohibition clause would, but instead leaves the actual length open to later assessment. Against this, one could argue any vague, seller-favorable timing clause should be struck automatically. However, Section 308 BGB is specifically built for insufficiently-specific additional-performance-period clauses reserved for the user, which is exactly this clause's structure — it is not on the Section 309 list. Consequently, the clause must be tested under Section 308 BGB with a valuation of the individual circumstances, not dismissed automatically under Section 309.

IV. Valuation: Questionable is whether three months was a reasonable additional period on these specific facts. This is supported by the nature of the defect — a wobbly chair leg is a minor, easily-diagnosed mechanical fix that does not require rare parts, specialized fabrication, or complex logistics. Against this, F might argue general scheduling backlogs or technician availability justify a longer window. However, F offered no interim explanation despite repeated follow-up from C, and nothing in the facts points to any genuine complexity justifying three months for a simple chair repair — the valuation of these circumstances shows the period actually taken was unreasonably long relative to the defect's nature. Consequently, the clause, though not automatically invalid in the abstract, is invalid as applied to this three-month delay under Section 308 No. 2 BGB's evaluation standard.

V. Legal consequence: the clause is ineffective for this delay under Section 306 I BGB; the rest of the sale contract remains valid; C is not bound to wait indefinitely and may proceed to statutory follow-on remedies (further cure demand with a shorter, C-set reasonable deadline, or move toward reduction/revocation/damages under Sections 437 ff. BGB if that deadline also lapses).

Final answer: As a result, F's clause is not automatically void, but on these facts a three-month delay for a minor defect fails the Section 308 BGB valuation of the individual circumstances and cannot justify the delay; C may treat the reasonable-period requirement as already exceeded and escalate toward the standard warranty remedy ladder.

### Question 20 (Case)

**Facts:** Fitness studio G's membership contract is a properly pre-formulated, incorporated SBT document, given to every new member with a clear opportunity to read it before signing. Under the heading "Membership Duration And Renewal" — the correct, expected heading for that kind of provision — sits this sentence, phrased in ordinary contract language and not hidden or in fine print: "By signing this agreement, the member irrevocably consents to G using their photograph and name in G's national television advertising campaigns without further compensation." New member M signs without reading closely and later discovers their photo in a nationwide TV ad.

**Question:** Did the image-rights clause become part of M's membership contract?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: G's image-rights clause may have become part of M's membership contract under Section 305 II BGB, unless it is excluded as a surprising clause under Section 305c I BGB despite its unhidden placement and ordinary print.

II. Requirements:
III. Ordinary incorporation elements (reference, opportunity, agreement)
IV. Surprise by content rather than by placement

III. Ordinary incorporation: G pointed M to the document, gave M a genuine opportunity to review it before signing, and M signed, satisfying Section 305 II BGB's reference, opportunity, and agreement elements on their face.

IV. Content-based surprise: Questionable is whether the clause is nonetheless excluded under Section 305c I BGB, even though it sits under the correct heading, in ordinary print, not buried or disguised. This is supported by the principle that a clause can be surprising through its content alone, not only through unusual placement — a term so unrelated to what a reasonable party would expect from that type of contract that its mere presence, regardless of where it sits, defeats reasonable expectation. Against this, one could argue that because the clause is clearly labeled and readable, any unfairness must be a content-control problem under Sections 307-309, not a surprise problem under 305c I. However, the two tests ask different questions: content control asks whether a properly-expected-and-incorporated clause is too harsh, while the surprise test asks whether the clause's very subject matter is something a member joining a gym could reasonably anticipate finding in a "Membership Duration And Renewal" section at all — an irrevocable, uncompensated grant of national advertising image rights bears no substantive connection to duration or renewal terms and is the kind of clause a reasonable member would expect to see (if anywhere) under a separate, clearly-flagged "Image Rights" or "Marketing Consent" heading, not folded into duration language. Consequently, the clause is a surprising clause by content, irrespective of its unhidden placement and clear print.

Final answer: As a result, the image-rights clause did not become part of M's membership contract under Section 305c I BGB; the rest of the membership agreement remains valid under Section 306 I BGB, and G has no contractual basis to use M's image in its advertising.

### Question 21

Explain the consequence ladder under Section 306 BGB when an SBT clause is not incorporated or fails content control, including the rare exception where the whole contract fails.

**Model Answer (List-Requirements Schema):**

The consequence ladder is:
1. Default rule: the offending clause (not incorporated under 305c I, or ineffective under 307-309) simply falls out; it does not become part of, or does not remain part of, the contract (Section 306 I BGB).
2. Gap-fill: the rest of the contract remains valid, and statutory default provisions replace the missing or invalid term (Section 306 II BGB); if no statutory provision directly fits, supplementary interpretation asks what reasonable parties would have agreed.
3. Exception (rare): the whole contract is void, not just the clause, if adherence to the remaining contract would represent an unreasonable hardship for one party (Section 306 III BGB) — this is the narrow escape hatch, not the default outcome.

If step 3's hardship threshold is not met, the analysis must stop at steps 1-2 and the contract survives with the statutory gap-fill; jumping straight to "the whole contract is void" without showing genuine unreasonable hardship for a specific party is a structural error.

Trap: exam answers overwhelmingly default to "clause falls out, contract survives, statutory law fills the gap" (steps 1-2) — do not reach for the Section 306 III total-failure exception unless the facts affirmatively show that continuing the stripped-down contract would be an unreasonable hardship for one side; merely losing a favorable clause is not hardship in this sense.

### Question 22 (Case)

**Facts:** Self-service parking garage P displays a large, clearly worded sign at the entrance barrier, visible to every driver before entry: "P is not liable for damage to vehicles caused by the driver's own improper parking or maneuvering. P remains liable for damage caused by P's own equipment or staff." Driver D drives in, taking no special notice of the sign, and parks. While D is parked, the garage's automated barrier arm malfunctions due to P's own poorly maintained mechanism and swings down onto D's car, denting the roof. P points to the sign and denies liability for "any damage in the garage."

**Question:** Did the exclusion clause become part of the contract, and does it cover the barrier-arm damage?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: D might have a damages claim against P under Sections 280 I, 241 II BGB for the barrier-arm damage, unless P's sign-based exclusion clause validly excludes it.

II. Requirements:
III. Incorporation of the sign as SBT despite no explicit individual reference
IV. Scope of the clause as actually worded

III. Incorporation: Questionable is whether a posted sign, rather than a signed document or explicit oral reference, can incorporate SBT into a self-service parking contract. This is supported by Section 305 II No. 1 Alternative 2 BGB, which permits a clearly visible notice at the place of contract conclusion to substitute for explicit reference precisely where explicit reference is impractical — a self-service garage with no attendant to hand over paperwork is exactly this situation. Against this, one could argue D took no notice of the sign and never read it. However, actual reading is not required for incorporation, only a reasonable opportunity to take notice, and a large, clearly worded sign positioned at the entry barrier gives every driver that opportunity; D's agreement is implied by driving in and parking under the posted terms. Consequently, the sign's terms, including the liability clause, are incorporated into the parking contract.

IV. Scope of the clause: Questionable is whether the incorporated clause, even though validly part of the contract, actually covers the barrier-arm malfunction. This is supported by the clause's own two-part wording, which excludes liability only for damage caused by the driver's own improper parking or maneuvering, while expressly preserving P's liability for damage caused by P's own equipment or staff. Against this, P argues the sign generally disclaims liability for "any damage in the garage." However, that broader gloss is not what the clause actually says — an incorporated and content-control-passing clause only excludes what its own wording covers, and a malfunctioning barrier arm caused by P's poor maintenance is expressly carved back into P's liability by the clause's own second sentence, not excluded by its first. Consequently, the clause does not apply to this damage at all.

Final answer: As a result, the exclusion clause is validly incorporated via the entrance sign, but by its own terms it does not cover damage caused by P's malfunctioning equipment; D can pursue the barrier-arm damage claim under Sections 280 I, 241 II BGB, and P cannot stretch a validly incorporated clause beyond what it actually says.

### Question 23 (Case — integrates Week 06 + Week 07)

**Facts:** Company P employs A as its general purchasing representative, with A openly introduced to suppliers as having full authority to negotiate and conclude supply contracts on P's behalf — nothing in A's external dealings signals any limit. Internally, unknown to any supplier, P's policy caps A's authority at EUR 10,000 per deal without additional sign-off from P's board. A concludes a EUR 50,000 supply contract with seller S in P's name, and as part of that deal, S's SBT — including a broad liability-limitation clause — are properly presented, and A agrees to their incorporation on P's behalf. P later argues neither the contract nor S's SBT bind P, because A exceeded A's internal EUR 10,000 authority.

**Question:** Is the EUR 50,000 contract binding on P, and are S's SBT validly incorporated against P?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: S might have a valid EUR 50,000 contract with P, including validly incorporated SBT, if A's exceeding of an undisclosed internal authority limit does not defeat either A's external power of representation or the SBT's incorporation.

II. Requirements:
III. Whether P is bound to the underlying contract at all (Section 164 I BGB, can-do vs. may-do)
IV. Whether S's SBT are validly incorporated on P's behalf despite A's internal breach (Section 305 II BGB)

III. Contract binding: Questionable is whether P is bound by the EUR 50,000 contract given A's internal EUR 10,000 cap. This is supported by the agency can-do/may-do distinction: A's external power of representation ("can do") was granted without any visible limit communicated to suppliers, while the EUR 10,000 cap is purely an internal restriction ("may do") on how A was permitted to use that external power. Against this, P argues that exceeding an internal policy should mean the deal was never properly authorized at all. However, internal restrictions generally do not bind or put on notice a third party who has no way of knowing about them, and nothing in the facts suggests S knew or should have known of P's internal cap — A's external authority to conclude supply contracts remained fully intact toward S. Consequently, P is bound to the EUR 50,000 contract; A's breach of the internal cap creates, at most, an internal recourse issue between P and A, not a defect in P's binding toward S.

IV. SBT incorporation: Questionable is whether S's SBT, including the liability-limitation clause, were validly incorporated against P given that A — not P personally — was the one who received the reference, had the opportunity to review, and agreed to their application under Section 305 II BGB. This is supported by the same can-do logic carried one layer further: because A had full external power of representation to conclude the underlying contract in P's name, A's declarations in doing so — including agreeing to incorporate the counterparty's SBT as part of striking that deal — are P's own declarations under Section 164 I BGB, exactly as if P had personally received the reference, reviewed the terms, and agreed. Against this, P might argue that incorporation is a separate, additional act requiring P's own direct assent beyond the main contract. However, SBT incorporation is not a freestanding transaction outside the contract-conclusion process — it is part of concluding the very contract A had full power to conclude, so the same authority that lets A bind P to the EUR 50,000 deal also lets A bind P to the SBT presented as part of striking it. Consequently, S's SBT, including the liability-limitation clause, are validly incorporated against P.

Final answer: As a result, both the EUR 50,000 supply contract and S's incorporated SBT bind P; A's violation of the undisclosed internal EUR 10,000 cap is a "may do" breach that exposes A to P's internal consequences but does not unwind P's external "can do" liability toward S on either the contract or its incorporated terms.
