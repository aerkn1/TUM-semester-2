## Clarification Explanation Saved

Standard Business Terms are pre-written contract rules. They are controlled because one party usually writes them in advance and the other party simply accepts. SBT law allows efficient mass contracts, but blocks hidden, unclear, or unfair pre-written clauses.

Friendly analogy: SBT are like house rules. Normal house rules are allowed, but a hidden or extreme rule should not bind the other person simply because it was buried in the paperwork.

Full route:

```text
0. Contract conclusion.
1. Is the clause SBT?
2. Was it incorporated?
3. Is it surprising or ambiguous?
4. Is the content valid?
5. What happens if it fails?
```

Plain section map:

| Section | Basic interpretation |
|---|---|
| Sections 145 et seq. BGB | Contract first: offer and acceptance. |
| Section 150 II BGB | Changed acceptance is a rejection plus new offer. |
| Section 305 I BGB | Ready-made rule provided by one party for repeated use. |
| Section 305 I sentence 3 BGB | Truly negotiated clauses are not SBT. |
| Section 305 II BGB | The other party must be told and given a chance to review. |
| Section 305c I BGB | Hidden or unexpected important rules are surprising and do not enter. |
| Section 305c II BGB | Unclear wording is interpreted against the SBT user. |
| Sections 307-309 BGB | Clear but too harsh rules are tested for content validity. |
| Section 306 BGB | Bad clause out; contract usually stays; statutory law fills the gap. |

## Prompts, User Answers, And Feedback

### Prompt 1

> A webshop sells a laptop. At checkout, its terms say: "For defective products, customers only receive a store voucher, never a refund." The buyer clicks accept. Why is this likely an SBT issue, and what is the first thing we must check before attacking the clause?

User answer:

> 1-yes, unfair obligation due to the seller defect
> 2-is it provided and the other party is told?

Feedback:

- Correct instinct: the voucher-only clause feels legally problematic.
- Correction: it is an SBT issue because it is a pre-written checkout clause used by the seller, not mainly because the product is defective.
- First route should be contract first, then SBT classification, then incorporation, then content control.

Refined model:

```text
Defect facts explain why the clause matters.
Pre-written repeated-use wording explains why it is SBT.
```

### Prompt 2

> If the buyer clicked "I accept terms" but the voucher clause was hidden under "technical information," what SBT problem does that sound like: incorporation, surprise, ambiguity, or content control?

User answer:

> surprsie?

Feedback:

- Correct: surprise.
- Exam-safe language: the clause may be surprising under Section 305c I BGB if a major limitation of defect remedies is hidden in a place where the buyer would not reasonably expect it.

Refined model:

```text
Hidden important rule = surprise.
```

### Prompt 3

> If the same voucher-only clause is clearly shown under "Warranty and Refunds," so it is not hidden, what would we check next: surprise, ambiguity, or content control?

User answer:

> ambiguşty

Feedback:

- Correction: ambiguity only applies if the wording is unclear.
- If the wording clearly says "voucher only, no refund," the main issue is content control.

Refined model:

```text
Hidden = surprise.
Unclear = ambiguity.
Clear but too harsh = content control.
```

### Prompt 4

> A seller's SBT says: "The seller is never liable for anything, under any circumstances." Which category is the main issue: surprise, ambiguity, or content control?

User answer:

> content control, too harsh

Feedback:

- Correct.
- Exam-safe language: this is content control because the clause is not mainly hidden or unclear; it is substantively too broad and one-sided.

### Prompt 5

> If the liability clause fails, what happens to the contract in normal SBT logic?

User answer:

> that clause is excluded from contract

Feedback:

- Correct core result.
- Precision: if the clause is surprising, it was never incorporated; if it is too harsh, it was incorporated but becomes ineffective. In both ordinary cases, the bad clause does not work.
- Use Section 306 BGB: the invalid clause drops out, the rest of the contract usually remains valid, and statutory default rules apply instead.

## Weak Spots

| Weak spot | Quality | Correction rule |
|---|---|---|
| SBT trigger versus unfairness trigger | yellow | SBT classification comes from pre-written repeated-use wording, not from the defect itself. |
| Incorporation versus SBT classification | yellow | First ask whether it is SBT under Section 305 I; then ask whether it entered under Section 305 II. |
| Ambiguity versus content control | yellow | Ambiguity needs unclear wording; clear harsh wording goes to Sections 307-309 BGB. |
| Consequence language | green/yellow | "Bad clause out" is right; add Section 306: contract usually stays, statutory law fills the gap. |
