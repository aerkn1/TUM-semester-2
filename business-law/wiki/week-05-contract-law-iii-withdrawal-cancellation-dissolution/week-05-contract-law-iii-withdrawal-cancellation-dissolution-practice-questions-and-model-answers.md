## Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 1

Define the consumer right of withdrawal and state the policy problem it solves.

**Model Answer:**
1. Withdrawal (Widerruf) is a statutory right allowing a consumer to exit certain contracts with a trader within a set period, without needing to show a defect or breach — a pure cooling-off right.
2. Statutory anchor: Sections 355, 312b, 312c BGB (355 for the declaration/effects mechanics; 312b for off-premises contracts; 312c for distance contracts).
3. Requirements/features: (1) personal scope — consumer (Section 13 BGB) contracting with a trader (Section 14 BGB); (2) material scope — a statutory withdrawal situation applies (most commonly distance or off-premises) with no exclusion under Section 312g II; (3) a clear declaration within the applicable time limit.
4. Example: a consumer buys a laptop from an online shop, receives it, and decides a few days later it does not meet their needs.
5. Consequence/trap: do not require any defect or wrongdoing by the trader — withdrawal exists precisely because the consumer faced structural disadvantage, information deficit, or surprise, not because anything went wrong with the goods.

### Question 2

Compare withdrawal and revocation.

**Model Answer:**
Withdrawal triggers when a consumer wants to exit a distance or off-premises contract with a trader, without any performance problem, anchored in Sections 355, 312b, 312c BGB, and leads to a consumer-protection restitution regime (Section 357) that is less strict than ordinary revocation restitution. Revocation triggers when a valid reciprocal contract exists but the debtor fails to perform, or performs badly, anchored in Sections 323, 324, 326 V BGB, and leads to full mutual restitution under Sections 346-349 BGB. Contrast example: a consumer who simply changes their mind about an online purchase needs withdrawal; a buyer who never receives the ordered goods at all after a Nachfrist expired needs revocation. Exam warning: do not reach for revocation just because a purchase was made online — check first whether anything actually went wrong with performance, or whether the consumer is merely exercising a cooling-off right.

### Question 3

State the four-step structure for extraordinary cancellation of a continuing obligation under Section 314 BGB.

**Model Answer:**
The requirements are:
1. A compelling reason — weighing both parties' interests and all circumstances, continuation until the agreed end or notice period cannot reasonably be expected of the cancelling party.
2. Expiry of a period for relief or a warning notice without result, unless dispensable (e.g. for gravity or special circumstances comparable to Section 323 II).
3. A declaration of cancellation within a reasonable period after gaining knowledge of the reason.
4. Effect: the continuing obligation ends immediately, ex nunc, without a notice period.

If these are met, the consequence is that the relationship ends for the future only — past performances are not undone, and damages can be combined under Section 314 IV BGB. The rule exists because continuing obligations (rent, employment, ongoing services) cannot usually be unwound retrospectively the way a one-time purchase can, so the law gives an immediate forward-looking exit instead of forcing retroactive restitution.

### Question 4

Compare cancellation and dissolution.

**Model Answer:**
Cancellation triggers when one party unilaterally ends a continuing obligation, either for a compelling reason (Section 314 BGB or a special provision) or, for indefinite-period relationships, by ordinary notice without needing a reason; it leads to termination ex nunc, with no restitution of past performances. Dissolution triggers when both parties agree to end a contract, resting on private autonomy (freedom to conclude contracts implies freedom to dissolve them by agreement); it leads to termination on whatever terms the parties negotiated (e.g. severance pay, paid leave, non-competition clauses). Contrast example: an employer who cannot easily cancel an employment relationship because of strong statutory protection may instead negotiate a dissolution agreement with the employee. Exam trap: dissolution is never unilateral — a single party's statement that they are "ending" the contract, without the other side's agreement, is not dissolution; it must be analyzed as cancellation or another exit route instead.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 5 (Case)

**Facts:** A door-to-door salesperson rings the doorbell of consumer C's home, uninvited, and demonstrates a vacuum cleaner. C is persuaded on the spot and signs a purchase contract for EUR 800. The salesperson leaves without giving C any information about a right of withdrawal. Three days later, C has second thoughts and tells the trader by phone that she wants to return the vacuum cleaner and get her money back.

**Question:** Can C still effectively withdraw from the contract, and if so, within what time frame?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: C could be entitled to withdraw from the purchase contract with the trader under Sections 355, 312b BGB if the requirements of an off-premises withdrawal right are met.

II. Requirements:
- I. Purchase contract concluded (Section 433 BGB)
- II. Personal scope: consumer and trader
- III. Material scope: off-premises contract (Section 312b BGB)
- IV. No exclusion under Section 312g II BGB
- V. Declaration and timing (Section 355 BGB)

III. Application:
- I. A purchase contract for the vacuum cleaner was concluded when C signed on the spot.
- II. C acted for private, non-business purposes, so she is a consumer under Section 13 BGB; the salesperson's principal sells vacuum cleaners commercially, so it is a trader under Section 14 BGB.
- III. The contract was concluded in the simultaneous physical presence of both parties at a location other than the trader's business premises (C's home), following an unsolicited approach — this is the paradigm off-premises contract under Section 312b BGB.
- IV. Nothing in the facts suggests a custom-made or quickly perishable good; no exclusion applies.
- V. C's phone call three days later is a clear, unambiguous statement that she wants to return the goods — no specific form or the word "withdrawal" is required.

IV. Interim results: because no proper withdrawal instruction was given, the ordinary 14-day period under Section 355 II does not start running; under Section 356 III, the period is instead extended up to 12 months and 14 days from the point it otherwise would have started.

V. Final answer: C's declaration three days after signing falls well within the extended window, so C effectively withdraws from the contract; both parties must return what they received under the consumer withdrawal restitution regime (Section 357), and C is not required to give any justification.

### Question 6 (Case — integrates Week 05 + Week 03)

**Facts:** Consumer D calls trader T's telephone sales hotline. T's employee describes a designer lamp in detail and states a price of EUR 300. D immediately replies, "Yes, I'll take it, please send it to me." T ships the lamp the same week. T never sends D any information about a right of withdrawal. Ten months after receiving the lamp, D emails T saying the lamp does not suit her new interior and she wants to return it for a full refund.

**Question:** (a) Was a purchase contract validly concluded over the phone? (b) Can D still effectively withdraw ten months after delivery?

**Model Answer:**

**Part (a): Formation**

I. Claim/issue sentence: D and T concluded a valid purchase contract if there are two matching declarations of intent — offer and acceptance — and no validity obstacle applies (Section 433 BGB in conjunction with Sections 145-150 BGB).

II. Requirements:
- I. Offer: essentialia negotii present, clear intention to be bound (Section 145 BGB)
- II. Acceptance: matches the offer, timely under Section 147 I BGB
- III. No formation obstacle (Sections 125, 134, 138 BGB)

III. Application:
- I. T's employee named a specific lamp, described its features, and stated a fixed price of EUR 300 directly to D — this contains the essentialia negotii (parties, object, price) and, in a direct sales call naming a concrete price to a specific customer, shows a clear intention to be bound, so it is an offer, not a mere invitatio ad offerendum.
- II. A telephone call is treated as a declaration between parties who are "present" for timing purposes under Section 147 I BGB, so acceptance had to occur immediately during the call. D's "Yes, I'll take it, please send it" is an unconditional, matching acceptance given immediately.
- III. No statutory form is required for an ordinary consumer purchase of this kind, and nothing indicates a statutory prohibition or public-policy problem.

IV. Interim result: all formation requirements are met.

**Part (b): Withdrawal**

I. Claim/issue sentence: D could be entitled to withdraw from the purchase contract under Sections 355, 312c BGB if the requirements are met.

II. Requirements:
- I. Personal scope: consumer and trader
- II. Material scope: distance contract (Section 312c BGB)
- III. No exclusion under Section 312g II BGB
- IV. Declaration and timing (Sections 355, 356 BGB)

III. Application:
- I. D bought the lamp for private use, so she is a consumer (Section 13 BGB); T sells lamps commercially by phone, so T is a trader (Section 14 BGB).
- II. The contract was negotiated and concluded exclusively by phone, an organized means of distance communication, without simultaneous physical presence — a distance contract under Section 312c BGB.
- III. A standard designer lamp sold from stock is not custom-made or perishable; no exclusion applies.
- IV. T never gave D the required withdrawal instructions. Under Section 356 III BGB, the ordinary 14-day period (which would otherwise start on receipt of the goods, Section 356 II No. 1) does not begin, and the withdrawal right is instead capped at 12 months and 14 days from the point it would have started.

IV. Interim result: D's email ten months after delivery falls inside the extended 12-months-plus-14-days window.

V. Final answer: the purchase contract was validly concluded over the phone through immediate offer and acceptance under Sections 145 and 147 I BGB, and because T failed to instruct D on her withdrawal right, D's withdrawal ten months later is still effective under Section 356 III BGB — D may return the lamp and reclaim the EUR 300.

### Question 7 (Case)

**Facts:** Consultant K has a two-year advisory service contract with company U. Six months into the relationship, U's new CEO publicly accuses K of fraud in a press interview, with no factual basis, seriously damaging K's professional reputation, and U also stops paying K's last two invoices without explanation. K wants to end the contract immediately rather than wait out the remaining eighteen months.

**Question:** Can K extraordinarily cancel the service contract with immediate effect?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: K could be entitled to extraordinarily cancel the continuing service obligation under Section 314 BGB if the requirements are met.

II. Requirements:
- I. Compelling reason
- II. Expiry of a relief/warning period without result, unless dispensable
- III. Declaration of cancellation within a reasonable period
- IV. Effect: ex nunc

III. Application:
- I. Questionable is whether a compelling reason exists. This is supported by the fact that the CEO's public, unfounded fraud accusation is a serious breach of the duty of consideration owed within the relationship (Section 241 II BGB), and combined with the unexplained non-payment, it destroys the mutual trust an ongoing advisory relationship depends on. Against this, one could argue a single public statement might be an isolated lapse rather than a fundamental breakdown; however, a public reputational attack of this severity, paired with withheld payment, makes continuing the relationship for another eighteen months unreasonable for K when weighing both parties' interests.
- II. A relief or warning period would ordinarily be required first. However, gravity comparable to a serious and final refusal (analogous to Section 323 II BGB) can make this dispensable — publicly and falsely accusing a contract partner of fraud is not the kind of breach a warning notice could meaningfully cure, since the reputational harm and trust breakdown are already done.
- III. K should declare the cancellation without unreasonable delay after learning of the interview and the missed payments to stay within a reasonable period.

IV. Interim results: compelling reason met; warning period dispensable given the gravity; timely declaration assumed on the facts given.

V. Final answer: K can extraordinarily cancel the service contract with immediate effect under Section 314 BGB. The contract remains valid and owed for the first six months already performed — there is no retrospective restitution — and K may combine the cancellation with a damages claim under Section 314 IV BGB for the unpaid invoices and any recoverable reputational harm.

### Question 8 (Case)

**Facts:** Consumer M orders a custom-tailored suit from an online tailoring shop, submitting his exact body measurements and selecting a specific fabric and color combination made only for him. Ten days after receiving the finished suit, M decides he does not like how the chosen color looks in person and wants to withdraw from the contract for a full refund, even though the suit was made exactly to his specifications.

**Question:** Can M withdraw from the purchase contract?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: M could be entitled to withdraw from the purchase contract with the online tailor under Sections 355, 312c BGB if the requirements are met.

II. Requirements:
- I. Personal scope: consumer and trader
- II. Material scope: distance contract (Section 312c BGB)
- III. No exclusion under Section 312g II BGB
- IV. Declaration and timing (Section 355 BGB)

III. Application:
- I. M ordered the suit for private use, so he is a consumer (Section 13 BGB); the online tailoring shop sells commercially, so it is a trader (Section 14 BGB).
- II. The order was placed and concluded exclusively online, an organized distance-sales scheme — a distance contract under Section 312c BGB.
- III. Questionable is whether an exclusion applies. This is supported by Section 312g II BGB, which excludes the withdrawal right for goods manufactured to consumer specifications or clearly personalized to the consumer. A suit made to M's exact measurements and his chosen fabric/color combination is manufactured specifically for him and cannot be resold to another customer without substantial loss — it fits squarely within this exclusion.
- IV. Because the material-scope requirement already fails at the exclusion step, the declaration/timing requirement does not need to be reached.

IV. Interim result: the material-scope requirement is not met — the custom-made exclusion applies.

V. Final answer: M cannot withdraw from the purchase contract. The customization exclusion under Section 312g II BGB blocks the statutory withdrawal right despite the contract otherwise qualifying as a distance contract, so M remains bound to the suit purchase regardless of his change of mind about the color.

## Short-Answer / Theory Questions (Continued)

### Question 9

Explain why Section 314 BGB is described as "subordinate," and give an example of a special provision that would displace it.

**Model Answer:**
1. Section 314 BGB is the default model for extraordinary cancellation of continuing obligations, but it only applies when no more specific statutory cancellation rule governs the same relationship.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 314 BGB, displaced where a special provision exists (e.g. rental, employment, service, partnership, or consumer digital-content cancellation rules).
3. Requirements/features: (1) identify the type of continuing obligation first; (2) check whether a dedicated cancellation regime exists for that contract type; (3) only fall back to Section 314's four-step structure if no special provision covers the situation.
4. Example: cancellation of a residential lease is governed primarily by the special rental-termination rules, not by Section 314 directly, even though a lease is a continuing obligation.
5. Trap: memorizing the Section 314 four-step structure and reaching for it automatically for every continuing obligation is itself a trap — always ask "is there a more specific rule for this contract type" before invoking 314.

### Question 10

State the requirements for an off-premises contract under Section 312b BGB (List-Requirements Schema).

**Model Answer:**
Requirements:
1. A contract is concluded in the simultaneous physical presence of both consumer and trader at a place that is not the trader's business premises, OR the consumer made an offer under such circumstances.
2. The setting is closely related to a surprise situation — the consumer is approached where they do not expect to enter into a legally binding transaction.
3. The other general withdrawal conditions still apply on top: personal scope (consumer/trader) and no exclusion under Section 312g II BGB.

If these requirements are met, the consequence is that the consumer gains a statutory withdrawal right under Sections 355, 312b BGB, generally 14 days from conclusion (or later triggers), extendable if instruction is missing. This exists because a consumer approached away from a business setting — for example at their own front door — faces a pressure or surprise element that an ordinary shop visit does not carry, so the law compensates with a cooling-off exit.

### Question 11

Compare distance contracts and off-premises contracts.

**Model Answer (Compare Schema):**
A distance contract under Section 312c BGB triggers when the contract is negotiated and concluded exclusively through means of distance communication (letters, phone, email, digital services) without the parties ever being simultaneously physically present, within an organized distance-sales or service scheme; the core rationale is that the consumer cannot inspect the goods in person before contracting. An off-premises contract under Section 312b BGB triggers when the parties are physically present together, but away from the trader's business premises, typically in a surprise or unsolicited-approach situation; the core rationale is pressure or surprise rather than an inability to inspect. Contrast example: ordering a lamp over the phone is a distance contract (no physical presence at all); a vacuum-cleaner sale concluded at the consumer's front door after an unannounced doorbell visit is an off-premises contract (physical presence, but away from business premises). Exam trap: do not assume every non-shop sale is "off-premises" — if the parties were never physically together at all, the correct route is the distance-contract provision, not the off-premises one, even though both lead to the same withdrawal mechanics under Sections 355, 357 BGB.

### Question 12

Explain the consequence of a trader's failure to properly instruct a consumer about the withdrawal right, and state the outer time limit.

**Model Answer:**
1. Proper withdrawal instruction is a precondition for the ordinary 14-day withdrawal clock to start running.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 356 III BGB (extension), read together with Sections 355 II and 356 II No. 1 BGB (ordinary period and its normal starting point).
3. Requirements/features: (1) the trader has a duty to inform the consumer of the withdrawal right before or at contract conclusion; (2) if that instruction is missing or defective, the 14-day period simply does not begin; (3) the withdrawal right is nonetheless not infinite — it is capped at 12 months and 14 days from the point the ordinary period would otherwise have started.
4. Example: a consumer who receives no withdrawal instructions after an online purchase can still validly withdraw ten months after delivery, because the extended cap has not yet expired.
5. Trap: do not conclude that a missing instruction gives the consumer a withdrawal right forever — the extension is generous but still capped at 12 months plus 14 days, not unlimited.

### Question 13

Explain how a dissolution agreement can loop back into the rescission doctrine from Contract Law II, and why this matters for exam analysis.

**Model Answer:**
1. Dissolution is the consensual termination of a contract by agreement, resting on private autonomy, but the agreement itself is a fresh declaration of intent and is therefore vulnerable to the same validity attacks as any other declaration.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 123 BGB (rescission for deceit or unlawful duress), applied to the dissolution agreement itself rather than to the original underlying contract.
3. Requirements/features: (1) identify that a dissolution agreement was reached; (2) check whether the consent to dissolve was procured through unlawful duress or deceit; (3) if so, the dissolution agreement itself — not necessarily the original contract — can be rescinded under Section 123 BGB.
4. Example: an employer who coerces an employee into "agreeing" to dissolve the employment contract under threat of unfounded criminal accusations has not obtained valid consent, and the employee may rescind the dissolution agreement.
5. Trap: do not treat "the parties signed a dissolution agreement" as automatically the end of the analysis — always check whether the consent behind that agreement was itself validly formed, since a coerced dissolution is functionally reopened through Section 123 BGB rescission of the dissolution declaration.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions (Continued)

### Question 14 (Case)

**Facts:** Tenant R has an indefinite-period gym membership contract with fitness studio F. R simply no longer wants to attend and wishes to end the membership, without pointing to any breach or compelling reason — she just wants out with reasonable notice.

**Question:** Can R end the membership without showing a compelling reason?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: R could be entitled to end the gym membership through ordinary cancellation without needing a compelling reason, since the membership is a continuing obligation of indefinite duration.

II. Requirements:
- I. Continuing obligation
- II. Contract is for an indefinite period
- III. Notice period observed

III. Application:
- I. A gym membership is a continuing obligation — an ongoing relationship of repeated performance (access to facilities) in exchange for periodic payment, not a one-time exchange.
- II. Questionable is whether the membership is fixed-term or indefinite. The facts describe no stated end date, and R is treated as free to leave subject only to notice — this points to an indefinite-period relationship rather than a fixed term requiring a compelling reason under Section 314 BGB.
- III. Because the obligation is indefinite, ordinary cancellation without any reason is available, provided R observes whatever notice period applies to the membership.

IV. Interim results: the membership is a continuing obligation of indefinite duration, so the compelling-reason requirement of Section 314 BGB does not need to be satisfied.

V. Final answer: R can end the gym membership through ordinary cancellation with proper notice, without showing any compelling reason — Section 314 BGB's stricter four-step structure is reserved for fixed-term continuing obligations or situations without a simpler ordinary-notice route.

### Question 15 (Case)

**Facts:** Employer B tells employee A that unless A signs a dissolution agreement ending her employment "voluntarily" today, B will report A to the police for a theft B knows never happened. Frightened, A signs the dissolution agreement. Two weeks later, A wants to have the dissolution agreement set aside and be reinstated.

**Question:** Can A have the dissolution agreement set aside?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: A could be entitled to rescind the dissolution agreement under Section 123 BGB if her consent to dissolve was procured through unlawful duress.

II. Requirements:
- I. A declaration of intent (the dissolution agreement)
- II. Threat of a considerable harm
- III. Unlawfulness of the threat or the means-end connection
- IV. Causality between the threat and the declaration
- V. Timely rescission declaration

III. Application:
- I. A signed a dissolution agreement, a declaration of intent aimed at consensually ending the employment relationship.
- II. Being reported to the police and facing a criminal investigation is a considerable harm to A.
- III. Questionable is whether the threat was unlawful. This is supported by the fact that B knew the theft accusation was false — threatening to report a fabricated crime is not a legitimate means to obtain agreement, unlike, say, threatening a lawful and truthful report of genuine wrongdoing. Against this, one could argue B was merely proposing a hypothetical consequence rather than "threatening" in a legal sense; however, conditioning A's job on signing "or else I report a crime I know you did not commit" is precisely the kind of illegitimate pressure Section 123 BGB targets. Consequently, the threat is unlawful.
- IV. A signed under the immediate pressure of the false accusation threat; nothing suggests she would have signed the dissolution agreement absent that threat, so causality is present.
- V. A must exercise rescission without undue delay after discovering she is free to do so; two weeks after the incident, and presumably shortly after realizing her rights, is within a reasonable window on these facts.

IV. Interim results: all requirements of Section 123 BGB duress-based rescission are met with respect to the dissolution agreement.

V. Final answer: A can rescind the dissolution agreement under Section 123 BGB. Because the dissolution agreement itself is set aside, it never validly ended the employment relationship, and A's employment continues as if the "voluntary" dissolution had never been signed.

### Question 16 (Case — integrates Week 05 + Week 08)

**Facts:** Consumer P orders a coffee machine from an online retailer's webshop. On delivery, P discovers the machine has a cracked water tank that was already cracked when it left the factory — a material defect present at handover. P is unhappy and, four days after delivery, emails the retailer saying she "wants to send it back and get her money back," without mentioning the crack at all, simply stating she changed her mind about needing a coffee machine.

**Question:** Which route(s) can P rely on, and does it matter that she didn't mention the defect?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: P could be entitled to return the coffee machine and reclaim her money either through the statutory consumer withdrawal right (Sections 355, 312c BGB) or through the warranty regime (Sections 434, 437 BGB), and the two routes must be checked separately because they rest on different facts.

II. Requirements:
- I. Withdrawal: personal scope, distance-contract material scope, no exclusion, timely declaration
- II. Warranty: valid purchase agreement, material defect at transfer of risk, no exclusion, choice of remedy

III. Application:
- I. Withdrawal route: P bought for private use as a consumer (Section 13 BGB) from a commercial retailer (Section 14 BGB); the order was placed entirely online, an organized distance-sales scheme, so it is a distance contract under Section 312c BGB; a standard coffee machine is not custom-made or perishable, so no Section 312g II exclusion applies; P's email four days after delivery, stating she wants to return the item and get her money back, is a sufficiently clear declaration with no defect or justification required, and falls comfortably within the ordinary 14-day period (or the extended period if instruction was missing). Withdrawal is therefore independently available regardless of the crack.
- II. Warranty route: a purchase agreement was concluded (Section 433 BGB); the cracked water tank is a deviation from the condition the machine should have had, present already at handover — a material defect under Section 434 BGB existing at transfer of risk (Section 446 BGB); nothing on the facts suggests P knew of the crack at conclusion, so no Section 442 exclusion applies; P could therefore also demand cure (repair or replacement) under Section 437 No. 1, 439 BGB as a first step, or, once cure fails or is dispensable, move toward revocation and refund under Sections 437 No. 2, 440, 323 BGB.
- III. Questionable is whether P's silence about the defect defeats either route. This is supported by the fact that withdrawal never requires a defect or any justification at all — the reason P gives ("changed her mind") is irrelevant to whether the withdrawal requirements are met. Against this, one might think that because a real defect exists, P is somehow forced into the warranty route instead — however, nothing in the withdrawal doctrine requires the consumer to disclose or rely on the true motivation, and nothing in the warranty doctrine bars a consumer from choosing the faster, no-questions-asked withdrawal route even where a defect happens to also exist. Consequently, the two routes coexist, and P may choose the simpler one.

IV. Interim results: both a valid withdrawal right and a valid warranty claim exist on these facts, and P's failure to mention the defect does not defeat either.

V. Final answer: P can validly withdraw under Sections 355, 312c BGB without needing to invoke the defect at all, since withdrawal requires no justification; the existence of a genuine material defect under Section 434 BGB gives her an independent, alternative warranty claim under Section 437 BGB if she preferred cure instead of a full return, but on these facts the simpler withdrawal declaration she actually sent is sufficient by itself to entitle her to return the machine and reclaim her money.

### Question 17 (Case — integrates Week 05 + Week 08)

**Facts:** Merchant X buys a batch of machine components from merchant Y for X's factory (a mutual commercial purchase between two merchants). On delivery, X properly and promptly inspects the goods under its commercial duty and discovers a material defect. X validly notifies Y in time. Y attempts a repair, but the repair fails to fix the defect. X now wants to end the contract and get its money back, and describes this in an internal memo as "withdrawing from the purchase."

**Question:** Is "withdrawal" the legally correct label for what X wants to do, and can X achieve this outcome?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: X could be entitled to end the contract and recover its payment, but the correct legal route is revocation under Sections 437 No. 2, 440, 323 BGB rather than the consumer withdrawal right of Sections 355, 312b/312c BGB, because X is a merchant relying on a failed cure, not a consumer exercising a cooling-off right.

II. Requirements (warranty/revocation route):
- I. Valid purchase agreement between merchants (Section 433 BGB)
- II. Material defect at transfer of risk (Sections 434, 446 BGB)
- III. Commercial inspection/notice duty satisfied (Section 377 HGB)
- IV. No exclusion (Section 442 BGB)
- V. Cure attempted and failed (Sections 439, 440, 323 BGB)

III. Application:
- I. X and Y concluded a purchase agreement for the components as merchants.
- II. The components had a material defect already present at handover, satisfying Sections 434, 446 BGB.
- III. As a merchant-to-merchant sale, Section 377 HGB required X to inspect and notify without delay; the facts state X did so properly, so the claim is preserved rather than deemed approved.
- IV. Nothing indicates X knew of the defect at conclusion, so Section 442 BGB does not exclude the claim.
- V. Y was given the chance to cure through repair (Section 439 BGB), consistent with cure's priority over immediate termination, but the repair failed to remedy the defect — this satisfies the "cure failed" trigger that opens the door to revocation under Sections 437 No. 2, 440, 323 BGB.
- VI. Questionable is whether X's own label, "withdrawal," is legally accurate. This is supported by the definitional split established for Contract Law III: withdrawal (Widerruf) under Sections 355, 312b/312c BGB is a no-fault, no-defect consumer-trader cooling-off right, requiring consumer/trader personal scope that X and Y — both merchants — do not have. Against this, one could argue the everyday English word "withdraw" fits what X is doing (getting out of the contract); however, in the doctrinal scheme taught for this course, the applicable remedy after a failed cure in a valid reciprocal contract is called revocation, not withdrawal, precisely because it responds to a performance failure rather than a pure change of mind. Consequently, X's internal memo uses the wrong legal label even though the underlying outcome — ending the contract and reclaiming payment — is available.

IV. Interim results: the warranty requirements for revocation after a failed cure are met; the personal/material scope for consumer withdrawal is not met because X and Y are both merchants, not a consumer-trader pair.

V. Final answer: X can end the contract and recover its payment, but the correct route and label is revocation under Sections 437 No. 2, 440, 323 BGB following Y's failed repair — not "withdrawal," which is reserved for the consumer-protection cooling-off right and does not apply to a merchant-to-merchant sale at all.

### Question 18 (Case — integrates Week 05 + Week 06)

**Facts:** Online trader T's pre-formulated terms and conditions, used in every one of T's consumer sales, include a clause stating: "The statutory right of withdrawal does not apply to any purchase made through this webshop." Consumer Q, who bought a jacket from T's webshop and received it a few days ago, wants to withdraw and is told by T that the clause blocks her from doing so.

**Question:** Is T's clause effective against Q?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: T's clause excluding the statutory withdrawal right is standard business terms that must pass the SBT control regime, and it is ineffective under content control, so Q's withdrawal right is unaffected.

II. Requirements:
- I. Existence of SBT (Section 305 I BGB)
- II. Incorporation (Section 305 II BGB)
- III. Content control (Sections 307-309 BGB)
- IV. Consequence (Section 306 BGB)

III. Application:
- I. The clause is pre-formulated by T, used in every one of T's consumer sales (well above the practical repeated-use threshold), and presented unilaterally to consumers like Q — it is SBT under Section 305 I BGB.
- II. Nothing suggests the clause was hidden or that Q lacked a reasonable opportunity to take notice of it within T's terms and conditions, so incorporation under Section 305 II BGB is assumed satisfied for purposes of reaching content control.
- III. Questionable is whether the clause survives content control. As a consumer contract, the direct Section 307-309 route applies. This is supported by the course's own Section 307 example that a clause "excluding a right to rescind despite serious performance failure" is a textbook illustration of a clause conflicting with basic principles of statutory law and threatening the contract's purpose — an outright exclusion of the statutory consumer withdrawal right is the same kind of move, stripping Q of a core consumer-protection mechanism the legislator specifically built into distance and off-premises sales. Against this, one could argue traders should be free to define their own terms of sale under private autonomy; however, the withdrawal right is a mandatory consumer-protection rule precisely because ordinary private autonomy is considered insufficiently protective in distance/off-premises settings, so a blanket contractual override conflicts with the basic principles of that statutory scheme and unreasonably disadvantages the consumer. Consequently, the clause fails content control under Section 307 BGB.
- IV. Because the clause fails content control, it falls out of the contract under Section 306 I BGB; the rest of the purchase contract survives, and the statutory withdrawal rules fill the gap under Section 306 II BGB precisely as if the clause had never been agreed.

IV. Interim results: the clause is SBT, is incorporated, but fails content control under Section 307 BGB and therefore does not become effective.

V. Final answer: T's clause is not effective against Q. It falls out of the contract under Section 306 BGB, the remainder of the purchase contract survives, and Q retains her full statutory withdrawal right under Sections 355, 312c BGB exactly as if T's exclusion clause had never existed.

## Short-Answer / Theory Questions (Continued 2)

### Question 19

Explain the consumer-protection restitution regime under Section 357 BGB and how it differs from ordinary revocation restitution.

**Model Answer:**
1. Section 357 BGB sets out the return duties that follow a valid consumer withdrawal, and it is deliberately gentler on the consumer than the ordinary revocation restitution rules.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 357 BGB (consumer withdrawal effects), contrasted with Sections 346-349 BGB (ordinary revocation restitution).
3. Requirements/features: (1) both parties return what they received, mirroring the general restitution idea; (2) the consumer may be required to bear the direct cost of returning the goods (Section 357 V BGB) if properly informed of this in advance; (3) the consumer may owe compensation for any deterioration in the goods, but only for handling that went beyond what was necessary to examine the goods, similar to how a customer would handle an item in a shop.
4. Example: a consumer who withdraws from an online clothing purchase after trying the item on at home in the ordinary way owes no deterioration compensation, but a consumer who used the item extensively before returning it may owe compensation for the excess wear.
5. Trap: do not import the full ordinary revocation restitution regime (Sections 346-349 BGB) into a withdrawal case — withdrawal's Section 357 regime is a distinct, consumer-favorable set of rules, and confusing the two can lead to overstating what the consumer owes back.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions (Continued 2)

### Question 20 (Case)

**Facts:** Consumer N calls an online furniture retailer's ordinary customer sales line and orders a standard, ready-made bookshelf model directly from the retailer's regular catalog (not customized in any way), to be delivered to her home. The bookshelf arrives exactly as described, with no damage or defect. A week after delivery, N decides she no longer likes how the bookshelf looks in her living room and wants to send it back for a full refund. She has not spoken to the retailer about ending the contract by mutual agreement, and there is no ongoing relationship between N and the retailer beyond this single purchase.

**Question:** Applying the full range of contract-exit routes covered this term, which route (if any) allows N to get her money back, and why do the other routes not apply?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: N could be entitled to return the bookshelf and reclaim her payment only if one of the five exit routes covered this term actually fits these facts; the correct route must be identified by ruling out the others.

II. Requirements (as headings):
   I. Rescission (Sections 119, 120, 123 BGB)
   II. Revocation (Sections 323, 324, 326 V BGB)
   III. Withdrawal (Sections 355, 312b, 312c BGB)
   IV. Cancellation (Section 314 BGB)
   V. Dissolution (offer and acceptance)

III. Application:
   I. Rescission requires a flaw in how N's own declaration of intent was formed — a mistake, transmission error, deceit, or duress. Nothing in the facts suggests N misunderstood what she was ordering, was misled, or was pressured; she got exactly the bookshelf she meant to order. Rescission does not apply.
   II. Revocation requires a performance problem in an otherwise valid contract — late, missing, or defective performance. The bookshelf arrived exactly as described, with no damage or defect, so there is no performance failure to revoke over. Revocation does not apply.
   III. Withdrawal requires a consumer-trader relationship in a statutory withdrawal situation (distance or off-premises), with no exclusion and a timely declaration. N ordered by phone from a retailer's ordinary sales line — an organized means of distance communication without simultaneous physical presence — for private use, so this is a distance contract under Section 312c BGB between a consumer (Section 13 BGB) and a trader (Section 14 BGB). A standard, off-the-shelf bookshelf is not custom-made or perishable, so no Section 312g II exclusion applies, and a request to return the item a week after delivery falls within the ordinary 14-day period. Withdrawal fits.
   IV. Cancellation requires a continuing obligation — an ongoing relationship of repeated performance. A single bookshelf purchase is a one-time exchange, not a continuing obligation, so cancellation has no continuing relationship to attach to. Cancellation does not apply.
   V. Dissolution requires mutual agreement to end the contract through a fresh offer and acceptance. N has not negotiated any termination agreement with the retailer — she is acting unilaterally. Dissolution does not apply.

IV. Interim results: rescission, revocation, cancellation, and dissolution are all ruled out on these facts; only withdrawal's requirements are met.

V. Final answer: N can get her money back only through withdrawal under Sections 355, 312c BGB. She may declare withdrawal without giving any reason within the 14-day period, and both parties must return what they received under Section 357 BGB; none of the other four exit routes has any factual basis here.

### Question 21 (Case)

**Facts:** Company V employed marketing manager W under an indefinite-term employment contract for the past three years. After W is caught deliberately leaking V's confidential client list to a competitor, V extraordinarily cancels the employment contract with immediate effect under Section 314 BGB. V then also demands that W repay all salary V paid over the past three years, arguing that because the contract is now "undone," W must return everything received, just as a buyer must return goods after revoking a purchase.

**Question:** Is V's demand for repayment of three years of past salary justified?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: V has no claim to recover W's past salary through the cancellation itself, because extraordinary cancellation under Section 314 BGB only ends the employment relationship for the future and does not trigger restitution of past performances.

II. Requirements (as headings):
   I. Effect of cancellation under Section 314 BGB
   II. Applicability of Sections 346-349 BGB restitution to a continuing obligation

III. Application:
   I. V validly cancelled the employment contract for a compelling reason — W's deliberate leak of confidential client information to a competitor is a serious breach of the duty of loyalty owed within the employment relationship, and given the gravity, a prior warning was likely dispensable. The cancellation ends the contract ex nunc, immediately and only for the future.
   II. Questionable is whether V can nonetheless demand return of the three years of salary already paid, by analogy to the mutual restitution owed after a revocation under Sections 346-349 BGB. This is supported by the surface similarity: both cancellation and revocation are exit routes that end a contract before its natural conclusion. Against this, the course material is explicit that Sections 346-348 BGB restitution is not suitable for continuing obligations, because a relationship built on ongoing, already-consumed performance (W's three years of work) cannot realistically be unwound retrospectively — W cannot "return" three years of labor already rendered and used by V, and V received real value for the salary paid throughout that period. However, applying revocation-style restitution here would produce an absurd and one-sided result, stripping W of past earnings for work already properly performed, which is precisely why cancellation is deliberately structured to operate ex nunc rather than ex tunc. Consequently, no restitution of the past salary is owed; V's only additional remedy, if any, lies in a separate damages claim under Section 314 IV BGB for losses caused by the disloyal conduct, not in reversing the entire employment relationship.

IV. Interim results: the cancellation is valid and ends the relationship immediately, but only for the future; Sections 346-349 BGB restitution does not apply to this continuing obligation.

V. Final answer: V's demand for repayment of three years of salary is not justified. Cancellation under Section 314 BGB works ex nunc — the employment relationship is validly ended going forward, past salary for work already performed is not returned, and V may only pursue a separate damages claim under Section 314 IV BGB for losses flowing specifically from W's breach, not a full unwinding of the past three years.

### Question 22 (Case — integrates Week 05 + Week 04)

**Facts:** Consumer J orders a used vintage watch from an online dealer's webshop, described in the listing as "genuine Swiss movement, fully serviced in 2025." J pays and receives the watch five days ago. J has since discovered, through an independent watchmaker, that the movement is not Swiss at all and was never serviced in 2025 — the online dealer knew this was false when writing the listing but wrote it anyway to secure the sale. J is furious and wants out of the contract as fast and as cleanly as possible.

**Question:** What route(s) can J rely on to get out of the contract, and does it matter which one J chooses?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: J could be entitled to get out of the contract either by rescinding for deceit under Section 123 BGB or by exercising the ordinary consumer withdrawal right under Sections 355, 312c BGB, and the two routes exist in parallel with different deadlines and consequences.

II. Requirements (as headings):
   I. Rescission for deceit (Section 123 BGB)
   II. Withdrawal (Sections 355, 312c BGB)

III. Application:
   I. Rescission: a declaration of intent was made when J agreed to buy the watch; the dealer's statement that the movement was genuine Swiss and serviced in 2025 was factually false; questionable is whether this amounts to deceit. This is supported by the facts stating the dealer knew the description was false and wrote it anyway to secure the sale — a deliberate false representation of fact intended to induce the purchase is textbook deceit under Section 123 BGB. J's decision to buy was caused by the false description, since nothing suggests J would have bought a watch he knew had a non-Swiss, unserviced movement at the same price. Rescission is available, and because deceit (not an ordinary mistake) is involved, J has a full year from discovery to declare it under Section 124 BGB, not the short "immediate" clock that applies to Section 119/120 mistakes.
   II. Withdrawal: J bought for private use as a consumer (Section 13 BGB) from a commercial online dealer (Section 14 BGB); the purchase was concluded entirely online, an organized distance-sales scheme, so it is a distance contract under Section 312c BGB. A used vintage watch sold from an existing listing is not custom-made or perishable, so no Section 312g II exclusion applies. J's wish to get out five days after receipt, even without mentioning the false description at all, would be a sufficient, no-justification-needed withdrawal declaration if made within the 14-day period (or the extended period if instructions were missing).
   III. Questionable is whether the existence of both routes matters for J's practical choice. This is supported by the different consequences: rescission under Section 123 BGB, once declared, voids the contract retroactively from the start (Section 142 I BGB) and additionally opens the door to damages for the deceit itself, while withdrawal simply ends the contract for the future via the consumer-protection restitution regime under Section 357 BGB, with no separate damages claim built into the withdrawal mechanism itself. Against this, one might think J should always pick withdrawal because it is simpler and needs no proof of deceit; however, if J wants to also pursue damages for having been deliberately misled, or if the 14-day withdrawal window has already lapsed, rescission under Section 123 BGB (with its much longer one-year clock) remains available where withdrawal would not be. Consequently, the two routes are not interchangeable, and which one serves J better depends on what J wants (a clean exit only, versus an exit plus damages) and how much time has passed.

IV. Interim results: both rescission for deceit and consumer withdrawal are independently available to J on these facts, with different deadlines (one year vs. 14 days) and different consequences (retroactive avoidance plus possible damages vs. forward-looking no-fault exit).

V. Final answer: J can get out of the contract either by rescinding under Section 123 BGB or by withdrawing under Sections 355, 312c BGB; the choice matters because rescission voids the contract from the start and preserves a damages claim for the deceit, with a full year to act, while withdrawal is simpler but only unwinds the contract going forward with no built-in damages claim — J should consider pursuing rescission if he wants compensation for having been deliberately misled, especially once the shorter withdrawal window is a concern.
