## Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 1

Explain reimbursement of futile expenses under Section 284 BGB.

**Model Answer:**
1. Definition: Reimbursement of futile expenses lets a creditor claim back expenses reasonably incurred in reliance on receiving proper performance, instead of claiming damages instead of performance.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 284 BGB.
3. Requirements: (1) the general requirements for damages instead of performance are otherwise met (via Section 280 I, III with 281, 283, or 311a); (2) the expense was made in the creditor's own interest and in reliance on receiving proper performance; (3) the expense was reasonable and would have been recouped through proper performance.
4. Example: a buyer pays vehicle registration costs anticipating delivery of a car the seller never conforms to the contract.
5. Consequence/trap: Section 284 is either/or with damages instead of performance for the same expense, and it excludes expenses incurred once non-performance was already foreseeable, or expenses grossly disproportionate to the value of performance.

### Question 2

Compare damages in addition to performance and damages instead of performance.

**Model Answer:**
Damages in addition to performance trigger when the loss would still exist even if the debtor rendered proper performance at the latest possible moment (e.g. a defective product damages other property), anchored in Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I BGB, leading to a straightforward claim with no additional-period requirement.
Damages instead of performance trigger when proper performance would have removed or avoided the loss (e.g. the defective item itself just needs fixing or replacing), anchored in Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III with 281 (curable), 283 (subsequently impossible), or 311a (initially impossible) BGB, and for the 281 route specifically require a reasonable additional period to have expired without result.
Contrast example: defective fuel that both damages an engine (in addition) and itself needs replacing (instead) — one fact pattern, two separate routes.
Exam trap: do not label every loss arising from a defect as "damages instead" merely because the underlying good was defective — always run the hypothetical-performance test first.

### Question 3

Compare small damages and big damages.

**Model Answer:**
Small damages trigger when the buyer keeps the defective item and claims the value deficit as compensation, anchored in Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB, leading to payment of the difference between a defect-free item's value and the defective item's value, conditional on seller responsibility.
Big damages trigger when the buyer gives up the entire defective performance and claims the non-fulfilment loss (e.g. the extra cost of a substitute purchase elsewhere), anchored in the same Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB, but the payment replaces the whole performance rather than closing a value gap.
Contrast example: a EUR 10,000 machine worth EUR 7,000 defective — small damages claims the EUR 3,000 gap while keeping the machine; big damages returns the machine and claims the EUR 2,000 extra cost of a EUR 12,000 replacement bought elsewhere.
Exam trap: both look similar to reduction/revocation because the buyer ends up with money either way, but damages require seller responsibility while reduction and revocation do not.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 4 (Case)

**Facts:** F sells industrial lubricant to a machine shop M for EUR 500. Due to a mixing error at F's warehouse, the lubricant delivered is corrosive rather than the ordinary type M ordered. F did not notice the mix-up. M's technician uses it in a precision lathe; the lathe's bearings are destroyed, costing EUR 4,000 to repair. M wants F to pay both the EUR 4,000 repair cost and refund the EUR 500 paid for the unusable lubricant. M has not yet asked F for a replacement delivery of correct lubricant.

**Question:** Does M have a claim against F for (a) the EUR 4,000 lathe repair and (b) the EUR 500 lubricant price, and under what legal bases?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: M could have a claim against F for damages under Section 437 No. 3 in conjunction with Section 280 I BGB for the lathe repair, and separately under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB for the lubricant price, if the requirements of these provisions are met.
II. Requirements:
   I. Valid purchase contract (Section 433 BGB).
   II. Defect at transfer of risk (Section 434, 446 BGB).
   III. For the lathe repair: obligation, breach, responsibility, causal damage (Section 280 I elements) — damages in addition.
   IV. For the lubricant price: the same elements plus, for Section 281, a reasonable additional period that expired without result, unless dispensable.
III. Application:
   I. Purchase contract: M and F concluded a purchase contract for lubricant under Section 433 BGB when M ordered and F agreed to supply it for EUR 500.
   II. Defect: Questionable is whether the delivered lubricant was defective. This is supported by the fact that it was corrosive rather than the ordinary type agreed, so it lacked the agreed quality under Section 434 II 1 No. 1 BGB. Consequently, the lubricant was defective at transfer of risk (Section 446 BGB).
   III. Lathe repair — damages in addition: Questionable is whether the EUR 4,000 repair is damages in addition to performance. This is supported by the hypothetical-performance test: even if F delivered conforming lubricant now, the lathe damage would remain. Against this, one could argue the damage flows directly from the defect and should follow the defect's own route. However, consequential damage to other property caused by a defective item is not cured by later proper performance. Responsibility is presumed under Section 280 I 2 BGB and not rebutted, since F's own mixing error caused the wrong delivery. Consequently, the lathe repair is damages in addition to performance under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I BGB.
   IV. Lubricant price — damages instead: Questionable is whether the EUR 500 lubricant price is damages instead of performance. This is supported by the fact that conforming lubricant could still cure the defect entirely — the defect concerns the lubricant itself, not other property. However, Section 281 additionally requires a reasonable additional period for cure that expired without result, unless dispensable under Section 281 II or Section 440 BGB. The facts show M never demanded cure and F never refused it. Consequently, this requirement is not met on the facts as given.
IV. Interim results: the lathe-repair claim is established (damages in addition); the lubricant-price claim is not yet established because the additional-period requirement under Section 281 has not been satisfied or dispensed with.
V. Final answer: As a result, M can currently claim the EUR 4,000 lathe repair from F as damages in addition to performance under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I BGB, but cannot yet claim the EUR 500 lubricant price as damages instead of performance unless M first sets F a reasonable additional period for cure (or such a period is dispensable).

### Question 5 (Case)

**Facts:** F sold a used delivery van to bakery B for EUR 12,000. The transmission was defective at handover; a defect-free equivalent van would be worth EUR 12,000, while the van's actual value with the defect is EUR 9,000. B keeps using the van as-is and writes to F: "I am not returning the van, and I want you to compensate me because I effectively overpaid due to the transmission problem — I hold you responsible for not disclosing this."

**Question:** Should B's claim be routed as small damages or as reduction, and what does each route require B to show?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: B's claim could be small damages under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB or reduction under Sections 437 No. 2, 441 BGB, depending on how B's demand is properly classified.
II. Requirements: small damages requires seller responsibility (Section 280 I 2 BGB, presumed but rebuttable) in addition to the warranty gateway; reduction requires no fault, only the warranty gateway and a reduction declaration under Section 441 I BGB.
III. Application: Questionable is whether B's letter is a demand for compensation (damages) or a price adjustment (reduction). This is supported by B's own wording — B explicitly "holds F responsible," asserting fault-based compensation for a loss, not merely adjusting the price to the lower value. Against this, one could argue the economic outcome (keeping the van, receiving EUR 3,000) is identical either way, so the label should not matter. However, the two routes carry different legal requirements: small damages presupposes F's responsibility, which must be checked and could be rebutted if F neither knew nor should have known of the defect, while reduction requires none. Consequently, because B's letter asserts fault, this is small damages, not reduction, and the responsibility requirement must be affirmatively established.
IV. Interim result: the requirements for small damages are met only if F's responsibility for the defect is shown or not rebutted; reduction would apply regardless of fault and yields the same EUR 3,000 figure through the proportional formula.
V. Final answer: As a result, B's demand as framed is small damages instead of performance under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB for EUR 3,000, conditional on F's responsibility being established or not rebutted; if F disproves responsibility, B would need to fall back on reduction under Sections 437 No. 2, 441 BGB for the same amount, without needing to prove fault.

### Question 6 (Case — integrates Week 09 + Week 04)

**Facts:** P buys a vintage racing bicycle advertised by seller Q as "an original 1985 frame, hand-built by the famous frame-builder Enzo Colnago," paying EUR 8,000. After delivery, P has the bicycle authenticated and learns the frame is a well-made modern replica worth about EUR 1,200. Q genuinely believed the frame was authentic when selling it, relying on paperwork that later turns out to have been forged by an earlier owner in the chain of title. P wants to undo the deal and recover the EUR 8,000.

**Question:** Should P's claim be pursued as rescission for an essential-characteristic mistake under Section 119 II BGB, or through the warranty route? Advise P.

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: P's claim could be pursued through rescission for an essential-characteristic mistake (Section 119 II BGB) or through the warranty gateway (Section 437 BGB), with revocation under Sections 437 No. 2, 323, 346 BGB as the likely remedy if warranty law applies.
II. Requirements: (I) determine whether the frame's inauthenticity is a formation-stage mistake (119 II) or a transfer-stage material defect (434 II); (II) purchase contract; (III) defect and transfer of risk; (IV) revocation requirements, including Nachfrist and its exclusions; (V) legal consequence.
III. Application: Questionable is whether Q's own mistaken belief about the frame's origin allows reliance on Section 119 II BGB. This is supported by the fact that authorship/origin of a collectible item is exactly the kind of "essential characteristic" Section 119 II covers in the abstract. Against this, one could argue that Section 119 II rescission is a defense available to the party who made the mistaken declaration — here Q, the seller — and does not assist P as buyer; moreover, once a purchased item is delivered and found not to match its agreed quality, that is precisely what warranty law under Section 434 BGB is designed to handle, not a mistake analysis on the buyer's side. However, applying rescission analysis to a delivered good that turns out defective would bypass the tiered warranty-remedy system (cure first) that Section 437 specifically builds in. Consequently, P's route is warranty law, not rescission: the frame's actual authorship differs from the agreed quality (an authentic 1985 hand-built Colnago frame), so it lacks the agreed quality under Section 434 II 1 No. 1 BGB, and the defect existed at transfer of risk (Section 446 BGB) since the frame was never actually authentic. Revocation ordinarily requires a Nachfrist for cure, but cure — turning a replica into an authentic original — is objectively impossible, so the additional period is dispensable. No fault/responsibility is required for revocation under Sections 437 No. 2, 323, 346 BGB.
IV. Interim result: P cannot pursue Section 119 II rescission as buyer; P's route is the Section 437 warranty gateway, and within it, revocation is available without needing to show Q's fault, because cure is objectively impossible.
V. Final answer: As a result, P should be advised to pursue revocation of the purchase contract under Sections 437 No. 2, 323, 346 BGB (Nachfrist dispensable due to impossibility of cure), not rescission under Section 119 II BGB; upon a valid revocation declaration (Section 349 BGB), P returns the bicycle and recovers the EUR 8,000 purchase price under Sections 346 ff. BGB.

### Question 7 (Case)

**Facts:** Interior fit-out company I delivers custom-built cabinetry to office tenant O for EUR 15,000. Several drawer fronts are warped due to a manufacturing defect. O immediately emails I: "This is unacceptable, I don't want you anywhere near my office again — I am buying replacement cabinetry from someone else and will bill you the difference." I is never given a chance to inspect or cure. Two weeks later, O buys replacement cabinetry from another supplier for EUR 17,000 and demands the EUR 2,000 difference from I as damages.

**Question:** Can O successfully claim the EUR 2,000 as big damages instead of performance without first setting I an additional period for cure?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: O could have a claim against I for EUR 2,000 in big damages instead of performance under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB if the requirements, including the additional-period rule, are met.
II. Requirements: (I) valid supply contract, (II) defect at transfer of risk, (III) reasonable additional period for cure that expired without result, or a dispensing exception, (IV) responsibility, (V) causal damage in the amount claimed.
III. Application: Questionable is whether O needed to set I a Nachfrist before buying elsewhere. This is supported by the general rule that damages instead of performance under Section 281 requires exactly that — a reasonable additional period must expire unsuccessfully first, preserving the debtor's chance to cure. Against this, one could argue O's email was so final in tone that it functions as an implicit refusal of cure. However, the dispensability exceptions under Section 281 II BGB require a serious and definitive refusal by the debtor — here it is O, not I, who unilaterally shuts out the opportunity for cure; I never refused. Consequently, O's own refusal to allow cure does not satisfy the exception.
IV. Interim result: the additional-period requirement under Section 281 is not met and not validly dispensed with.
V. Final answer: As a result, O cannot currently claim the EUR 2,000 as damages instead of performance, because O never gave I a reasonable additional period to cure and no recognized exception applies; O would first need to have set (or been excused from setting) such a period.

### Question 8 (Case)

**Facts:** Homeowner H hires contractor K to design and build a custom walk-in wine cellar in H's basement, including all materials and labor, for a lump sum of EUR 40,000. After H formally accepts the finished cellar, H notices the climate-control unit K installed does not maintain the agreed humidity range.

**Question:** Which warranty regime governs H's claim, and what remedies — including any not found in ordinary sale-of-goods warranty law — are available to H?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: H could have a claim against K for cure, self-help, reduction, revocation, or damages under the works-contract warranty regime (Section 634 BGB and its referrals) if the requirements are met — not under the ordinary purchase warranty gateway (Section 437 BGB).
II. Requirements: (I) classify the contract type, (II) confirm acceptance of the work (Section 640 BGB) as the relevant timing point, (III) confirm a defect under Section 633 BGB, (IV) select the appropriate Section 634 remedy and its own sub-requirements.
III. Application: Questionable is whether the cellar project is a purchase contract or a works contract. This is supported by the fact that K did not merely deliver a pre-made item but designed and built a bespoke result — a custom wine cellar meeting a specific agreed outcome (the humidity range) — which is the hallmark of a works contract under Section 631 BGB, not a sale under Section 433 BGB. Against this, one could argue materials were also supplied, which sounds sale-like. However, where labor and a specific promised result dominate the transaction, works-contract law under Section 631 BGB and its warranty referral in Section 634 BGB applies. Consequently, this is a works contract. The decisive timing point is acceptance of the work under Section 640 BGB, already satisfied here, and the humidity-control failure means the work lacks the agreed quality under Section 633 BGB. Available remedies under Section 634 BGB are: cure (Section 634 No. 1, 635 BGB, with the contractor — not the customer — choosing the type of cure, unlike purchase law); self-help (Section 634 No. 2, 637 BGB, letting H fix it and bill K, a remedy purchase law does not have); revocation (Section 634 No. 3 with Section 323 BGB); reduction (Section 634 No. 3 with Section 638 BGB); and damages or reimbursement (Section 634 No. 4 with Sections 280 ff. or 284 BGB), following the same damages logic as this week's purchase-law analysis.
IV. Interim result: the works-contract warranty regime applies, and H has the full remedy menu under Section 634 BGB, including self-help, which purchase law does not offer.
V. Final answer: As a result, H should pursue a claim under the works-contract warranty regime (Section 634 BGB), most naturally starting with a demand for cure under Sections 634 No. 1, 635 BGB (with K choosing the method), and escalating to self-help, reduction, revocation, or damages if cure fails.

## Additional Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 9

Explain secondary-duty damages under Section 282 BGB, using the drunk-craftsman fact pattern to illustrate.

**Model Answer:**
1. Definition: Secondary-duty damages under Sections 280 I, III, 282 BGB compensate for damages instead of performance when a breach of a secondary duty (Section 241 II BGB) destroys the trust basis of the contract, making continued performance by that debtor unacceptable to the creditor.
2. Statutory anchor: Sections 280 I, III, 282 BGB, referencing Section 241 II BGB.
3. Requirements: (1) breach of a secondary duty, not the main performance duty; (2) the breach destroys the base of trust; (3) the breach is relevant to the purpose of the contract; (4) weighing of interests makes further performance by that debtor unacceptable.
4. Example: a craftsman properly installs a kitchen but works drunk, smokes, and damages the floor. The floor damage is damages in addition to performance under Sections 280 I, 241 II BGB, since it survives correct later performance; the cost of hiring a new craftsman to finish the job is damages instead of performance under Sections 280 I, III, 282 BGB, because the trust basis for continuing with this particular craftsman is destroyed.
5. Trap: Section 282 is not a catch-all for "the debtor behaved badly" — it specifically requires that the secondary-duty breach be serious enough to undermine confidence in that debtor's ability to properly perform the main duty, not just any incidental misconduct.

### Question 10

Explain when the additional-period (Nachfrist) requirement under Section 281 BGB is dispensable.

**Model Answer:**
1. Definition: Section 281 damages instead of performance ordinarily require a reasonable additional period for cure that expires without result; certain circumstances dispense with this requirement.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 281 II BGB (general dispensability); Section 440 BGB (purchase-law-specific dispensability).
3. Requirements/features: (1) under Section 281 II, dispensable if the debtor seriously and definitively refuses performance, or special circumstances justify immediate damages after weighing both parties' interests; (2) under Section 440 BGB, also dispensable if both cure types fail, are refused, or are unreasonable for the buyer, mirroring revocation-dispensability logic; (3) objective impossibility of the needed cure routes around Section 281 entirely via Sections 275/283.
4. Example: a seller who states "I will never repair or replace this item" makes a serious and definitive refusal that dispenses with the Nachfrist requirement under Section 281 II.
5. Trap: the buyer's own refusal to allow cure does not dispense with the additional-period requirement — only the debtor's own serious refusal or an objective impossibility qualifies (see Question 7 above, where the buyer's own refusal to let the seller cure did not count).

### Question 11

Compare Section 283 BGB and Section 311a BGB damages instead of performance.

**Model Answer (Compare Schema):**

Section 283 damages instead of performance apply when performance becomes impossible after the contract was validly concluded, anchored in Sections 280 I, III, 283 BGB together with Section 275 BGB, and require no additional period since demanding one for genuinely impossible performance would be pointless. Section 311a damages instead of performance apply when performance was already impossible at the moment of contract conclusion, anchored in Section 311a II BGB, and additionally require that the debtor knew or should have known of the initial impossibility. Contrast example: a seller promises to deliver a specific vintage machine that is destroyed by fire the week after the contract is signed — Section 283 applies; if the same machine had already been destroyed the day before the contract was signed and neither party knew, Section 311a governs instead, and additionally asks whether the seller should have known the machine no longer existed. Exam trap: do not default to Section 283 for every "impossible" fact pattern — always fix the exact moment impossibility arose relative to contract conclusion before choosing between Sections 283 and 311a.

### Question 12

Distinguish "damage" from a "futile expense," and state the difference hypothesis.

**Model Answer:**
1. Definition: damage under Sections 249 ff. BGB is an involuntary loss of material or immaterial goods/interests; a futile expense under Section 284 BGB is voluntary spending made in reliance on receiving proper performance.
2. Statutory anchor: Sections 249 ff. BGB (damage); Section 284 BGB (futile expenses).
3. Requirements/features: (1) difference hypothesis formula: damage = hypothetical situation without the damaging event minus the actual situation; (2) damage categories are material (Sections 249 ff.), immaterial (Section 253, only if a statute allows compensation), and lost profit (Section 252); (3) a futile expense is not "damage" in this sense because it was spent voluntarily rather than lost involuntarily, so it needs its own gateway (Section 284) rather than Sections 249 ff.
4. Example: a buyer's engine destroyed by defective fuel is damage (involuntary loss, difference hypothesis applies); a buyer's voluntary vehicle-registration fee paid in reliance on receiving a conforming car is a futile expense (Section 284), not damage.
5. Trap: Section 284 and damages instead of performance are either/or for the same expense — a claimant cannot recover a given outlay as both damages instead of performance and as a Section 284 futile expense.

## Additional Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 13 (Case)

**Facts:** Print shop P buys a used industrial printer from dealer D for EUR 20,000. The printer's color calibration sensor is defective at handover; a properly calibrated equivalent machine is worth EUR 20,000, while the actual machine with the defect is worth EUR 16,000. P keeps using the printer for black-and-white jobs and writes to D demanding EUR 4,000 "in compensation because you are responsible for selling me a defective machine." D responds that the sensor defect was caused by a manufacturing fault that neither D nor any reasonable dealer performing a standard pre-sale check could have detected.

**Question:** Can P recover the EUR 4,000 as small damages, and what does D's response change?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: P could have a claim against D for EUR 4,000 in small damages instead of performance under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB, if D's responsibility for the defect is established and not rebutted.
II. Requirements: (I) valid purchase contract; (II) defect at transfer of risk; (III) P's demand properly classified as damages, not reduction; (IV) D's responsibility under Section 280 I 2 BGB, presumed but rebuttable; (V) value-deficit amount.
III. Application: the printer sale is a valid purchase contract (Section 433 BGB), and the sensor defect deviates from the agreed/objective quality under Section 434 II BGB, present at handover under Section 446 BGB. P's letter explicitly frames the demand as "compensation because you are responsible," so, as with the fault-based wording seen elsewhere in this set, this is small damages, not reduction. Questionable is whether D can rebut the presumed responsibility under Section 280 I 2 BGB. This is supported by D's factual claim that neither D nor any reasonable dealer performing a standard pre-sale check could have detected the manufacturing-origin defect — this speaks to an absence of negligence under Section 276 BGB. Against this, one could argue dealers bear a general risk for what they sell. However, Section 280 I 2 BGB only presumes responsibility and allows the debtor to disprove intent and negligence, and an undetectable manufacturing defect is exactly the kind of fact that rebuts negligence. Consequently, if D's factual claim is proven, D is not responsible under Section 280 I 2 BGB.
IV. Interim result: the defect and its classification as small damages are established, but D's responsibility — the decisive requirement — is successfully rebutted on the facts given.
V. Final answer: As a result, P cannot recover the EUR 4,000 as small damages because D has rebutted the presumption of responsibility; P would instead need to fall back on reduction under Sections 437 No. 2, 441 BGB, which reaches the same EUR 4,000 figure via the proportional formula without requiring D's fault.

### Question 14 (Case — integrates Week 09 + Week 08)

**Facts:** Bakery B buys a commercial oven from supplier S for EUR 25,000. The oven's door seal is defective at handover, causing heat loss; a defect-free equivalent oven is worth EUR 25,000, the actual oven with the defect is worth EUR 23,000, replacing the entire oven would cost S EUR 25,000, and repairing just the seal would cost S EUR 300. B demands S replace the entire oven. S refuses, offering only to repair the seal; B refuses that offer, sets S no further deadline, and claims EUR 2,000 as small damages.

**Question:** Was S entitled to refuse B's chosen cure type, and does that affect B's damages claim?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: whether B can claim EUR 2,000 in small damages depends first on whether S validly refused B's chosen cure type (replacement) under Section 439 IV BGB, and second on whether B's own refusal of the remaining valid cure option (repair) forecloses the damages claim.
II. Requirements: (I) valid purchase contract; (II) defect at transfer of risk; (III) Section 439 IV disproportionality test for the refused cure type; (IV) effect of B's refusal of the remaining cure type on the Section 281 additional-period/dispensability requirement.
III. Application: the oven purchase and seal defect at handover are established (Sections 433, 434, 446 BGB). Questionable is whether S could refuse replacement under Section 439 IV BGB. This is supported by the stark cost gap: replacement costs S EUR 25,000 against a EUR 300 repair that fully resolves the defect — precisely the gross disproportion Section 439 IV lets the seller avoid, weighing defect-free value, defect significance, and whether the other cure type works without substantial disadvantage to B. Against this, one could argue B has an unqualified right to choose between the two cure types under Section 439 I BGB. However, Section 439 IV specifically limits that choice where one type is disproportionately expensive and the other remains fully effective and reasonable for the buyer — here repair fully eliminates the heat-loss defect. Consequently, S validly refused only the replacement type, not cure as such, and B remained restricted to repair. Because B then refused the very cure type S was entitled to insist on, and set no further deadline, the Section 281 additional-period requirement is not satisfied; B's own refusal is not a dispensing "serious and definitive refusal" by the debtor S under Section 281 II, since S never refused cure altogether, only the disproportionate type.
IV. Interim result: S validly limited B to the repair option under Section 439 IV BGB; B's refusal of that valid cure option, without setting a further deadline, means the Section 281 additional-period requirement remains unmet.
V. Final answer: As a result, S was entitled to refuse replacement but not repair, and B cannot currently claim the EUR 2,000 as small damages because B refused the cure option S was entitled to offer instead of setting S a deadline for it; B would first need to give S a reasonable opportunity to repair the seal.

### Question 15 (Case — integrates Week 09 + Week 11)

**Facts:** Wholesaler W (a merchant) sells 500 packaged industrial gaskets to manufacturer M (also a merchant) for EUR 10,000, a mutual commercial purchase. An employee of M signs for the delivery, but the shipment sits unopened in M's warehouse for three weeks due to a production slowdown. When finally opened, 200 gaskets are the wrong diameter — a defect that would have been visible on a routine visual count-and-sample check. M immediately notifies W and claims EUR 4,000 in small damages for the value deficit of the wrong-diameter gaskets.

**Question:** Can M successfully pursue the EUR 4,000 small-damages claim against W?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: M's EUR 4,000 small-damages claim under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB is only viable if M first cleared the Section 377 HGB inspection-and-notice gateway that precedes any Section 437 BGB warranty claim in a mutual commercial purchase.
II. Requirements: (I) mutual commercial purchase between merchants (Section 343 HGB); (II) prompt inspection and notice of defects under Section 377 HGB; (III) if that gateway is cleared, the ordinary Section 437/280 ff. small-damages requirements.
III. Application: W and M are both merchants and the gasket sale is a mutual commercial purchase under Section 343 HGB, so Section 377 HGB applies before Section 437 BGB is reached. Questionable is whether M's three-week delay in opening the shipment satisfies the "promptly" requirement of Section 377 HGB. This is supported by the fact that the wrong diameter was discoverable on a routine visual count-and-sample check — exactly the ordinary-course inspection Section 377 HGB expects a merchant buyer to perform without delay. Against this, one could argue M's production slowdown is a legitimate business reason for the delay. However, Section 377 HGB ties the inspection duty to the ordinary course of business, not the buyer's own scheduling convenience, and the employee's receipt of the goods is attributed to M under Section 278 BGB regardless of who within M's organization caused the delay. Consequently, a three-week delay in opening and inspecting a routinely checkable delivery is not prompt, and the gaskets are deemed approved under Section 377 HGB.
IV. Interim result: M failed to satisfy the Section 377 HGB inspection-and-notice duty, so the goods are deemed approved.
V. Final answer: As a result, M cannot pursue the EUR 4,000 small-damages claim, or any Section 437 BGB warranty remedy, against W, because the goods are deemed approved under Section 377 HGB for M's failure to inspect and notify promptly — this bars the claim regardless of how genuine the diameter defect actually was.

### Question 16 (Case)

**Facts:** Gallery G contracts to sell art dealer A a specific antique sculpture for EUR 50,000, to be delivered next month after G completes a scheduled restoration. Before delivery, the restoration workshop suffers a fire and the unique sculpture is destroyed. G had no direct involvement in the fire, but the workshop's faulty wiring — which G had been warned about twice by the workshop's own inspector and failed to have fixed — caused the blaze. A demands EUR 50,000 as damages instead of performance without ever setting G a deadline for delivery.

**Question:** Does A need to set a deadline, and is G responsible for the destruction?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: A's claim is for damages instead of performance due to subsequent impossibility under Sections 280 I, III, 283 BGB, which does not require a deadline but does require G's responsibility for the impossibility.
II. Requirements: (I) valid contract for the specific sculpture; (II) performance became impossible after conclusion (Section 275 I BGB); (III) no additional period needed for the Section 283 route; (IV) responsibility for the impossibility (Section 276 BGB, presumed under Section 280 I 2 but rebuttable).
III. Application: G and A concluded a valid contract for a specific, non-fungible sculpture. Performance became genuinely impossible under Section 275 I BGB once the unique sculpture was destroyed by fire before delivery. Questionable is whether A must first set G a deadline. This is supported by the general rule that Section 283, unlike Section 281, applies to subsequently impossible performance precisely because a deadline would be pointless — no amount of additional time restores a destroyed unique object. Consequently, no deadline is required. Questionable is whether G is responsible for the fire. This is supported by the fact that G was twice warned about the faulty wiring by the workshop's own inspector and failed to have it fixed — a clear failure to exercise the care of a reasonable party under Section 276 BGB. Against this, one could argue G did not personally start the fire and did not control the workshop's premises. However, the standard is not who lit the match but whether G's own failure to act on a known, specific risk contributed negligently to the loss, and ignoring two direct warnings about faulty wiring is exactly that kind of negligent omission. Consequently, G is responsible for the impossibility.
IV. Interim result: performance is subsequently impossible, no deadline is required under Section 283, and G's responsibility is established through negligent disregard of known warnings.
V. Final answer: As a result, A does not need to set G a deadline and can claim the EUR 50,000 as damages instead of performance under Sections 280 I, III, 283 BGB, because G is responsible for the impossibility.

### Question 17 (Case)

**Facts:** Restaurant owner R buys a custom-built pizza oven from manufacturer P for EUR 18,000, to be installed by a specific date so R can open a new location. In reliance on timely delivery, R pays EUR 1,500 to a contractor to build a reinforced platform for the oven and pays EUR 500 for a permit specific to that oven model. P never delivers; after a reasonable additional period passes without result, R cancels and buys a comparable oven elsewhere. The platform remains usable for the substitute oven, but the EUR 500 permit was specific to P's oven model and is now worthless.

**Question:** Can R recover the EUR 500 permit fee, and what about the EUR 1,500 platform cost?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: R may recover the EUR 500 permit fee as reimbursement of a futile expense under Section 284 BGB, while the EUR 1,500 platform cost is not futile and falls outside Section 284's reach.
II. Requirements: (I) the requirements for damages instead of performance are otherwise met; (II) the expense was made in R's own interest and in reliance on proper performance; (III) the expense was reasonable; (IV) the expense would have been recouped through proper performance.
III. Application: the requirements for damages instead of performance are met in the background — P failed to deliver by the deadline, a reasonable additional period expired without result, and P's non-delivery is presumptively its responsibility under Section 280 I 2 BGB, not rebutted on the facts. Questionable is whether the EUR 500 permit fee qualifies as a futile expense under Section 284 BGB. This is supported by the fact that R paid it in reliance on receiving P's specific oven, it was a reasonable step given the planned installation, and it would have been recouped had P delivered as promised — but because the permit is specific to P's oven model, it is now worthless following P's non-performance, satisfying the futility element. Questionable is whether the EUR 1,500 platform cost is also futile. This is supported by the fact that it, too, was spent in reliance on the contract. Against this, one could argue both expenses should be treated identically since both preceded the same broken promise. However, Section 284 requires that the expense have failed to achieve its purpose — the platform remains usable for the substitute oven R ultimately bought, so its purpose was still achieved. Consequently, only the permit fee lost its value because of the non-performance.
IV. Interim result: the EUR 500 permit fee satisfies all Section 284 requirements and is a futile expense; the EUR 1,500 platform cost served its purpose regardless of P's breach and is not futile.
V. Final answer: As a result, R can recover the EUR 500 permit fee from P under Section 284 BGB as reimbursement of a futile expense, but cannot recover the EUR 1,500 platform cost, since that expense was not rendered futile by P's non-performance.

### Question 18 (Case — integrates Week 09 + Week 08)

**Facts:** Furniture retailer F sells a designer sofa to buyer C for EUR 6,000. The sofa's fabric has a dye-batch defect visible on close inspection; a defect-free sofa of the same design is worth EUR 6,000, the actual sofa is worth EUR 4,800. Without first asking F for repair or a replacement sofa, C writes: "I'm keeping the sofa but I am reducing what I owe you — please refund the difference; I'm not blaming you for it, these things happen with dye batches." F had already delivered and been paid in full.

**Question:** Is C's demand governed by reduction or small damages, and does C need to have given F a chance to cure first?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: C's demand, as framed, is reduction under Sections 437 No. 2, 441 BGB, not small damages under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB, and the two routes differ on whether a prior cure opportunity is required.
II. Requirements: (I) valid purchase contract; (II) defect at transfer of risk; (III) proper classification of C's demand; (IV) for reduction, a right to revoke as the gateway condition, itself requiring the ordinary cure-first sequence unless dispensable; (V) reduction formula.
III. Application: the sofa purchase and dye-batch defect at handover are established (Sections 433, 434 II, 446 BGB). Questionable is whether C's letter is reduction or small damages. This is supported by C's own wording — C explicitly disclaims blame ("I'm not blaming you... these things happen"), the opposite framing from a fault-based compensation demand, and asks only for a price adjustment to the lower value. Against this, one could argue the economic outcome (keep sofa, get EUR 1,200) looks identical to small damages. However, as with the small-damages-versus-reduction test used elsewhere in this set, the legal route turns on whether the buyer is asserting fault-based compensation or a no-fault price adjustment, and C's language signals the latter. Consequently, this is reduction, not small damages. Questionable is whether C needed to give F a chance to cure first. This is supported by the rule, carried over from Warranty Rights I, that reduction requires a right to revoke as its gateway (Sections 437 No. 2, 441 I BGB reference the same preconditions as revocation), which ordinarily requires cure to have first failed, been refused, or been dispensable — C never asked F for repair or replacement and points to no dispensing circumstance. However, unlike a damages claim, reduction does not additionally require fault, so once the cure-first gateway is satisfied reduction follows automatically from the value gap. Consequently, C's demand is correctly classified as reduction, but the cure-first gateway it depends on is not yet shown to be satisfied.
IV. Interim result: the demand is reduction, not small damages, but the cure-first gateway underlying the right to reduce has not yet been shown to be satisfied or dispensable.
V. Final answer: As a result, C's demand should be routed as reduction under Sections 437 No. 2, 441 BGB, using the formula `reduced price = agreed price × actual value / defect-free value` = EUR 6,000 × EUR 4,800 / EUR 6,000 = EUR 4,800, yielding a refund of EUR 1,200 — but only once C is shown to have first given F a genuine opportunity to cure (or such an opportunity is dispensable), since reduction's gateway requires the same cure-first sequence as revocation even though reduction itself needs no fault.

## Further Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 19

Explain how a seller's guarantee under Section 443 BGB changes the responsibility analysis for damages claims under Section 280 I BGB.

**Model Answer:**
1. Definition: A guarantee (Garantie) under Section 443 BGB is a voluntary additional promise by the seller (or manufacturer) that the item has certain qualities or will retain them, layered on top of statutory warranty rights; once a genuine guarantee is given, the responsibility standard under Section 276 I BGB can be stricter than ordinary intent/negligence, so the guarantor answers for the guaranteed outcome without needing separate proof of fault.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 443 BGB (guarantee); Section 276 I BGB (permits an assumed, stricter standard of responsibility); Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I BGB (the damages claim itself).
3. Requirements/features: (1) a genuine guarantee must first be identified — express guarantees are straightforward, but a tacit (implied) guarantee is possible only where the seller's conduct or statements clearly go beyond ordinary sales talk, and course materials flag this threshold as high; (2) once a guarantee is established, the responsibility element of Section 280 I BGB is satisfied by the guaranteed quality simply being absent — the seller cannot rebut responsibility by showing an absence of fault, unlike the ordinary Section 280 I 2 presumption; (3) Section 444 BGB reinforces this by blocking any warranty-exclusion clause where a guarantee was given.
4. Example: a seller who advertises a used generator as "guaranteed to run for 500 hours without failure" cannot escape a damages claim by proving they neither knew nor should have known about a hidden manufacturing flaw that caused failure after 100 hours — the guarantee itself, not fault, is the basis of liability.
5. Trap: do not treat every confident sales statement as a guarantee — casual assurances or ordinary advertising puffery do not meet the high threshold for a tacit guarantee, and without a genuine guarantee the seller keeps the ordinary chance to rebut the Section 280 I 2 responsibility presumption.

## Further Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 20 (Case)

**Facts:** Antique dealer A agrees to sell collector C a specific 18th-century clock that A describes from a photograph sent by a consignor. A and C sign the sale contract on 3 March. Unknown to both parties, the clock had already been destroyed in a warehouse fire on 1 March, two days before the contract was signed. C demands damages instead of performance. A protests that C never set a deadline for delivery.

**Question:** Does C need to have set A a deadline, and can C recover damages given that neither party knew about the fire at the time of contracting?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: C's claim is for damages instead of performance due to initial impossibility under Section 311a II BGB, which does not require a deadline but does require that A knew or should have known of the impossibility at the time of contracting.
II. Requirements: (I) a contract was concluded for a specific, non-fungible item; (II) performance was already impossible at the moment of conclusion (Section 275 I BGB, tested as of contract conclusion rather than afterward); (III) no additional period is needed for the Section 311a route; (IV) A knew or, applying reasonable care, should have known that the clock no longer existed.
III. Application: A and C concluded a valid contract on 3 March for a specific clock. Questionable is whether Section 283 or Section 311a governs, since both deal with impossibility. This is supported by the fact that the clock was destroyed on 1 March, before the 3 March contract — fixing the moment of impossibility before conclusion routes the case to Section 311a, not Section 283, which only covers impossibility arising after conclusion. Consequently, no deadline is required under either route, since demanding one for a destroyed unique object would be pointless. Questionable is whether A knew or should have known of the fire. This is supported by the fact that A relied solely on a photograph sent by the consignor and never independently verified the clock's continued existence immediately before signing. Against this, one could argue A had no realistic way to learn of a fire that happened only two days earlier in someone else's warehouse. However, Section 311a II BGB specifically asks whether A, applying the care reasonably expected of an antique dealer transacting on a stranger's unverified photograph, should have confirmed the item's continued existence before contracting — the facts as given do not show A made any such basic check. Consequently, A's failure to verify supports a finding that A should have known.
IV. Interim result: the contract-conclusion-versus-impossibility timing places this under Section 311a, dispensing with any deadline requirement; A's failure to verify the clock's existence before contracting supports a finding that A should have known of the impossibility.
V. Final answer: As a result, C does not need to have set A a deadline, and C can claim damages instead of performance under Section 311a II BGB, because the impossibility already existed at contract conclusion and A should have known of it through a basic pre-contract check.

### Question 21 (Case)

**Facts:** Furniture maker M sells and installs a custom wardrobe for buyer K. M subcontracts the installation to an independent carpentry crew, C-Crew, whom M regularly uses for deliveries. During installation, a C-Crew worker carelessly drills through a live electrical cable in K's wall, causing a short circuit that damages K's home entertainment system worth EUR 2,000. M argues M personally did nothing wrong and cannot be held responsible for C-Crew's carelessness.

**Question:** Is M responsible for the EUR 2,000 damage despite not personally causing it?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: K could have a claim against M for EUR 2,000 in damages under Sections 280 I, 241 II BGB for breach of a care duty during performance of the installation, with C-Crew's negligence attributed to M under Section 278 BGB, if the requirements are met.
II. Requirements: (I) a valid contract obliging M to install the wardrobe, generating an ancillary duty of care toward K's other property (Section 241 II BGB); (II) breach of that duty; (III) responsibility, including attribution of a performance-assistant's fault under Section 278 BGB; (IV) causal damage that would survive even correct later performance of the wardrobe installation itself.
III. Application: M and K concluded a contract under which M owed proper installation of the wardrobe, carrying an ancillary duty under Section 241 II BGB not to damage K's other property while doing so. Questionable is whether M is responsible for C-Crew's carelessness given that M personally did nothing wrong. This is supported by Section 278 BGB, which attributes the fault of a person the debtor uses to perform their obligation to the debtor as if it were the debtor's own fault — M chose to use C-Crew to carry out the installation M owed to K, so C-Crew acted within the scope of M's own obligation. Against this, one could argue M exercised no direct control over how C-Crew's individual worker handled the drill, so fault should stay with C-Crew alone. However, Section 278 BGB does not require the debtor's own control over the helper's methods — it is enough that the helper was used to perform the debtor's obligation, and installation was squarely within that scope. Consequently, C-Crew's careless drilling is attributed to M as if M had drilled through the cable personally. The cable damage to the entertainment system would remain even if the wardrobe were later installed correctly, since the harm is to separate property rather than the wardrobe itself.
IV. Interim result: C-Crew's negligence is attributed to M under Section 278 BGB, and the entertainment-system damage survives correct later performance of the installation.
V. Final answer: As a result, M is responsible for the EUR 2,000 damage despite not personally causing it, because Section 278 BGB attributes C-Crew's carelessness to M, and K can claim the EUR 2,000 under Sections 280 I, 241 II BGB.

### Question 22 (Case)

**Facts:** Furniture retailer F sells buyer D a dining table for EUR 3,000. On delivery, D notices a single small scratch, about 2cm long, on the underside of the table, invisible unless the table is tipped over — cosmetically and functionally irrelevant to ordinary use. D immediately refuses the table entirely, buys an equivalent table elsewhere for EUR 3,200, and demands the EUR 200 price difference as big damages instead of performance, without ever asking F to touch up the scratch.

**Question:** Can D recover the EUR 200 as big damages instead of performance for the whole contract?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: D's claim for EUR 200 as big damages instead of the entire performance under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB fails if the scratch is an insignificant defect excluded under Section 281 I 3 BGB.
II. Requirements: (I) valid purchase contract; (II) a defect at transfer of risk; (III) the defect must not be insignificant, or Section 281 I 3 BGB bars damages instead of the entire performance; (IV) if not excluded, the ordinary Nachfrist and responsibility requirements.
III. Application: F and D concluded a purchase contract for the table (Section 433 BGB), and the underside scratch is a deviation from the agreed/expected quality under Section 434 II BGB, present at handover. Questionable is whether D can claim big damages for the whole contract given the scratch's minor character. This is supported by Section 281 I 3 BGB, which expressly excludes damages instead of the entire performance where the breach of duty is insignificant — a defect described as a small, cosmetically and functionally irrelevant mark on a surface never seen in ordinary use is exactly the kind of minor deviation this exclusion targets. Against this, one could argue any deviation from the agreed defect-free condition entitles the buyer to full big-damages treatment. However, Section 281 I 3 BGB specifically singles out insignificant defects to prevent buyers from using a trivial flaw to unwind an entire high-value contract, and the scratch here affects neither function nor visible appearance. Consequently, the defect is insignificant, and big damages for the whole contract are excluded regardless of whether D set a deadline.
IV. Interim result: the scratch is an insignificant defect under Section 281 I 3 BGB, which bars big damages instead of the entire performance.
V. Final answer: As a result, D cannot recover the EUR 200 as big damages instead of the entire performance; D would at most be limited to small damages for the value deficit caused specifically by the scratch (subject to F's responsibility) or reduction, not a claim treating the whole delivery as rejected.

### Question 23 (Case)

**Facts:** Boutique owner N buys a designer display cabinet from supplier V for EUR 9,000. The cabinet's glass shelving arrives cracked, a defect V fails to cure after N sets a reasonable deadline. N writes to V: "I no longer want anything to do with this cabinet — please take it back and return my EUR 9,000." N has not bought a replacement elsewhere and states no lost profit or extra cost.

**Question:** Is N's demand big damages instead of performance or revocation, and what does the classification change?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: N's demand, as framed, is revocation under Sections 437 No. 2, 323, 346 BGB, not big damages instead of performance under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB, and the two routes differ on whether V's responsibility must be shown.
II. Requirements: (I) valid purchase contract; (II) defect at transfer of risk; (III) proper classification of N's demand as unwinding (revocation) or loss-compensation (big damages); (IV) for revocation, an expired Nachfrist and no exclusion; for big damages, the same procedural gateway plus V's responsibility.
III. Application: N and V concluded a purchase contract for the cabinet (Section 433 BGB), and the cracked shelving is a material defect under Section 434 II BGB present at handover; V failed to cure after a reasonable deadline set by N, satisfying the Nachfrist requirement common to both routes. Questionable is whether N's letter should be read as revocation or as big damages. This is supported by N's own wording — N asks only to return the cabinet and get the EUR 9,000 back, stating no additional loss such as a substitute-purchase price difference or lost profit; this is a pure request to unwind the exchange, not a demand for compensation of an extra loss. Against this, one could argue any request for "my money back" could be read loosely as a damages demand. However, big damages specifically compensates a non-fulfilment loss beyond the price itself (e.g. a pricier replacement), which N does not claim, while revocation simply reverses the exchange of performances — N's letter matches the latter. Consequently, this is revocation, not big damages.
IV. Interim result: N's demand is properly classified as revocation; the Nachfrist requirement is already satisfied.
V. Final answer: As a result, N can revoke the purchase contract under Sections 437 No. 2, 323, 346 BGB and recover the EUR 9,000 purchase price against return of the cabinet, without needing to show V's responsibility for the defect — had N instead claimed an extra loss beyond the price (e.g. a pricier replacement), that portion would need to be routed as big damages under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB and would require V's responsibility to be established or not rebutted.

### Question 24 (Case — integrates Week 09 + Week 05)

**Facts:** Consumer P orders a wireless speaker online from retailer Q for EUR 300, a distance contract under Section 312c BGB. Q ships proper withdrawal instructions with the delivery. Twenty days after receiving the speaker, P discovers a manufacturing defect that causes intermittent audio cutouts — a material defect under Section 434 BGB present at handover. P wants to return the speaker and get a full refund with the least hassle.

**Question:** Is P's 14-day consumer withdrawal right still available, and if not, what route remains open, and by when must P act?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: P's route to return the speaker and recover the EUR 300 could run through either consumer withdrawal under Sections 312c, 312g, 355 BGB or the warranty gateway under Section 437 BGB, but only one of the two remains available on these facts.
II. Requirements: (I) for withdrawal: distance-contract status, no exclusion under Section 312g II BGB, and the 14-day period (Sections 355 II, 356 II No. 1 BGB) not yet expired; (II) for warranty: valid purchase contract, defect at transfer of risk, no exclusion, and the applicable warranty limitation period (Section 438 I No. 3 BGB, two years) not yet expired.
III. Application: P ordered the speaker from Q, a distance contract under Section 312c BGB. Questionable is whether P can still withdraw. This is supported by the general 14-day withdrawal window under Section 355 II BGB. Against this, the facts state that Q shipped proper withdrawal instructions with the delivery, so the clock started running on receipt of the goods under Section 356 II No. 1 BGB and was not extended under Section 356 III BGB, which only extends the period where instructions were missing or wrong. Since P discovered the defect twenty days after receipt — six days past the 14-day mark — the withdrawal right has already lapsed, and nothing in the facts suggests P withdrew earlier. Consequently, the withdrawal route is closed. Questionable is whether the warranty route remains open. This is supported by the fact that the speaker's intermittent audio cutouts are a material defect under Section 434 BGB present at handover (Section 446 BGB), triggering the Section 437 gateway, and the applicable limitation period for warranty claims on movable goods is two years from delivery under Section 438 I No. 3 BGB — twenty days is far inside that window. Against this, one could argue the online-distance facts should default P to withdrawal since that is a "no questions asked" route. However, where the facts emphasize a defect/performance failure, the performance route leads and withdrawal is only a fallback where its own window is still open — here withdrawal's own 14-day window is independently expired regardless of the defect. Consequently, P must pursue the warranty route.
IV. Interim result: the withdrawal right expired six days before P acted and cannot be revived by the later-discovered defect; the warranty route remains available for nearly two more years.
V. Final answer: As a result, P's 14-day withdrawal right is no longer available, but P can still pursue the warranty route under Section 437 BGB — ordinarily starting with a demand for cure under Section 439 BGB, and escalating to revocation (Sections 437 No. 2, 323, 346 BGB) or reduction (Sections 437 No. 2, 441 BGB) if cure fails or is dispensable — with up to two years from delivery under Section 438 I No. 3 BGB to act.
