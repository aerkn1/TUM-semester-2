## Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 1

Explain reimbursement of futile expenses under Section 284 BGB.

**Model Answer:**
1. Definition: Reimbursement of futile expenses lets a creditor claim back expenses reasonably incurred in reliance on receiving proper performance, instead of claiming damages instead of performance.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 284 BGB.
3. Requirements: (1) the general requirements for damages instead of performance are otherwise met (via Section 280 I, III with 281, 283, or 311a); (2) the expense was made in the creditor's own interest and in reliance on receiving proper performance; (3) the expense was reasonable and would have been recouped through proper performance.
4. Example: a buyer pays vehicle registration costs anticipating delivery of a car the seller never conforms to the contract.
5. Consequence/trap: Section 284 is either/or with damages instead of performance for the same expense, and it excludes expenses incurred once non-performance was already foreseeable, or expenses grossly disproportionate to the value of performance.

### Question 2

Compare damages in addition to performance and damages instead of performance.

**Model Answer:**
Damages in addition to performance trigger when the loss would still exist even if the debtor rendered proper performance at the latest possible moment (e.g. a defective product damages other property), anchored in Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I BGB, leading to a straightforward claim with no additional-period requirement.
Damages instead of performance trigger when proper performance would have removed or avoided the loss (e.g. the defective item itself just needs fixing or replacing), anchored in Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III with 281 (curable), 283 (subsequently impossible), or 311a (initially impossible) BGB, and for the 281 route specifically require a reasonable additional period to have expired without result.
Contrast example: defective fuel that both damages an engine (in addition) and itself needs replacing (instead) — one fact pattern, two separate routes.
Exam trap: do not label every loss arising from a defect as "damages instead" merely because the underlying good was defective — always run the hypothetical-performance test first.

### Question 3

Compare small damages and big damages.

**Model Answer:**
Small damages trigger when the buyer keeps the defective item and claims the value deficit as compensation, anchored in Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB, leading to payment of the difference between a defect-free item's value and the defective item's value, conditional on seller responsibility.
Big damages trigger when the buyer gives up the entire defective performance and claims the non-fulfilment loss (e.g. the extra cost of a substitute purchase elsewhere), anchored in the same Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB, but the payment replaces the whole performance rather than closing a value gap.
Contrast example: a EUR 10,000 machine worth EUR 7,000 defective — small damages claims the EUR 3,000 gap while keeping the machine; big damages returns the machine and claims the EUR 2,000 extra cost of a EUR 12,000 replacement bought elsewhere.
Exam trap: both look similar to reduction/revocation because the buyer ends up with money either way, but damages require seller responsibility while reduction and revocation do not.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 4 (Case)

**Facts:** F sells industrial lubricant to a machine shop M for EUR 500. Due to a mixing error at F's warehouse, the lubricant delivered is corrosive rather than the ordinary type M ordered. F did not notice the mix-up. M's technician uses it in a precision lathe; the lathe's bearings are destroyed, costing EUR 4,000 to repair. M wants F to pay both the EUR 4,000 repair cost and refund the EUR 500 paid for the unusable lubricant. M has not yet asked F for a replacement delivery of correct lubricant.

**Question:** Does M have a claim against F for (a) the EUR 4,000 lathe repair and (b) the EUR 500 lubricant price, and under what legal bases?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: M could have a claim against F for damages under Section 437 No. 3 in conjunction with Section 280 I BGB for the lathe repair, and separately under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB for the lubricant price, if the requirements of these provisions are met.
II. Requirements:
   I. Valid purchase contract (Section 433 BGB).
   II. Defect at transfer of risk (Section 434, 446 BGB).
   III. For the lathe repair: obligation, breach, responsibility, causal damage (Section 280 I elements) — damages in addition.
   IV. For the lubricant price: the same elements plus, for Section 281, a reasonable additional period that expired without result, unless dispensable.
III. Application:
   I. Purchase contract: M and F concluded a purchase contract for lubricant under Section 433 BGB when M ordered and F agreed to supply it for EUR 500.
   II. Defect: Questionable is whether the delivered lubricant was defective. This is supported by the fact that it was corrosive rather than the ordinary type agreed, so it lacked the agreed quality under Section 434 II 1 No. 1 BGB. Consequently, the lubricant was defective at transfer of risk (Section 446 BGB).
   III. Lathe repair — damages in addition: Questionable is whether the EUR 4,000 repair is damages in addition to performance. This is supported by the hypothetical-performance test: even if F delivered conforming lubricant now, the lathe damage would remain. Against this, one could argue the damage flows directly from the defect and should follow the defect's own route. However, consequential damage to other property caused by a defective item is not cured by later proper performance. Responsibility is presumed under Section 280 I 2 BGB and not rebutted, since F's own mixing error caused the wrong delivery. Consequently, the lathe repair is damages in addition to performance under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I BGB.
   IV. Lubricant price — damages instead: Questionable is whether the EUR 500 lubricant price is damages instead of performance. This is supported by the fact that conforming lubricant could still cure the defect entirely — the defect concerns the lubricant itself, not other property. However, Section 281 additionally requires a reasonable additional period for cure that expired without result, unless dispensable under Section 281 II or Section 440 BGB. The facts show M never demanded cure and F never refused it. Consequently, this requirement is not met on the facts as given.
IV. Interim results: the lathe-repair claim is established (damages in addition); the lubricant-price claim is not yet established because the additional-period requirement under Section 281 has not been satisfied or dispensed with.
V. Final answer: As a result, M can currently claim the EUR 4,000 lathe repair from F as damages in addition to performance under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I BGB, but cannot yet claim the EUR 500 lubricant price as damages instead of performance unless M first sets F a reasonable additional period for cure (or such a period is dispensable).

### Question 5 (Case)

**Facts:** F sold a used delivery van to bakery B for EUR 12,000. The transmission was defective at handover; a defect-free equivalent van would be worth EUR 12,000, while the van's actual value with the defect is EUR 9,000. B keeps using the van as-is and writes to F: "I am not returning the van, and I want you to compensate me because I effectively overpaid due to the transmission problem — I hold you responsible for not disclosing this."

**Question:** Should B's claim be routed as small damages or as reduction, and what does each route require B to show?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: B's claim could be small damages under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB or reduction under Sections 437 No. 2, 441 BGB, depending on how B's demand is properly classified.
II. Requirements: small damages requires seller responsibility (Section 280 I 2 BGB, presumed but rebuttable) in addition to the warranty gateway; reduction requires no fault, only the warranty gateway and a reduction declaration under Section 441 I BGB.
III. Application: Questionable is whether B's letter is a demand for compensation (damages) or a price adjustment (reduction). This is supported by B's own wording — B explicitly "holds F responsible," asserting fault-based compensation for a loss, not merely adjusting the price to the lower value. Against this, one could argue the economic outcome (keeping the van, receiving EUR 3,000) is identical either way, so the label should not matter. However, the two routes carry different legal requirements: small damages presupposes F's responsibility, which must be checked and could be rebutted if F neither knew nor should have known of the defect, while reduction requires none. Consequently, because B's letter asserts fault, this is small damages, not reduction, and the responsibility requirement must be affirmatively established.
IV. Interim result: the requirements for small damages are met only if F's responsibility for the defect is shown or not rebutted; reduction would apply regardless of fault and yields the same EUR 3,000 figure through the proportional formula.
V. Final answer: As a result, B's demand as framed is small damages instead of performance under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB for EUR 3,000, conditional on F's responsibility being established or not rebutted; if F disproves responsibility, B would need to fall back on reduction under Sections 437 No. 2, 441 BGB for the same amount, without needing to prove fault.

### Question 6 (Case — integrates Week 09 + Week 04)

**Facts:** P buys a vintage racing bicycle advertised by seller Q as "an original 1985 frame, hand-built by the famous frame-builder Enzo Colnago," paying EUR 8,000. After delivery, P has the bicycle authenticated and learns the frame is a well-made modern replica worth about EUR 1,200. Q genuinely believed the frame was authentic when selling it, relying on paperwork that later turns out to have been forged by an earlier owner in the chain of title. P wants to undo the deal and recover the EUR 8,000.

**Question:** Should P's claim be pursued as rescission for an essential-characteristic mistake under Section 119 II BGB, or through the warranty route? Advise P.

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: P's claim could be pursued through rescission for an essential-characteristic mistake (Section 119 II BGB) or through the warranty gateway (Section 437 BGB), with revocation under Sections 437 No. 2, 323, 346 BGB as the likely remedy if warranty law applies.
II. Requirements: (I) determine whether the frame's inauthenticity is a formation-stage mistake (119 II) or a transfer-stage material defect (434 II); (II) purchase contract; (III) defect and transfer of risk; (IV) revocation requirements, including Nachfrist and its exclusions; (V) legal consequence.
III. Application: Questionable is whether Q's own mistaken belief about the frame's origin allows reliance on Section 119 II BGB. This is supported by the fact that authorship/origin of a collectible item is exactly the kind of "essential characteristic" Section 119 II covers in the abstract. Against this, one could argue that Section 119 II rescission is a defense available to the party who made the mistaken declaration — here Q, the seller — and does not assist P as buyer; moreover, once a purchased item is delivered and found not to match its agreed quality, that is precisely what warranty law under Section 434 BGB is designed to handle, not a mistake analysis on the buyer's side. However, applying rescission analysis to a delivered good that turns out defective would bypass the tiered warranty-remedy system (cure first) that Section 437 specifically builds in. Consequently, P's route is warranty law, not rescission: the frame's actual authorship differs from the agreed quality (an authentic 1985 hand-built Colnago frame), so it lacks the agreed quality under Section 434 II 1 No. 1 BGB, and the defect existed at transfer of risk (Section 446 BGB) since the frame was never actually authentic. Revocation ordinarily requires a Nachfrist for cure, but cure — turning a replica into an authentic original — is objectively impossible, so the additional period is dispensable. No fault/responsibility is required for revocation under Sections 437 No. 2, 323, 346 BGB.
IV. Interim result: P cannot pursue Section 119 II rescission as buyer; P's route is the Section 437 warranty gateway, and within it, revocation is available without needing to show Q's fault, because cure is objectively impossible.
V. Final answer: As a result, P should be advised to pursue revocation of the purchase contract under Sections 437 No. 2, 323, 346 BGB (Nachfrist dispensable due to impossibility of cure), not rescission under Section 119 II BGB; upon a valid revocation declaration (Section 349 BGB), P returns the bicycle and recovers the EUR 8,000 purchase price under Sections 346 ff. BGB.

### Question 7 (Case)

**Facts:** Interior fit-out company I delivers custom-built cabinetry to office tenant O for EUR 15,000. Several drawer fronts are warped due to a manufacturing defect. O immediately emails I: "This is unacceptable, I don't want you anywhere near my office again — I am buying replacement cabinetry from someone else and will bill you the difference." I is never given a chance to inspect or cure. Two weeks later, O buys replacement cabinetry from another supplier for EUR 17,000 and demands the EUR 2,000 difference from I as damages.

**Question:** Can O successfully claim the EUR 2,000 as big damages instead of performance without first setting I an additional period for cure?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: O could have a claim against I for EUR 2,000 in big damages instead of performance under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB if the requirements, including the additional-period rule, are met.
II. Requirements: (I) valid supply contract, (II) defect at transfer of risk, (III) reasonable additional period for cure that expired without result, or a dispensing exception, (IV) responsibility, (V) causal damage in the amount claimed.
III. Application: Questionable is whether O needed to set I a Nachfrist before buying elsewhere. This is supported by the general rule that damages instead of performance under Section 281 requires exactly that — a reasonable additional period must expire unsuccessfully first, preserving the debtor's chance to cure. Against this, one could argue O's email was so final in tone that it functions as an implicit refusal of cure. However, the dispensability exceptions under Section 281 II BGB require a serious and definitive refusal by the debtor — here it is O, not I, who unilaterally shuts out the opportunity for cure; I never refused. Consequently, O's own refusal to allow cure does not satisfy the exception.
IV. Interim result: the additional-period requirement under Section 281 is not met and not validly dispensed with.
V. Final answer: As a result, O cannot currently claim the EUR 2,000 as damages instead of performance, because O never gave I a reasonable additional period to cure and no recognized exception applies; O would first need to have set (or been excused from setting) such a period.

### Question 8 (Case)

**Facts:** Homeowner H hires contractor K to design and build a custom walk-in wine cellar in H's basement, including all materials and labor, for a lump sum of EUR 40,000. After H formally accepts the finished cellar, H notices the climate-control unit K installed does not maintain the agreed humidity range.

**Question:** Which warranty regime governs H's claim, and what remedies — including any not found in ordinary sale-of-goods warranty law — are available to H?

**Model Answer:**
I. Claim/issue sentence: H could have a claim against K for cure, self-help, reduction, revocation, or damages under the works-contract warranty regime (Section 634 BGB and its referrals) if the requirements are met — not under the ordinary purchase warranty gateway (Section 437 BGB).
II. Requirements: (I) classify the contract type, (II) confirm acceptance of the work (Section 640 BGB) as the relevant timing point, (III) confirm a defect under Section 633 BGB, (IV) select the appropriate Section 634 remedy and its own sub-requirements.
III. Application: Questionable is whether the cellar project is a purchase contract or a works contract. This is supported by the fact that K did not merely deliver a pre-made item but designed and built a bespoke result — a custom wine cellar meeting a specific agreed outcome (the humidity range) — which is the hallmark of a works contract under Section 631 BGB, not a sale under Section 433 BGB. Against this, one could argue materials were also supplied, which sounds sale-like. However, where labor and a specific promised result dominate the transaction, works-contract law under Section 631 BGB and its warranty referral in Section 634 BGB applies. Consequently, this is a works contract. The decisive timing point is acceptance of the work under Section 640 BGB, already satisfied here, and the humidity-control failure means the work lacks the agreed quality under Section 633 BGB. Available remedies under Section 634 BGB are: cure (Section 634 No. 1, 635 BGB, with the contractor — not the customer — choosing the type of cure, unlike purchase law); self-help (Section 634 No. 2, 637 BGB, letting H fix it and bill K, a remedy purchase law does not have); revocation (Section 634 No. 3 with Section 323 BGB); reduction (Section 634 No. 3 with Section 638 BGB); and damages or reimbursement (Section 634 No. 4 with Sections 280 ff. or 284 BGB), following the same damages logic as this week's purchase-law analysis.
IV. Interim result: the works-contract warranty regime applies, and H has the full remedy menu under Section 634 BGB, including self-help, which purchase law does not offer.
V. Final answer: As a result, H should pursue a claim under the works-contract warranty regime (Section 634 BGB), most naturally starting with a demand for cure under Sections 634 No. 1, 635 BGB (with K choosing the method), and escalating to self-help, reduction, revocation, or damages if cure fails.

## Quick Answer Route Summary

| Question # | Primary Route/Section | One-line takeaway |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Section 284 BGB | Futile-expense reimbursement is either/or with damages instead of performance. |
| 2 | Sections 280 I vs. 281/283/311a BGB | Hypothetical-performance test decides in addition vs. instead. |
| 3 | Sections 280 I, III, 281 BGB | Small vs. big damages both need seller responsibility; the difference is keep-item vs. give-up-item. |
| 4 | Sections 280 I; 280 I, III, 281 BGB | One defective delivery can split into a damages-in-addition claim and a not-yet-ripe damages-instead claim. |
| 5 | Sections 280 I, III, 281 vs. 441 BGB | Fault-based wording ("I hold you responsible") signals small damages, not reduction. |
| 6 | Section 437 gateway vs. Section 119 II BGB | Buyer cannot use seller's own mistake defensively; defect goes through warranty law, with revocation via impossible-cure dispensability. |
| 7 | Section 281 BGB | Buyer's own refusal to allow cure does not create a 281 II dispensability exception. |
| 8 | Section 634 BGB | Works contracts run a parallel remedy system, including self-help, absent from purchase warranty law. |
