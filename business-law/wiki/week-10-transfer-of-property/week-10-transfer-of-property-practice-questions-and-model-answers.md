## Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 1 (Compare)

Distinguish ownership (Eigentum) from possession (Besitz).

**Model Answer:**

Ownership is the strongest legal right over a thing — the power to use, enjoy, and exclude others, protected under Article 14 GG and transferred through the disposition rules (Section 929 ff. BGB). Possession is actual physical control over a thing plus the will to possess it, governed by Sections 854 ff. BGB, and can exist entirely independent of any right to the thing.

Ownership changes through a disposition transaction: agreement, delivery (or a replacement), and authorization under Section 929, good-faith acquisition, or a statutory acquisition mode. Possession changes simply by gaining or losing actual physical control (Section 854), regardless of any underlying legal right.

Consequence: ownership determines who may ultimately dispose of or reclaim the thing (e.g. vindication under Section 985 BGB). Possession only determines factual control, but it triggers the legal presumption under Section 1006 BGB that the possessor is the owner, and supports possession-based remedies against interference (Sections 861 ff. BGB).

Contrast example: a thief who steals a watch has possession of it but never becomes its owner — the original owner remains owner despite losing possession.

Exam warning: never write "the possessor owns the thing" as a legal conclusion. Possession only creates a rebuttable legal appearance under Section 1006 BGB, not ownership itself.

### Question 2 (Compare)

Distinguish the principle of separation (Trennungsprinzip) from the principle of abstraction (Abstraktionsprinzip).

**Model Answer:**

The principle of separation treats the obligation contract (e.g. a purchase agreement under Section 433 BGB) and the disposition contract (the actual transfer of ownership) as two legally distinct transactions, even though they usually happen together in everyday perception. The principle of abstraction builds on separation by treating the validity of the obligation contract and the validity of the disposition contract as independent of each other.

Separation applies to every sale by structure — it triggers whenever a contract creates a duty to transfer a right. Abstraction is specifically triggered once one of the two transactions turns out to be defective (void, rescinded) and the question becomes whether that defect drags the other transaction down with it.

Consequence: separation means signing a purchase contract alone never transfers ownership — delivery/disposition is still required. Abstraction means an invalid purchase contract does not automatically undo an already-completed ownership transfer (and vice versa); unwinding instead runs through restitution, typically unjust enrichment under Section 812 I sentence 1 alternative 1 BGB, or vindication under Section 985 BGB if ownership never passed at all.

Contrast example: if a Section 119 I mistake affects only the purchase price term, rescinding the purchase contract does not by itself undo an already-completed delivery, because the separate transfer agreement was untainted by that particular mistake.

Exam warning: watch for "identity of the flaw" cases — incapacity under Sections 104 No. 2, 105 I BGB, or deceit/duress under Section 123 I BGB, can taint both transactions identically. Abstraction does not save the disposition contract there.

### Question 3 (List Requirements)

What are the requirements for transferring ownership of a movable thing under Section 929 sentence 1 BGB?

**Model Answer:**

The requirements are:
1. Agreement between transferor and acquirer that ownership shall pass, satisfying the principle of determination (an objective third party must be able to identify the concrete object).
2. Delivery — the acquirer obtains possession through the transferor, and the transferor loses possession (or one of the delivery replacements under Section 929 sentence 2, or Sections 930/931 applies instead).
3. The agreement must still exist at the moment of delivery — it is freely revocable at the property-law level until then.
4. The transferor is the owner, or otherwise authorized to dispose of the thing (e.g. by the owner's consent under Section 185 I BGB, or a statutory power of disposition).

If these are met, the consequence is that the acquirer becomes owner of the movable thing. The rule exists to give legal certainty to everyday exchanges of goods: ownership passes at a clearly identifiable moment (agreement plus a real, publicly visible act), rather than by contract alone, which third parties cannot observe.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 4 (Case)

**Facts:** S owns a delivery van used in his catering business. S agrees to sell the van to B for EUR 8,000. On the agreed date, S and B meet at S's premises; S hands the van's keys and registration documents to B and both confirm "the van is now yours." B drives the van away.

**Question:** Did B become the owner of the van?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Claim/issue sentence.** B became owner of the van if the requirements of Section 929 sentence 1 BGB are met.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. Agreement that ownership shall pass.
   II. Delivery.
   III. Agreement still exists at delivery.
   IV. S is authorized (owner) to dispose of the van.

III.–IV. **Application and interim results.**
   - Agreement: S and B expressly confirmed "the van is now yours" — a clear agreement that ownership should pass, and the van is sufficiently determined as the specific object of the sale. Met.
   - Delivery: S handed over the keys and documents, and B drove the van away, so B obtained possession through S and S lost possession. Met.
   - Agreement at delivery: nothing in the facts suggests the agreement was revoked before the handover; it persisted through the moment of delivery. Met.
   - Authorization: S owned the van (it was used in his own business, with no facts suggesting otherwise). Met.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, B became owner of the van under Section 929 sentence 1 BGB.

### Question 5 (Case)

**Facts:** S runs a print shop and owns an industrial printing press worth EUR 40,000. S needs a EUR 15,000 loan from L. S and L agree that S transfers ownership of the press to L as security for the loan, while S keeps the press in the shop and continues printing with it under a security-purpose agreement (Sicherungsübereignung).

**Question:** Does L become owner of the press despite never physically receiving it?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Claim/issue sentence.** L became owner of the press if the requirements of Section 929 sentence 1 BGB, with delivery replaced under Section 930 BGB, are met.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. Agreement that ownership shall pass to L.
   II. A possession relationship (Besitzmittlungsverhältnis) substituting for physical delivery, under Section 930 BGB.
   III. Agreement still exists at the relevant moment.
   IV. S is authorized (owner) to dispose of the press.

III.–IV. **Application and interim results.**
   - Agreement: S and L agreed ownership of the press should pass to L as collateral. Met.
   - Delivery replacement: physical handover did not occur, and would defeat the purpose of the security arrangement (S needs to keep using the press). Instead, the security-purpose agreement establishes that S continues to possess the press on L's behalf — a possession relationship under Section 930 BGB, which substitutes for delivery. Met.
   - Agreement at delivery: the security agreement remained in force at the moment the constructive-delivery relationship was established. Met.
   - Authorization: S owned the press. Met.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, L became owner of the press under Sections 929 sentence 1, 930 BGB, even though S retains physical possession — S has possession, L has ownership.

### Question 6 (Case)

**Facts:** After the security transfer to L (Question 5's facts), S — still physically holding the press — sells the same press to B, who has no reason to know that L owns it. S hands the press to B, who takes it to his own workshop.

**Question:** Does B acquire ownership of the press?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Claim/issue sentence.** B became owner of the press if the requirements of Section 929 sentence 1 BGB are met, or, failing authorization, if B acquired ownership in good faith under Sections 929 sentence 1, 932 BGB.

II. **Requirements (ordinary transfer).**
   I. Agreement that ownership shall pass to B.
   II. Delivery.
   III. Agreement still exists at delivery.
   IV. S is authorized (owner) to dispose of the press.

III.–IV. **Application and interim results (ordinary transfer).**
   - Agreement, delivery, and agreement at delivery are all satisfied on the facts.
   - Authorization: S is not authorized — L became owner in Question 5, and L never consented to this resale. Not met.

Because ordinary transfer fails on authorization, the analysis continues into good-faith acquisition.

II (continued). **Requirements (good-faith acquisition).**
   V. All Section 929 sentence 1 elements except authorization (already established above).
   VI. B's good faith — B neither knew nor was grossly negligently unaware that S was not the owner.
   VII. Legal appearance from S's possession, Section 1006 BGB.
   VIII. The press was not lost or stolen from L, Section 935 BGB.
   IX. A transaction between two distinct parties (S and B).

III.–IV (continued). **Application and interim results (good-faith acquisition).**
   - Good faith: the facts state B had no reason to know of L's ownership. Met.
   - Legal appearance: S's continued possession of the press created the appearance that S was still owner. Met.
   - Section 935 block: L voluntarily left the press with S as part of the security arrangement — L did not lose possession involuntarily, so Section 935 does not apply. Met (no block).
   - Distinct parties: S and B are different legal persons. Met.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, B acquires ownership of the press in good faith under Sections 929 sentence 1, 932 BGB, and L loses ownership. L's remedy lies against S (e.g. breach of the security agreement), not against B.

### Question 7 (Case)

**Facts:** Thief T steals O's antique watch from O's apartment. T sells the watch to B, an antique dealer who has no reason to suspect it was stolen and pays a fair market price. T hands the watch to B.

**Question:** Does B acquire ownership of the watch?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Claim/issue sentence.** B became owner of the watch if the requirements of Section 929 sentence 1 BGB are met, or, failing authorization, if B acquired ownership in good faith under Sections 929 sentence 1, 932 BGB — unless the Section 935 BGB block applies.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. Agreement, delivery, and agreement at delivery between T and B.
   II. Authorization — T is not the owner and has no consent from O, so this fails, requiring the good-faith route.
   III. B's good faith.
   IV. Legal appearance from T's possession, Section 1006 BGB.
   V. The watch was not lost or stolen, Section 935 BGB.

III.–IV. **Application and interim results.**
   - Agreement, delivery, and agreement at delivery: satisfied on the facts between T and B.
   - Authorization: T is not the owner and O never consented to any transfer. Not met.
   - Good faith: the facts state B had no reason to suspect the watch was stolen. Met, considered alone.
   - Legal appearance: T's possession of the watch created the appearance of ownership. Met, considered alone.
   - Section 935 block: O lost the watch through theft — an involuntary loss of possession. This blocks good-faith acquisition regardless of how honest B was.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, B does not acquire ownership of the watch. O remains the owner, and Section 935 BGB protects that ownership precisely because O lost possession involuntarily.

### Question 8 (Case — integrates Week 10 + Week 08)

**Facts:** S, a machine dealer, sells an industrial mixer to B for EUR 20,000. Instead of B collecting the mixer, S arranges for a haulage company of S's own choosing to transport the mixer to B's factory, at S's cost. While the haulage company's truck is en route, it is involved in an accident and the mixer is destroyed beyond repair. B has never taken possession of the mixer and has not paid the price yet.

**Question:** (a) Did B ever become owner of the mixer, and (b) must B still pay the purchase price?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Claim/issue sentence.** Two separate questions arise: (a) whether ownership of the mixer passed to B under Section 929 sentence 1 BGB, and (b) whether the risk of accidental loss passed to B under Section 446 BGB, which determines whether B's payment duty survives the mixer's destruction.

II. **Requirements — ownership (Section 929 sentence 1 BGB).**
   I. Agreement that ownership shall pass.
   II. Delivery — B obtains possession through S, and S loses possession.
   III. Agreement still exists at delivery.
   IV. S is authorized (owner).

II (continued). **Requirements — transfer of risk (Section 446 BGB).**
   V. Handover (Übergabe) of the item to the buyer.

III.–IV. **Application and interim results.**
   - Agreement: S and B agreed ownership should pass under the sale. Met.
   - Delivery: delivery under Section 929 requires that the *acquirer* obtain possession through the transferor. Here, S chose and instructed the haulage company itself — the carrier is S's own transport agent, not B's. B has not yet obtained possession; S has not yet lost possession to B, only handed the mixer to a third party still acting for S. Delivery to B has therefore not occurred. Not met.
   - Because delivery to B has not occurred, ownership has not passed — the analysis can stop here for part (a).
   - Transfer of risk under Section 446: risk passes upon handover of the item *to the buyer*. Handing the mixer to a carrier chosen by the seller, for transport to the buyer, is not equivalent to handover to the buyer. Section 446's handover requirement is therefore not met either.
   - Consequence of risk staying with S: since S still bore the risk of accidental loss when the mixer was destroyed in transit, S's duty to deliver became impossible under Section 275 BGB, and B's counter-performance duty (the payment obligation) lapses under Section 326 I BGB.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, (a) B never became owner of the mixer, because delivery to B never occurred; and (b) B does not have to pay the purchase price, because risk never passed to B under Section 446 BGB and S's own performance became impossible. The case shows that transfer of risk and transfer of ownership are two independently tested questions that happen to coincide at "handover" in the ordinary case, but can come apart on facts like these.

### Question 9 (List Requirements)

What are the requirements for a good-faith acquisition of a movable from a non-owner under Sections 929 sentence 1, 932 BGB, and what statutory limit can block it?

**Model Answer:**

The requirements are:
1. All Section 929 sentence 1 elements except authorization: agreement that ownership shall pass, delivery (or a delivery replacement), and the agreement still existing at that moment.
2. Good faith of the acquirer — defined negatively: the acquirer is not in good faith if aware, or grossly negligently unaware, that the transferor is not the owner.
3. A legal appearance created by the transferor's possession, Section 1006 BGB.
4. The thing was not lost or stolen, and the true owner did not otherwise involuntarily lose possession, Section 935 BGB.
5. A legal transaction between two distinct parties.

If these are met, the consequence is that the acquirer becomes owner even though the transferor was not authorized to dispose of the thing, and the true owner loses ownership. The statutory limit is Section 935 BGB: if the true owner lost possession involuntarily (theft, loss), no good-faith acquisition is possible no matter how honest the acquirer was — voluntary loss of possession (e.g. leaving a thing with someone as collateral) does not trigger this block.

### Question 10 (Compare)

Compare the three delivery replacements under Section 929 sentence 2, Section 930, and Section 931 BGB.

**Model Answer:**

Section 929 sentence 2 BGB dispenses with delivery entirely because the acquirer already possesses the thing — nothing needs to change hands, so a bare agreement that ownership shall pass suffices (e.g. a borrower who already holds the item being told "it is yours now"). Section 930 BGB replaces delivery with a possession relationship (Besitzmittlungsverhältnis): the transferor keeps physical possession but now holds the thing *for* the acquirer under some legal relationship, typically a security-purpose agreement in chattel-mortgage facts. Section 931 BGB replaces delivery with assignment of the transferor's claim to possession against a third party who currently holds the thing, used when neither transferor nor acquirer physically has the object.

Contrast example: a chattel mortgage where the borrower keeps using the machine runs through Section 930 (constructive delivery), while a machine stored with an independent third-party warehouseman runs through Section 931 (assignment of the claim for return of the machine against the warehouseman) if the transferor never regains direct possession.

Exam trap: do not default to Section 930 for every "no physical handover" fact — check first whether the acquirer already possesses the thing (929 II) or whether a third party, not the transferor, holds it (931), since each replacement fits a different possession structure.

### Question 11 (Compare)

Compare how ownership of a movable, a piece of land, and a claim is each transferred.

**Model Answer:**

A movable transfers under Section 929 sentence 1 BGB: agreement, delivery (or a replacement), agreement still existing at that moment, and authorization. Land transfers under a stricter regime: the underlying purchase contract requires notarial form under Section 311b I BGB, and ownership itself passes only through conveyance (Auflassung) plus entry in the land register, Sections 873 and 925 BGB — a real act of handover plays no role at all. A claim is not a corporeal thing under Section 90 BGB, so it is never transferred by delivery; instead it passes by assignment under Section 398 BGB, requiring only a disposition agreement between assignor and assignee, with no debtor participation needed.

Contrast example: handing over a laptop completes an ownership transfer once the four Section 929 elements are met; handing over the keys to a house does nothing for ownership of the house without a notarized conveyance and land-register entry; and a claim against a debtor changes creditors purely by agreement between old and new creditor, without any physical or registry act at all.

Exam trap: do not apply Section 929's delivery concept to land (land-register entry is the operative "publicity act," not handover) or to claims (there is no delivery concept for an intangible right at all).

### Question 12

Explain what "authorization" means in the Section 929 sentence 1 BGB test, and name two ways a non-owner can still be authorized to transfer ownership.

**Model Answer:**

1. Authorization is the fourth cumulative element of Section 929 sentence 1 BGB: in principle, only the owner of a thing can validly transfer ownership of it.
2. No single section stands alone here — authorization can flow from the owner's own status, from the owner's consent, or from a statutory power of disposition.
3. Requirements/features: (1) ordinarily, the transferor is authorized simply because the transferor is the owner; (2) a non-owner can still be authorized if the true owner consented to the disposition, Section 185 I BGB; (3) a non-owner can also be authorized by a statutory power of disposition, such as an insolvency administrator's power under Section 80 I InsO once insolvency proceedings restrict the debtor's own disposition power under Section 81 InsO.
4. Example: an auction house selling a client's painting with the client's advance consent transfers ownership validly under Section 185 I BGB even though the auction house itself is not the owner.
5. Trap: do not treat every non-owner transferor as automatically routed into good-faith acquisition — first check whether the non-owner was nonetheless *authorized* (consent or statutory power); good-faith acquisition under Section 932 is only reached once authorization genuinely fails.

### Question 13

What is the "principle of determination" in the transfer agreement under Section 929 sentence 1 BGB, and why does it matter?

**Model Answer:**

1. The principle of determination is a requirement built into the agreement element of the Section 929 sentence 1 test.
2. It has no separate statutory section of its own — it is a doctrinal gloss on what counts as a valid agreement that ownership shall pass.
3. Requirements/features: (1) the transfer agreement must identify the object with enough precision that an objective third party, not just the parties themselves, can tell exactly which object is meant; (2) this can be satisfied by pointing to a specific item, naming it precisely (e.g. "machine #1"), or describing a class precisely enough to fix its members (e.g. "all items manufactured in January 2023"); (3) if the object cannot be pinpointed this way, the agreement element of Section 929 sentence 1 fails regardless of the parties' subjective intent.
4. Example: "I'll sell you one of my three delivery vans" fails determination until a specific van is identified; "I'll sell you the blue delivery van parked outside" satisfies it.
5. Trap: do not confuse the principle of determination (an objective-identifiability requirement for the disposition agreement) with the essentialia negotii requirement for a valid offer under Section 145 BGB from Contract Law — they operate at different layers (disposition vs. obligation) even though both police how definite an agreement must be.

### Question 14 (Case)

**Facts:** S orally agrees to sell his house to B for EUR 300,000. They shake hands on the deal, and B moves in the following week without paying anything yet. No notarial deed is ever executed, and the land register still lists S as the owner.

**Question:** Is B the owner of the house?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Claim/issue sentence.** B became owner of the house only if the requirements for transfer of immovable property under Sections 873, 925 BGB are met, and only a validly formed purchase contract could have obliged that transfer in the first place.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. A valid purchase contract in the required notarial form, Section 311b I BGB.
   II. Conveyance (Auflassung), Section 925 BGB.
   III. Entry in the land register, Section 873 BGB.

III.–IV. **Application and interim results.**
   - Purchase contract: a real-property purchase contract requires notarial form under Section 311b I BGB. S and B only shook hands on an oral agreement — no notary was involved. The purchase contract is void for want of form. Not met.
   - Conveyance: no facts indicate any conveyance was declared before a notary or other competent authority. Not met.
   - Land register entry: the register still lists S as owner; no entry changing this has occurred. Not met.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, B is not the owner of the house. None of the requirements for transferring immovable property are met — moving in and an informal handshake have no effect on ownership of land, which passes only through notarized conveyance plus land-register entry, regardless of how the movable-property Section 929 route might have worked for a different kind of object.

### Question 15 (Case)

**Facts:** Manufacturer M sells goods to Retailer R on credit, creating a EUR 50,000 claim against R for the price. M is short on cash and enters into a factoring agreement with Bank F, under which M and F agree that the claim against R is transferred to F in exchange for immediate payment of EUR 47,000 to M.

**Question:** Did F become the creditor entitled to collect the EUR 50,000 from R?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Claim/issue sentence.** F became the new creditor of the claim if the requirements for assignment under Section 398 BGB are met.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. A disposition contract between assignor (M) and assignee (F), Section 398 sentence 1 BGB.
   II. The claim exists.
   III. The claim is identifiable.
   IV. The claim is transferable (not excluded by statute or contract).

III.–IV. **Application and interim results.**
   - Disposition contract: M and F agreed that the claim against R is transferred to F as part of the factoring agreement. Met.
   - Existence: R owes M EUR 50,000 for goods already sold on credit — the claim exists. Met.
   - Identifiability: the claim is specifically described as the EUR 50,000 price claim against R from this sale. Met.
   - Transferability: nothing in the facts indicates a statutory or contractual exclusion of assignment. Met.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, F becomes the new creditor of the EUR 50,000 claim against R under Section 398 BGB. No participation or consent from R, the debtor, was required for the assignment to be effective between M and F.

### Question 16 (Case)

**Facts:** Owner O leaves a stack of reclaimed oak planks with craftsman C for temporary storage in C's workshop. Without asking O, C cuts, joins, and finishes the planks into a custom dining table — a new, distinct, and substantially more valuable object than the raw planks.

**Question:** Who owns the dining table?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Claim/issue sentence.** Ownership of the dining table depends on the statutory acquisition-by-processing rule, Section 950 BGB, rather than on the ordinary Section 929 sentence 1 transfer test, since there was no agreement between O and C to transfer ownership of the planks at all.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. A new, distinct thing is manufactured through processing or transformation.
   II. The processing is carried out by the person claiming acquisition under Section 950 BGB.

III.–IV. **Application and interim results.**
   - New thing: cutting, joining, and finishing raw planks into a finished dining table produces an object that is distinct in form and substantially more valuable than the input planks. Met.
   - Processing by the claimant: C is the one who carried out the processing. Met.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, C acquires ownership of the dining table under Section 950 BGB, as a statutory acquisition mode operating independently of any transfer agreement with O and independently of O's consent to the processing. This is not a Section 929 case at all — no one agreed to transfer ownership of the planks, so the ordinary transfer test is the wrong route; O's lack of consent to the processing is a separate matter outside this transfer-of-property analysis and does not by itself give O ownership of the finished table.

### Question 17 (Case — integrates Week 10 + Week 06)

**Facts:** Wholesaler S regularly supplies retailer B under S's own pre-printed order-confirmation form, used in every delivery for years. Printed on the reverse of the form, referenced by a note on the front, is the clause: "Ownership of the delivered goods passes to the buyer only once the full purchase price has been paid in full." On the latest delivery, S hands over a shipment of goods to B; B's warehouse manager signs the delivery note without reading the reverse side. B has not yet paid.

**Question:** (a) Is the retention-of-title clause validly incorporated as a standard business term, and (b) has ownership of the goods passed to B?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Claim/issue sentence.** Two questions arise: (a) whether the retention-of-title clause was validly incorporated under Section 305 II BGB, and (b) whether that answer changes the outcome of the ordinary Section 929 sentence 1 BGB transfer test — specifically, whether the transfer agreement was conditional under Section 158 I BGB.

II. **Requirements — incorporation (Section 305 II BGB).**
   I. The clause is SBT: a contract term, pre-formulated, intended for repeated use, presented unilaterally by S, Section 305 I BGB.
   II. S pointed to the clause.
   III. B had a reasonable opportunity to review it.
   IV. B agreed.

II (continued). **Requirements — transfer of ownership (Section 929 sentence 1 BGB, conditional under Section 158 I BGB).**
   V. Agreement that ownership shall pass — here, conditionally on full payment.
   VI. Delivery.
   VII. Agreement still exists at delivery.
   VIII. S is authorized (owner).

III.–IV. **Application and interim results.**
   - SBT existence: the form has been used in every delivery for years and was drafted unilaterally by S — pre-formulated, repeated use, presented by the user. Met.
   - Pointing to the clause: the front of the form references the clause on the reverse. Met.
   - Reasonable opportunity to review: the clause is legibly printed and referenced; actual reading is not required for incorporation, only a reasonable chance to take notice. Met.
   - Agreement: B's warehouse manager signed the delivery note, the standard channel through which B accepts S's terms in this ongoing course of dealing. Met.
   - Incorporation interim result: the retention-of-title clause is validly incorporated under Section 305 II BGB.
   - Transfer agreement: because the incorporated clause makes ownership conditional on full payment, the disposition agreement itself is a conditional agreement under Section 158 I BGB — ownership shall pass only once the condition (full payment) occurs.
   - Delivery: S handed the goods to B. Met.
   - Agreement at delivery: the conditional agreement was in force at delivery. Met.
   - Authorization: S owned the goods, but the condition attached to the agreement element is questionable-whether satisfied — supported by the fact that B has not yet paid the price in full; against this, one might argue delivery alone should suffice as in an ordinary sale; however, this fails because the parties' own validly incorporated agreement made passage of ownership conditional, and Section 158 I BGB gives full effect to that condition until it occurs; consequently, ownership has not yet passed.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, (a) the retention-of-title clause is validly incorporated as an SBT under Section 305 II BGB, and (b) ownership of the goods has not passed to B — S remains owner under the conditional Section 929 sentence 1, 158 I BGB agreement until B pays the price in full.

### Question 18 (Case — integrates Week 10 + Week 03)

**Facts:** Antique dealer S and collector B are discussing the sale of a violin. B says, "I am seriously interested, but let's fix the final price once I've had it independently appraised next week — nothing is agreed yet." S, excited by B's interest, immediately says "take it home and try it out — it's yours now," and hands B the violin. B takes it home.

**Question:** Did B become the owner of the violin, even though no purchase contract was ever concluded?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Claim/issue sentence.** Two separate questions arise under the principle of separation: (a) whether a valid purchase (obligation) contract was concluded, and (b) whether ownership of the violin nonetheless passed under the separately tested disposition contract, Section 929 sentence 1 BGB — the abstraction principle meaning that a negative answer to (a) does not automatically answer (b).

II. **Requirements — purchase contract (Sections 145, 133, 157 BGB).**
   I. A definite offer containing the essentialia negotii, including price.

II (continued). **Requirements — disposition contract (Section 929 sentence 1 BGB).**
   II. Agreement that ownership shall pass, satisfying the principle of determination.
   III. Delivery.
   IV. Agreement still exists at delivery.
   V. S is authorized (owner).

III.–IV. **Application and interim results.**
   - Purchase contract: B's statement deliberately leaves the price open pending independent appraisal and says "nothing is agreed yet" — a letter-of-intent-style statement showing the parties intentionally left the deal unfinished, not a definite offer with essentialia negotii. No valid offer was made, so no purchase contract was concluded. Not met.
   - Because separation treats the obligation and disposition contracts as distinct transactions, the disposition contract must be tested independently rather than assumed to fail alongside the purchase contract.
   - Disposition agreement: questionable-whether S's "it's yours now" is itself a sufficient, independent agreement that ownership shall pass. This is supported by the fact that S's statement is unconditional, immediate, and refers to one specific, already-identified violin, satisfying the principle of determination; against this, one could argue that without a settled price there can be no valid transfer at all; however, this argument fails because the disposition agreement under Section 929 sentence 1 BGB does not require a price term, which belongs to the separate obligation contract; consequently, S's statement is a valid, independent agreement that ownership shall pass. Met.
   - Delivery: S handed the violin to B. Met.
   - Agreement at delivery: the agreement and the handover were contemporaneous. Met.
   - Authorization: S, an antique dealer, is not stated to be selling on anyone else's behalf, so S is treated as owner. Met.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, B becomes owner of the violin under Section 929 sentence 1 BGB, even though no valid purchase contract was ever concluded — the abstraction principle means the disposition contract's validity is tested on its own terms and does not collapse merely because the obligation contract never came into existence. S has no contractual price claim against B, since there is no valid sales contract, but this is a separate consequence at the obligation-contract level and does not undo B's ownership; any restitution S might seek would have to run through non-contractual routes such as unjust enrichment, not through reversing the property transfer itself.

### Question 19 (Case)

**Facts:** Owner S stores 50 barrels of wine with W, an independent bonded warehouse operator who holds the barrels purely as a professional storage-keeper — W is not S's employee and has no other relationship to S. S sells all 50 barrels to B. Since the barrels never leave W's warehouse, S and B agree that ownership shall pass to B, and S assigns to B S's claim against W for release of the barrels. W is later informed of the assignment.

**Question:** Did B become the owner of the wine barrels?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Claim/issue sentence.** B became owner of the barrels if the requirements of Section 929 sentence 1 BGB are met, with delivery replaced by assignment of the claim to possession under Section 931 BGB.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. Agreement that ownership shall pass.
   II. Assignment of the transferor's claim to possession against the third-party holder, in place of delivery, Section 931 BGB.
   III. Agreement still exists at the relevant moment.
   IV. S is authorized (owner) to dispose of the barrels.

III.–IV. **Application and interim results.**
   - Agreement: S and B agreed that ownership of the specifically identified barrels held at W's warehouse should pass — sufficiently determined. Met.
   - Delivery replacement: questionable is which of the three delivery replacements fits, since neither S nor B ever physically holds the barrels. This is supported by the fact that Section 930 requires the transferor itself to remain in a possession relationship with the acquirer after the transfer, which cannot fit here because S never possesses the barrels at all, only W does; against this, one might default to Section 930 simply because no physical handover occurred; however, this fails because Section 931 exists precisely for this configuration — a third party, not the transferor, holds the thing — and S's assignment of the claim against W for the barrels' release is exactly the claim-assignment mechanism Section 931 substitutes for delivery; consequently, Section 931, not Section 930, is the applicable replacement. Met.
   - Agreement at the relevant moment: nothing in the facts suggests revocation before the assignment took effect. Met.
   - Authorization: S owned the barrels before the sale. Met.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, B becomes owner of the wine barrels under Sections 929 sentence 1, 931 BGB. W's later notification is not itself a requirement of the property-law transfer between S and B — it matters practically (so W knows to release the barrels to B), but the disposition between S and B is already complete once the agreement and the assignment of the claim against W have occurred.

### Question 20 (Case)

**Facts:** S, a used-car dealer, deliberately lies to buyer B about a car's accident history to induce the sale — in truth the car was in a severe accident, but S tells B it has a clean history. Relying on this lie, B agrees to buy the car, and moments later, still relying on the same lie, B and S conclude the transfer agreement: S hands over the keys and documents, and B pays. Two months later, B discovers the truth and immediately declares rescission to S under Section 123 BGB.

**Question:** Does B's rescission undo only the purchase contract, leaving B as owner of the car, or does it also undo the ownership transfer?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Claim/issue sentence.** Whether B remains owner of the car depends on whether B's rescission for deceit reaches only the purchase contract or also the disposition contract, under the principle of abstraction and its "identity of the flaw" exception.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. A valid ground for rescission under Section 123 I BGB (fraudulent deceit) affecting the purchase contract.
   II. Rescission declared within the Section 124 BGB time limit.
   III. Whether the same deceit also affected the disposition contract (identity of the flaw).

III.–IV. **Application and interim results.**
   - Ground for rescission: S deliberately lied about the accident history to induce B to buy — fraudulent deceit under Section 123 I BGB affecting B's decision to enter the purchase contract. Met.
   - Timing: B discovered the deceit and rescinded immediately, well within the one-year period under Section 124 BGB. Met.
   - Identity of the flaw: questionable is whether the same deceit that tainted the purchase contract also tainted the disposition contract. This is supported by the fact that B relied on the identical lie moments later when agreeing to the transfer — the deceit did not target a narrow, severable term (like a price figure) but drove B's entire willingness to engage in the transaction, including handing over payment and accepting the car; against this, one could argue that abstraction should shield the disposition contract by default, as it did for a mere price typo in this week's other materials; however, this argument fails because that price-typo example only shields the disposition contract where the flaw is narrowly confined to a detail the disposition contract does not concern — here the deceit is the same single continuous inducement behind both declarations, which is exactly the "identity of the flaw" scenario this week's materials flag for deceit and duress; consequently, the deceit taints both the purchase contract and the disposition contract identically.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, B's rescission under Section 123 BGB avoids both the purchase contract and the disposition contract, each void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB. Ownership of the car therefore never validly passed to B in the first place, and S remains owner throughout. S's remedy to recover the car runs through vindication (Section 985 BGB), not through unjust enrichment, precisely because ownership never left S — this contrasts with a case where only the purchase contract fails (e.g. a narrow price-term mistake), where the buyer would keep ownership and the seller's remedy would instead run through unjust enrichment.

## Short-Answer / Theory Questions (Continued)

### Question 21

Explain why delivery under Section 929 sentence 1 BGB is treated as a "real act" rather than a declaration of intent, and how an employee handling goods on a business's behalf fits into that framework.

**Model Answer:**

1. Delivery, the second element of the Section 929 sentence 1 BGB movable-transfer test, is classified as a real act (a factual occurrence with legal consequences), not a declaration of intent.
2. Statutory anchor: no separate section defines "real act" as its own category; it follows from how Section 929 sentence 1 BGB frames delivery as the acquirer obtaining possession through the transferor, assessed against the possession rules of Sections 854 ff. BGB, with Section 855 BGB governing possession held through another person.
3. Requirements/features: (1) because delivery is a real act, the rules that police a declaration of intent — capacity, rescission for mistake or deceit, interpretation — apply to the disposition agreement but not to the physical handover itself; (2) agency rules under Sections 164 ff. BGB likewise do not govern the handover as such, only the agreement; (3) where an employee physically holds or hands over goods for a business, the governing framework is Section 855 BGB (possession through an agent in possession): the employee's physical control counts in law as the employer's possession, not as a separate possession the employee holds in their own right.
4. Example: a warehouse worker who hands a buyer a crate on the employer's instructions performs the real act of delivery for the employer under Section 855 BGB; the worker's own state of mind is irrelevant to the disposition agreement, which exists between the employer and the buyer.
5. Trap: do not reach for ordinary agency doctrine (authority, ratification under Sections 164 ff.) to explain why an employee's handover counts as the employer's delivery — that is a possession question under Section 855 BGB, not a representation question, even though both scenarios involve someone acting "for" the business.

### Question 22

Beyond the basic requirements tested in a straightforward assignment case, what limits can still prevent a claim from being validly assigned under Section 398 BGB?

**Model Answer:**

1. Section 398 BGB assignment is not unlimited — even where the ordinary disposition-contract, existence, identifiability, and transferability elements look satisfied, specific limits can still block or complicate a transfer.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 398 BGB (assignment), read together with the transferability requirement already built into the ordinary assignment test.
3. Requirements/features: (1) a statutory exclusion or a contractual clause can remove transferability entirely, so the fourth ordinary requirement fails outright even if everything else is satisfied; (2) future claims — not yet in existence when the assignment agreement is made — can still be validly assigned, but only if they are identifiable in advance, the same determination logic used for movable transfers; (3) there is generally no good-faith acquisition of a claim that does not actually exist — unlike movable good-faith acquisition under Sections 929, 932 BGB, an assignee cannot become creditor of a claim that was never real, however honestly the assignee believed otherwise.
4. Example: a supplier assigning its future purchase-price claims against customers under an ongoing supply relationship can validly assign those claims in advance if the debtor and claim category are identifiable, but a bank taking assignment of a claim that in fact never arose (e.g. an already-fully-paid invoice mistakenly assigned again) does not become creditor of anything, regardless of its good faith.
5. Trap: do not treat an assignment as automatically valid just because assignor and assignee agreed — always separately check that the claim exists, is identifiable, and is actually transferable (not excluded by statute or by a contractual anti-assignment clause); these are the same requirement categories as an ordinary factoring case, but here the question is testing the limits, not the successful case.

### Question 23 (Case — integrates Week 10 + Week 09)

**Facts:** B buys a new laptop from retailer S. S delivers the laptop and B pays; ownership passes to B under Section 929 sentence 1 BGB. Two weeks later, B discovers a material defect in the screen that already existed at the moment of transfer of risk, and demands cure by replacement (Nachlieferung) under Section 439 BGB. S agrees, ships B a brand-new, defect-free laptop, and asks B to send the defective one back. B does so.

**Question:** (a) Does ownership of the defective laptop revert to S automatically once B demands replacement, or only later, and (b) does B become owner of the replacement laptop the moment S dispatches it, or only later?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Claim/issue sentence.** Both questions are governed by the ordinary Section 929 sentence 1 BGB transfer test, applied separately in each direction — replacement cure under Section 439 BGB is a warranty-law remedy generating obligation-contract duties to transfer, but, consistent with the principle of separation, it does not itself perform either property-law transfer.

II. **Requirements — return transfer, defective laptop (Section 929 sentence 1 BGB).**
   I. Agreement that ownership of the defective laptop shall pass back to S.
   II. Delivery — S obtains possession through B.
   III. Agreement still exists at that delivery.
   IV. B is authorized (owner, having received ownership at the original sale).

II (continued). **Requirements — replacement laptop (Section 929 sentence 1 BGB).**
   V. Agreement that ownership of the replacement laptop shall pass to B.
   VI. Delivery — B obtains possession through S.
   VII. Agreement still exists at that delivery.
   VIII. S is authorized (owner of the new unit).

III.–IV. **Application and interim results.**
   - Return transfer timing: questionable is whether ownership of the defective laptop reverts to S automatically the moment B demands replacement. This is supported by the practical framing that replacement cure functionally substitutes one item for another; against this, the principle of separation means a duty to transfer is not itself a transfer — ownership changes hands only through a real Section 929 disposition (agreement plus delivery); however, this second view prevails, because nothing in the Section 439 BGB warranty remedy substitutes for an actual disposition, and Week 10's own materials treat every ownership change as requiring its own agreement-plus-delivery act; consequently, ownership of the defective laptop stays with B until B actually agrees to return it and ships it to S.
   - Once B ships the defective laptop back under the shared understanding that it is being returned as part of the cure arrangement, agreement and delivery both occur, and B — still owner at that point — is authorized. Met: ownership of the defective laptop passes back to S only at that moment.
   - Replacement transfer timing: symmetrically, S's shipment of the new laptop, understood by both parties as the agreed replacement performance, is a fresh Section 929 sentence 1 disposition — agreement (the replacement performance under the cure arrangement), delivery (B obtains possession through S upon actual receipt, not upon dispatch), agreement still existing at that point, and S's authorization as owner of the new unit before shipping it. Met once B actually receives the replacement.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, (a) ownership of the defective laptop does not revert to S automatically when cure is demanded — it passes back only through a separate Section 929 sentence 1 disposition completed when B actually returns it — and (b) B becomes owner of the replacement laptop only once it is actually delivered to B, not the moment S dispatches it. Replacement cure under Section 439 BGB creates duties to transfer in both directions, but the property-law consequences still run through two independent Section 929 sentence 1 transfers, exactly as the principle of separation requires.
