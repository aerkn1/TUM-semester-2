## Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 1

Define a declaration of intent and explain why it has two dimensions.

**Model Answer:** A declaration of intent is the expression of a person's will aimed at producing a legal consequence. There is no single statutory definition section for the term itself, but its effects are anchored in Sections 130, 133, and 157 BGB. It has two dimensions: an objective element (the will must be expressed outwardly, e.g. by writing, speech, or conclusive behavior) and a subjective element (the person must internally intend the relevant legal act, especially the intention to be legally bound). For example, nodding at an auction can be a declaration of intent even without words, because conclusive behavior is a valid objective expression. The trap is treating the objective element alone as sufficient — contract law asks how the declared behavior is legally understood from the recipient's perspective, not only what was outwardly done.

### Question 2

Compare an offer with an invitatio ad offerendum.

**Model Answer:** An offer is a declaration of intent requesting contract conclusion so that the contract depends only on the recipient's consent; it is triggered once a party expresses definite essentialia negotii (parties, good/service, price) with a clear intention to be bound, and its legal consequence is that the offeror is bound under Section 145 BGB unless bound-ness was excluded. An invitatio ad offerendum is an invitation to make an offer, not an offer itself; it is triggered when the declaror lacks intention to be legally bound toward every possible respondent (interpreted under Sections 133, 157 BGB), and its legal consequence is that the other party's response is the offer, requiring a further acceptance from the original declaror. Contrast example: a laptop displayed in a shop window with a price tag is normally an invitatio, so a customer saying "I'll take it" is the offer, not an acceptance. The exam trap is treating any advertisement, catalog listing, or price display as an offer simply because it states object and price.

### Question 3

List the requirements for a legal transaction to be void under Section 134 BGB, and explain why the rule exists.

**Model Answer:** The requirements are: (1) a legal transaction (e.g. a contract) exists; (2) the transaction violates a statutory prohibition, meaning a rule outside the BGB itself (criminal, administrative, or EU law) that the legal order treats as mandatory; (3) the statute does not itself provide a different legal consequence for the violation (e.g. a mere fine instead of voidness). If these are met, the consequence is that the legal transaction is void under Section 134 BGB. The rule exists to preserve the unity of the legal order — the civil law should not enforce an agreement, such as an illegal export deal or a cartel agreement violating Art. 101 II TFEU, that another branch of law has already declared prohibited.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 4 (Case)

**Facts:** Bakery owner B places a tray of pastries in the shop window with a sign reading "Croissant — EUR 2.50." Customer K walks up to the counter and says, "I'll take one croissant." The employee at the till, T, has just been told by B that the croissant price was mistakenly listed too low and should be EUR 3.50, so T tells K the price is EUR 3.50. K insists on paying EUR 2.50, citing the window sign.

**Question:** Did K and B conclude a purchase agreement for one croissant at EUR 2.50?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/Issue Sentence
K and B concluded a valid purchase agreement at EUR 2.50 if there are two matching declarations of intent, offer and acceptance, and no validity obstacle applies.

II. Requirements
1. An effective offer with essentialia negotii (parties, good, price) and intention to be legally bound.
2. A matching acceptance.

III. Application
Questionable is whether the window display with the sign "EUR 2.50" is itself an offer. This is supported by the fact that the sign names both the good (croissant) and a price. Against this, one could argue that a shop window display is normally an invitatio ad offerendum under Sections 133, 157 BGB, because the baker does not intend to be bound to sell to every possible customer regardless of stock or pricing errors — a reasonable recipient would understand the display as an invitation to make an offer, not a binding offer itself. Consequently, the display is not an offer; K's statement "I'll take one croissant" is the offer, referencing the displayed price of EUR 2.50 as its implied term.

Questionable is whether T's response of "EUR 3.50" is an acceptance of K's EUR 2.50 offer. This is supported by nothing in the facts suggesting agreement; T explicitly names a different price. Against this, there is no room to argue T accepted, since a reply naming a different price does not mirror the offer. However, under Section 150 II BGB, a response changing the terms of an offer counts as rejection of the original offer combined with a new offer at the changed terms. Consequently, T's EUR 3.50 statement is a rejection of K's EUR 2.50 offer and a new offer at EUR 3.50, which K has not accepted (K insists on EUR 2.50, which is now a renewed offer at the old price, not an acceptance of T's new offer).

IV. Interim Result
No matching declarations exist at EUR 2.50: K's offer at EUR 2.50 was rejected and countered at EUR 3.50, and K's repeated insistence on EUR 2.50 is a further offer that T/B has not accepted.

V. Final Answer
As a result, no purchase agreement was concluded between K and B at EUR 2.50; the parties are still in the offer/counteroffer stage, and no contract exists at either price unless one side accepts the other's most recent offer.

### Question 5 (Case)

**Facts:** Company S sends a letter to buyer C offering 500 units of packaging material at EUR 4 per unit, stating "This offer is valid for acceptance until Friday, 5 p.m." C's acceptance letter is posted on Thursday but, due to a postal delay, does not reach S's mailroom until the following Monday. S had already sold the units to another buyer on Saturday, believing the offer had lapsed.

**Question:** Was a valid purchase agreement concluded between S and C?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/Issue Sentence
S and C concluded a valid contract if there are two matching declarations of intent, offer and acceptance, and the acceptance was effective and timely.

II. Requirements
1. An effective offer (Section 145 BGB), here with a fixed acceptance deadline under Section 148 BGB.
2. An acceptance that reaches the offeror within the fixed deadline (Section 130 BGB reception requirement, read together with Section 148 BGB).

III. Application
Questionable is whether S's letter is an effective, binding offer. This is supported by the letter naming the goods, quantity, and price (essentialia negotii) and expressing clear intention to be bound by fixing an acceptance deadline. Against this, there is no serious argument the letter is a mere invitatio, since it is addressed to a specific buyer with definite terms. Consequently, S made an effective offer under Section 145 BGB, with a fixed deadline under Section 148 BGB of Friday 5 p.m.

Questionable is whether C's acceptance was effective in time. This is supported by C posting the letter on Thursday, before the deadline. However, under Section 130 BGB, a declaration to an absent party becomes effective only when it reaches the recipient's sphere so that notice could normally be taken — not when it is sent. Against this, one could argue C should not bear the risk of postal delay since C acted diligently, but the general rule places the risk of transmission delay on the declaring party (the sender bears the risk that a declaration arrives late), and Section 149 BGB only rescues a late-arriving acceptance if the offeror recognized (or should have recognized) the delay and failed to notify the acceptor immediately — nothing in the facts suggests S recognized any delay before selling to the other buyer. Consequently, C's acceptance reached S only on Monday, after the Friday 5 p.m. deadline, so the offer had already expired under Section 146 BGB by the time the acceptance arrived.

IV. Interim Result
The offer lapsed under Section 146 BGB before C's acceptance became effective; C's Monday-arriving acceptance is therefore a new offer, not an acceptance of S's original offer.

V. Final Answer
As a result, no purchase agreement was concluded on the original terms; C's late acceptance is a new offer that S has not accepted (S in fact acted inconsistently with it by selling to another buyer).

### Question 6 (Case)

**Facts:** Retailer R sends wholesaler W an offer to buy 100 chairs at EUR 60 each. W replies by email: "Confirmed — 100 chairs, EUR 60 each, delivery within two weeks." Two days later, before delivery, W discovers a competitor would have paid EUR 80 per chair and tells R the "agreement" was never binding because W's email was just a "preliminary confirmation."

**Question:** Is W bound to deliver 100 chairs to R at EUR 60 each?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/Issue Sentence
R could have a claim against W for delivery of 100 chairs at EUR 60 each under Section 433 I BGB if a valid purchase agreement was concluded and W cannot avoid it merely by relabeling the exchange.

II. Requirements
1. An effective offer by R.
2. A matching acceptance by W.
3. No validity obstacle, and no basis for W to unilaterally withdraw after the fact.

III. Application
Questionable is whether R's message was an effective offer. This is supported by the message stating quantity (100 chairs), object (chairs), and price (EUR 60 each) — all essentialia negotii for a purchase agreement — with clear intention to be bound, since it is directed at a specific business partner. Consequently, R made an effective offer under Section 145 BGB.

Questionable is whether W's email is a matching acceptance. This is supported by W's email repeating the same quantity, object, and price, and adding a delivery term consistent with (not contradicting) the offer. Against this, one could argue W's later description of the email as a mere "preliminary confirmation" shows no intention to be bound. However, under the objective recipient horizon (Sections 133, 157 BGB), what matters is not W's subsequent, self-serving characterization but how a reasonable recipient in R's position would have understood the word "Confirmed" together with the matching terms at the time it was sent — this reads as unconditional agreement, not as a preliminary or non-binding step. Consequently, W's email is a matching, binding acceptance.

IV. Interim Result
A valid purchase agreement was concluded when W's acceptance reached R; W's later relabeling does not retroactively remove that binding effect, since intention to be bound is judged at the time of the declaration, from the recipient's objective perspective.

V. Final Answer
As a result, R can claim delivery of 100 chairs at EUR 60 each from W under Section 433 I BGB; W's discovery of a better price after the fact does not undo the concluded contract.

### Question 7 (Case)

**Facts:** During negotiations for a custom-built delivery van, manufacturer M emails buyer B: "Here is our proposal: base price EUR 45,000 for the specification we discussed. Before we finalize anything, please come back to us once you've confirmed the exact interior layout with your logistics team, since that affects the final configuration." B replies, "Great, EUR 45,000 works for us, let's proceed," without ever confirming the interior layout.

**Question:** Did B and M conclude a contract for the delivery van at EUR 45,000?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/Issue Sentence
B and M concluded a valid contract at EUR 45,000 if M's email was an effective offer and B's reply was a matching acceptance.

II. Requirements
1. An offer containing essentialia negotii with clear intention to be bound.
2. A matching acceptance.

III. Application
Questionable is whether M's email is an offer at all. This is supported by the email naming a price (EUR 45,000) and a good (the delivery van in the discussed specification). Against this, one could argue the wording shows M did not yet intend to be bound: "before we finalize anything" and the request that B first confirm the interior layout with the logistics team, since that "affects the final configuration," indicate that a material term (the exact configuration) is still open and that M wants to review it before concluding anything. Under the objective recipient horizon (Sections 133, 157 BGB), a reasonable recipient would understand this as a letter of intent or a step within ongoing negotiations rather than a definite offer, because the essentialia negotii (the actual good to be delivered, i.e. the configuration) are not yet fixed and the parties have deliberately left the deal open pending further confirmation. Consequently, M's email is not an effective offer.

Questionable is whether B's reply could nonetheless create a contract. This is supported by nothing, since without an effective offer from M, B's reply can at most be B's own offer to contract at EUR 45,000 on the previously discussed (but unconfirmed) specification. However, M never accepted B's reply — the facts show no further response from M. Consequently, no acceptance of B's offer occurred either.

IV. Interim Result
Neither party made and had accepted an effective, fully-defined offer; the negotiations remained open pending confirmation of the interior layout.

V. Final Answer
As a result, no purchase agreement was concluded between B and M at EUR 45,000; B's reply is at most an unaccepted offer, and M remains free to negotiate further or walk away.

### Question 8 (Case — integrates Week 03 + Week 04)

**Facts:** Art dealer A offers collector K a painting for EUR 20,000, describing it in the offer letter as "an original work by the renowned painter Falkner." K accepts in writing, and the parties conclude the purchase agreement. Two weeks later, before any warranty issue is even raised, K discovers through an independent appraiser that the painting was in fact painted by an unknown apprentice in Falkner's workshop, not by Falkner himself, and neither A nor K knew this at the time of contracting. K wants to know whether the contract can be undone.

**Question:** Can K avoid the purchase agreement with A?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/Issue Sentence
The contract between K and A was validly concluded under the ordinary offer/acceptance rules (Sections 145, 130 BGB), so K's claim could be excluded or modified only if K can rely on rescission of the declaration of intent.

II. Requirements
1. A valid contract was concluded (formation check, Week 03).
2. A rescission ground exists under Section 119 II BGB (mistake about an essential characteristic).
3. Rescission is timely declared under Sections 143, 121 BGB.
4. No confirmation under Section 144 BGB bars rescission.

III. Application
On formation: the offer letter names the good (a specific painting), attributes authorship to Falkner, and states a price (EUR 20,000), satisfying the essentialia negotii; K's written acceptance mirrors these terms. Consequently, a valid purchase agreement was concluded under Sections 145, 130 BGB — this closes the Week 03 formation question and shifts the analysis to whether K can now get out of that valid contract.

On the rescission ground: questionable is whether K can rescind for a mistake about the painting's authorship. This is supported by the fact that authorship by a specific renowned artist is generally regarded in the art trade as an essential characteristic of a painting for purposes of Section 119 II BGB, since it directly affects value and identity of the object, not a peripheral detail. Against this, one could argue this should instead be treated as a warranty (defect) issue under Sections 434, 437 BGB, since the good delivered does not match the agreed description. However, the facts state neither party knew the truth at the time of contracting — this is a case of mutual, innocent mistake about a characteristic assumed at formation, which is squarely a Section 119 II mistake case, not a case where A knowingly delivered something different (which would instead push toward warranty law or, if A had lied, toward Section 123 deceit — neither applies here since the facts rule out knowledge on both sides). Consequently, K has a valid ground to rescind under Section 119 II BGB.

On timing and confirmation: K discovered the mistake and, on these facts, is raising it promptly (within the same short window), so rescission would be without culpable delay under Section 121 I BGB. Nothing in the facts suggests K did anything to confirm the transaction after discovering the truth, so Section 144 BGB does not bar rescission.

IV. Interim Result
K has an effective, timely, unconfirmed ground to rescind the declaration of intent under Section 119 II BGB.

V. Final Answer
As a result, if K declares rescission under Section 143 BGB, the purchase agreement is treated as void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB, and K must return the painting while A returns the EUR 20,000 (with A potentially owing K reliance damages under Section 122 BGB if K incurred reliance costs, since this is an error case, not a deceit case).

## Short-Answer / Theory Questions (Continued)

### Question 9

List the requirements for a valid, binding offer under Section 145 BGB.

**Model Answer:**

The requirements are:
1. A declaration of intent with both an objective element (external expression) and a subjective element (intention to be legally bound), judged from the objective recipient horizon (Sections 133, 157 BGB).
2. Sufficiently definite essentialia negotii — for a purchase, the parties, the good, and the price.
3. The declaration is effective, meaning it was issued and, for an absent recipient, received under Section 130 BGB.
4. No exclusion of being bound, and the offer has not expired under Section 146 BGB.

If these are met, the consequence is that the offeror is bound under Section 145 BGB and the contract depends only on the recipient's matching acceptance. The rule exists so that a business partner can rely on a definite proposal without the offeror being free to walk away the moment it becomes inconvenient — private autonomy is exercised, and responsibly, the moment a definite offer is made.

### Question 10

Explain the difference between issuing and reception of a declaration of intent, and state when a declaration to an absent party becomes effective.

**Model Answer:**

1. Issuing and reception are the two steps by which a declaration of intent becomes effective toward an absent recipient.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 130 BGB.
3. Requirements/features: (1) issuing occurs when the declarant sends the declaration on its way, e.g. by speaking the words or posting/sending the message; (2) reception occurs when the declaration enters the recipient's sphere of influence so that, under normal circumstances, the recipient could take notice of it — actual reading is not required; (3) for present parties (including phone or other real-time communication), issuing and reception are normally simultaneous; for absent parties, they happen successively, and a gap in time is expected.
4. Example: a letter placed in a shop's letterbox on Saturday evening, when the shop is closed for the weekend, is not received Saturday evening — reception occurs when the recipient could normally be expected to check the letterbox, e.g. Monday morning.
5. Trap: students conflate "sent" with "received." Section 130 BGB effectiveness turns on reception (entering the recipient's sphere), not on the moment the declarant let go of the message, and a revocation only blocks the original declaration if it reaches the recipient before or at the same time.

### Question 11

Compare late acceptance under Section 150 I BGB with modified acceptance under Section 150 II BGB.

**Model Answer (Compare Schema):**

Late acceptance under Section 150 I BGB arises when a recipient agrees to the exact terms of an offer but the agreement reaches the offeror only after the offer has already expired under Section 146 BGB (through rejection or lapse of the acceptance period under Sections 147-149 BGB); its legal consequence is that the late, otherwise-matching acceptance is treated as a new offer from the original acceptor, which the original offeror is now free to accept or ignore. Modified acceptance under Section 150 II BGB arises when a reply changes any term of the offer — price, quantity, object, or other conditions — regardless of timing; its legal consequence is that the reply operates as a rejection of the original offer combined with a new offer on the changed terms. Contrast example: a buyer who says "yes" to a seller's EUR 200 bike offer, but whose letter arrives a week after the offer lapsed, has made a late acceptance (Section 150 I); a buyer who replies "I'll take it for EUR 150" the same day has made a modified acceptance (Section 150 II), independent of timing. Exam trap: do not reach for Section 150 II merely because a reply arrived late — timing problems belong to Section 150 I/146, while content changes belong to Section 150 II; a reply can also do both if it is both late and modified, but the two sections address different defects.

### Question 12

Explain the default rule on formal requirements for legal transactions and name the main statutory exceptions.

**Model Answer:**

1. Formal requirements refer to whether a declaration of intent must take a specific form (written, text, electronic, or notarial) to be valid.
2. Statutory anchor: Sections 125, 126, 126a, 126b, 128 BGB.
3. Requirements/features: (1) the default rule, flowing from private autonomy, is that no form is required — an oral or even conclusive declaration suffices; (2) a statute can impose written form (Section 126 BGB), electronic form (Section 126a BGB), text form (Section 126b BGB), or notarial recording (Section 128 BGB) for specific transaction types; (3) if a required form is missing, the legal transaction is void under Section 125 BGB.
4. Example: a guarantee (suretyship) under Section 766 BGB requires written form, and a transfer of real estate or of GmbH shares requires notarial recording — an oral promise to guarantee a friend's debt is void for lack of form even if both parties clearly agreed.
5. Trap: students treat "no form required" as a universal rule and forget that specific statutes carve out exceptions; conversely, Section 125 must never be used to attack a transaction merely because the price seems unfair — that is a Section 138 problem, not a form problem.

### Question 13

Distinguish a service contract from a works contract and explain why the distinction matters.

**Model Answer (Compare Schema):**

A service contract under Sections 611 ff. BGB is triggered whenever a party promises to perform an activity or exert effort, and its legal consequence is that only the performance of the promised service is owed — no particular result is guaranteed, so no result-based warranty regime attaches merely because the outcome disappoints. A works contract under Sections 631 ff. BGB is triggered whenever a party promises to bring about a specific successful result, and its legal consequence is that the result itself is owed, exposing the party to a functional warranty-style regime (Sections 633 ff. BGB) if the promised result is not achieved. Contrast example: a doctor treating a patient or a lawyer advising a client owes diligent effort under a service contract, not a guaranteed cure or a guaranteed winning case; a tailor commissioned to produce a suit, or a repair shop commissioned to fix a car, owes the finished, functioning result under a works contract. Exam trap: do not classify a contract as "works" just because it involves skilled labor — the decisive question is whether a specific, objectively verifiable result was promised (works) or only diligent performance of an activity (service), since this determines which remedy regime applies if things go wrong.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions (Continued)

### Question 14 (Case)

**Facts:** Antique dealer A tells collector K on the phone, "I have a 19th-century oak writing desk, in the condition we discussed, for EUR 3,000." K replies immediately, "I need to check with my restorer before I can say yes, I'll call you back." Twenty minutes later, K calls back and says, "Yes, I'll take it." In the meantime, A has already sold the desk to a walk-in customer, five minutes after the first call ended.

**Question:** Did K and A conclude a purchase agreement for the desk?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/Issue Sentence
K and A concluded a valid contract for the desk if there are two matching declarations of intent, offer and acceptance, and K's acceptance was timely.

II. Requirements
1. An effective offer by A containing essentialia negotii with intention to be bound.
2. A timely acceptance under the applicable timing rule for the mode of communication used.

III. Application
Questionable is whether A's phone statement is an effective offer. This is supported by the statement naming the good (a specific 19th-century oak writing desk "in the condition we discussed") and a price (EUR 3,000), addressed to a specific counterparty. Consequently, A made an effective offer under Section 145 BGB.

Questionable is whether K's acceptance twenty minutes later was timely. This is supported by nothing suggesting A fixed a different deadline under Section 148 BGB, so the default timing rule of Section 147 BGB applies. However, a phone call is treated as communication between present parties under Section 147 I BGB, meaning the offer could only be accepted immediately, during that same call; K's own reply during the call ("I need to check... I'll call you back") did not accept but instead signaled that no immediate decision was being made. Against this, one could argue twenty minutes is a short delay that should not matter — however, Section 147 I BGB does not tolerate any delay for present-party/real-time communication, precisely because the offeror needs to know at once whether they remain bound. Consequently, A's offer expired under Section 146 BGB at the latest when the phone call ended without an immediate "yes."

IV. Interim Result
A's offer lapsed at the end of the phone call because K did not accept immediately; K's follow-up call twenty minutes later is a new offer, not a valid acceptance of A's original offer.

V. Final Answer
As a result, no purchase agreement was concluded between K and A for the desk; K's callback is merely a new offer, and A — having already sold the desk — is free not to accept it.

### Question 15 (Case)

**Facts:** Landowner L, who has just inherited a small plot and has no experience with real-estate transactions, is approached by developer D. D knows the plot is worth roughly EUR 200,000 on the open market but, exploiting L's inexperience and eagerness to quickly settle the inheritance, pressures L into signing a notarized sale agreement for EUR 60,000, a price D privately describes to a colleague as "an absolute steal off a clueless heir."

**Question:** Is the sale agreement between L and D valid?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/Issue Sentence
The sale agreement between L and D is valid unless it is void for violating public policy through usury under Section 138 II BGB.

II. Requirements
1. A valid contract was concluded on ordinary formation grounds (offer, acceptance; here also satisfying the notarial form required for land transfers under Section 128 BGB).
2. A striking disproportion between performance and consideration.
3. Exploitation of the other party's weaker position (e.g. inexperience, need, or lack of judgment).

III. Application
On formation and form: the facts show a notarized sale agreement naming the plot and the price, satisfying essentialia negotii and the notarial-form requirement for land transactions. Consequently, an ordinary valid contract was concluded on its face.

Questionable is whether Section 138 II BGB nonetheless voids it. This is supported by the price (EUR 60,000) being roughly 70% below the plot's market value (EUR 200,000) — a striking disproportion between performance and consideration. This is further supported by D's own description of the deal as "an absolute steal off a clueless heir," which evidences D's awareness of the imbalance. Against this, one could argue a low sale price alone is not automatically usury, since parties are free to strike a bad bargain under ordinary private autonomy. However, Section 138 II BGB requires not just disproportion but also exploitation of the other party's inexperience, need, lack of judgment, or significantly weaker bargaining position, and here the facts show exactly that: L's inexperience with real-estate transactions and eagerness to quickly settle the inheritance were the levers D consciously used ("exploiting L's inexperience and eagerness"). Consequently, both the objective disproportion and the subjective exploitation elements of Section 138 II BGB are met.

IV. Interim Result
The sale agreement satisfies both requirements of usury under Section 138 II BGB.

V. Final Answer
As a result, the sale agreement between L and D is void from the outset under Section 138 II BGB; L is not bound to transfer the plot for EUR 60,000, and any performance already exchanged would need to be reversed under unjust-enrichment principles rather than the contract itself.

### Question 16 (Case)

**Facts:** On Monday morning, supplier S posts a letter to buyer B offering 200 office chairs at EUR 45 each. On Monday afternoon, S has a change of heart and sends a same-day courier with a revocation notice. The courier delivers the revocation notice to B's office at 3 p.m. Monday. The original letter, sent by regular post, does not reach B's mailroom until Tuesday morning.

**Question:** Is S still bound by the Monday morning offer?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/Issue Sentence
S remains bound by the offer under Section 145 BGB unless the revocation was effective to block the offer under Section 130 I sentence 2 BGB.

II. Requirements
1. An effective offer was issued by S.
2. A revocation was also issued.
3. The revocation reached the recipient before or at the same time as the original declaration.

III. Application
Questionable is whether S's letter is an effective offer. This is supported by the letter naming quantity (200 chairs), object (office chairs), and price (EUR 45 each), addressed to a specific buyer. Consequently, S issued an effective offer under Section 145 BGB when the letter was posted Monday morning.

Questionable is whether the revocation blocks the offer's effectiveness. This is supported by Section 130 I sentence 2 BGB, under which a declaration is not effective if a revocation reaches the recipient before or at the same time as the original declaration. Against this, one could argue the offer was issued (posted) before the revocation was sent, so the revocation should be too late. However, Section 130 I sentence 2 BGB does not compare issuing times — it compares reception (arrival) times of the two declarations at the recipient's sphere: the revocation notice reached B's office at 3 p.m. Monday, while the original offer letter did not reach B's mailroom until Tuesday morning. Consequently, the revocation reached B before the offer itself ever became effective.

IV. Interim Result
Because the revocation arrived before the offer reached B's sphere, the offer never became effective in the first place.

V. Final Answer
As a result, S is not bound by the Monday morning offer; the offer was validly blocked by the revocation under Section 130 I sentence 2 BGB, and no offer exists for B to accept.

### Question 17 (Case — integrates Week 03 + Week 06)

**Facts:** Wholesaler W sends retailer R a written order confirmation for a bulk furniture purchase: "500 units, EUR 30/unit, per our standard terms." On the reverse side of the confirmation, in the same size print as the rest of the document, printed terms include a clause stating that W may unilaterally change the delivery date at any time without notice. W and R have done business together many times before using materially the same confirmation form, and R has always signed without comment. R signs and returns this confirmation as well, then later objects when W changes the delivery date without any notice, causing R to miss a resale window.

**Question:** Is W's delivery-date clause part of the contract and can W rely on it?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/Issue Sentence
R's objection to the delivery-date change succeeds only if the clause either never became part of the contract under Section 305c I BGB or is ineffective under content control (Sections 307-309 BGB), after first confirming that a valid underlying contract was formed at all.

II. Requirements
1. A valid contract was concluded on ordinary formation grounds (Week 03: offer, acceptance, essentialia negotii).
2. The clause qualifies as a standard business term under Section 305 I BGB.
3. The clause was validly incorporated under Section 305 II BGB, and is not a surprising clause under Section 305c I BGB.
4. The clause survives content control (Sections 307-309 BGB, as modified for B2B by Section 310 I BGB).

III. Application
On formation: W's confirmation names quantity (500 units), object (furniture), and price (EUR 30/unit); R's signature and return mirror these terms. Consequently, a valid purchase agreement was concluded under Sections 145, 130 BGB — this satisfies the Week 03 formation layer before any clause-specific analysis begins.

On SBT status: the clause is pre-formulated, printed on a reused confirmation form, and presented unilaterally by W for repeated use — the parties' history of "many times before using materially the same confirmation form" satisfies the repeated-use threshold. Consequently, the clause is a standard business term under Section 305 I BGB.

On incorporation and surprise: questionable is whether the clause is nonetheless excluded as a surprising clause under Section 305c I BGB. This is supported by the clause sitting on the reverse side of the document — a location commonly associated with SBT and thus not inherently hidden — printed in the same size as the rest of the text, not concealed in fine print. Against this, one could argue a clause letting W change the delivery date "at any time without notice" is substantively surprising given the businesses' established pattern of reliable delivery dates. However, surprise under Section 305c I BGB is assessed by placement and framing, not merely by how one-sided the content is — a clause that is readable, positioned in the conventional location for terms, and consistent with a repeated course of dealing where R "always signed without comment" does not meet the placement-based surprise threshold; its harshness is instead a content-control question. Consequently, the clause is validly incorporated and not excluded under Section 305c I BGB.

On content control: questionable is whether the clause survives Sections 307-309 BGB. Both W and R appear to be businesses (a wholesaler and a retailer transacting in bulk furniture), so this is a B2B relationship and, under Section 310 I BGB, Sections 308-309 do not apply directly — Section 307 is the central route, using 308/309 values only as indirect guidance. This is supported by the fact that an unlimited, notice-free right to change the delivery date at any time removes a core element of R's ability to plan resale, shifting significant risk onto R without any counterbalancing protection, similar in spirit to the kind of unreasonable, one-sided disadvantage Section 308 would flag directly in a B2C context. However, one could argue against this that some flexibility clauses are common and reasonable in bulk-supply relationships. Consequently, an unlimited, notice-free change right of this breadth unreasonably disadvantages R under the general Section 307 I/II standard, since it deprives R of the reliability that is a core purpose of fixing a delivery term in the first place.

IV. Interim Result
The underlying contract is valid; the clause is a validly incorporated SBT term (not surprising), but it fails content control under Section 307 BGB because it grants W an unreasonably one-sided, unlimited change right.

V. Final Answer
As a result, W's delivery-date clause is ineffective; under Section 306 I, II BGB the rest of the contract remains valid, and the statutory default delivery-term expectations fill the gap, meaning W could not unilaterally change the delivery date without notice and may be liable to R for the resulting harm.

### Question 18 (Case — integrates Week 03 + Week 07)

**Facts:** Procurement manager M works for buyer company B and regularly negotiates supply contracts on B's behalf, always signing as "M, on behalf of B GmbH." M emails supplier S: "B GmbH would like to order 1,000 units of component X at EUR 12 each, per our usual terms." S replies by email, "Confirmed, 1,000 units at EUR 12, delivery in three weeks." M has, in fact, exceeded her internal authorization limit of EUR 10,000 per order (1,000 x EUR 12 = EUR 12,000) without realizing it, but S has no way of knowing about this internal cap.

**Question:** Is B GmbH bound to purchase 1,000 units at EUR 12 each from S?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/Issue Sentence
S could have a claim against B GmbH for acceptance and payment under Section 433 II BGB if a contract was validly formed between S and M's declarations, and if M's declaration is attributable to B GmbH under the rules of agency.

II. Requirements
1. A valid contract was concluded on ordinary formation grounds between the declarations exchanged (Week 03).
2. M acted with her own declaration of intent, in B GmbH's name (publicity), and within her power of representation (Section 164 I BGB).
3. If M lacked sufficient internal authorization, whether that internal limit defeats her external power to bind B GmbH.

III. Application
On formation: M's email states quantity (1,000 units), object (component X), and price (EUR 12/unit); S's reply mirrors these terms and adds a delivery term consistent with, not contradicting, the offer. Consequently, on their face, matching declarations exist that would form a valid contract under Sections 145, 130 BGB, subject to confirming who the contract binds.

On agency, own declaration and publicity: this is supported by M composing and sending her own email (own declaration) and consistently signing "on behalf of B GmbH" (publicity — S can clearly recognize M is acting for B GmbH, not for herself). Consequently, the first two elements of Section 164 I BGB — own declaration and publicity — are met.

On power of representation: questionable is whether M acted within her power of representation, given that the order (EUR 12,000) exceeds her internal EUR 10,000 authorization limit. This is supported by the fact that M's authorization was granted internally and capped at EUR 10,000, which she exceeded. Against this, one could argue this internal cap should limit what M can bind B GmbH to, so the contract should not bind B GmbH for an order above EUR 10,000. However, the course distinguishes external "can do" (power of representation, which binds the principal toward third parties) from internal "may do" (internal instructions, breach of which creates only internal recourse) — nothing in the facts shows S knew or should have known of the EUR 10,000 internal cap, and M's outward, express authority to negotiate and conclude supply contracts "on behalf of B GmbH" remained externally intact and unqualified from S's perspective. Consequently, M's breach of the internal cap does not defeat her external power of representation toward S.

IV. Interim Result
All three elements of Section 164 I BGB are satisfied: M's own declaration, in B GmbH's name, within externally intact power of representation despite the internal-limit breach.

V. Final Answer
As a result, B GmbH is bound by the contract and must accept and pay for 1,000 units at EUR 12 each under Section 433 II BGB; B GmbH's recourse for M exceeding her EUR 10,000 authorization lies only internally, against M under the employment relationship, not against S.

## Short-Answer / Theory Questions (Further Additions)

### Question 19

Explain Section 151 BGB and why it does not rescue ordinary silence as acceptance.

**Model Answer:**

1. Section 151 BGB dispenses with the need for an acceptance to be *communicated* (received) by the offeror in narrow circumstances, without dispensing with the need for an acceptance to exist in the first place.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 151 BGB.
3. Requirements/features: (1) a declaration of acceptance is not expected by the offeror, or the offeror has waived the need for it under trade custom; (2) the acceptor must still form and externalize an actual intent to accept — some outward, objectively identifiable act of acceptance (e.g. starting to prepare a shipment) — it is only the step of *sending that acceptance to the offeror* that is skipped, not the acceptance itself.
4. Example: a hotel that ships an ordered case of wine without first writing back "confirmed" has accepted under Section 151 BGB the moment it starts performing, since a written confirmation is not expected in that kind of retail exchange.
5. Trap: Section 151 BGB is not a general license to treat silence as acceptance. It still requires the acceptor to actually decide to accept and act on it — a party who does and says nothing at all, with no identifiable accepting act, has not accepted under Section 151 BGB any more than under the general silence rule.

### Question 20

Explain the maxim falsa demonstratio non nocet and its effect on contract formation.

**Model Answer:**

1. Falsa demonstratio non nocet ("a false designation does not harm") means that if both parties actually intend the same thing, their shared true intention controls over the literal, incorrect label or description they used.
2. Statutory anchor: Sections 133, 157 BGB (interpretation of declarations of intent).
3. Requirements/features: (1) both parties use an incorrect or ambiguous label, name, or description when communicating; (2) despite the incorrect wording, both parties actually mean and understand the same real-world object or term; (3) because interpretation under Section 133 BGB looks for the true intent behind a declaration rather than freezing on its literal wording, the shared actual meaning is what the parties are bound to, not the mislabeled one.
4. Example: if a buyer and seller both use the wrong lot number for a specific horse they both physically inspected and clearly meant, the contract is for that actual horse, not for whatever animal happens to carry the misstated lot number.
5. Trap: falsa demonstratio only rescues a mismatch between words and shared intent — if the parties actually meant *different* things (a genuine misunderstanding, not just a shared mislabeling), there is no true consensus at all, and the analysis shifts to whether any contract was concluded rather than to which object it covers.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions (Further Additions)

### Question 21 (Case)

**Facts:** Neighbor N tells homeowner H, who is leaving for a five-day vacation, "Sure, I'll water your garden while you're away, no problem." H thanks N and leaves. N forgets on day three, and H's prized rose bushes wilt. H wants to know if N breached a contract.

**Question:** Did N and H conclude a binding contract for N to water the garden?

**Model Answer:**

**I. Claim/issue sentence.** H could only have a contractual claim against N for the wilted roses if N's statement was a declaration of intent made with the intention to be legally bound, not a mere social courtesy.

**II. Requirements.**
   I. A declaration of intent containing definite essentialia (the parties, the service, and its scope).
   II. Intention to be legally bound, assessed from the objective recipient horizon under Sections 133, 157 BGB.

**III. Application.**

*Requirement I.* N's statement identifies the parties (N and H), the service (watering the garden), and its temporal scope (the five-day absence), so the content is definite enough to describe a service arrangement in principle.

*Requirement II.* Questionable is whether N had the intention to be legally bound. This is supported by the fact that N used an affirmative, seemingly committing phrase ("Sure, I'll water your garden"). Against this, one must weigh the surrounding circumstances the objective recipient horizon requires: the exchange is between neighbors, arises informally on the doorstep as H is leaving, involves no discussion of compensation, liability, or consequences for failure, and concerns the kind of small, everyday favor neighbors routinely do for one another. However, precisely these indicators — nature of the transaction, its economic insignificance, and the absence of any compensation or formality — are the course's own markers for withheld legal-binding intent in a favor situation; a reasonable person in N's position would understand the exchange as a social courtesy, and a reasonable person in H's position could not, in good faith, expect a legally enforceable service contract to have arisen from a casual doorstep favor. Consequently, N's statement lacked the intention to be legally bound.

**IV. Interim result.** No declaration of intent with binding intent was made by N.

**V. Final answer.** As a result, no contract was concluded between N and H; N's forgetfulness is a breach of a social courtesy, not of a legal obligation, and H has no contractual claim for the wilted roses.

### Question 22 (Case — integrates Week 03 + Week 11)

**Facts:** Wholesaler W and retailer R have an ongoing business relationship of several years, regularly trading office supplies. R sends W an order for 300 boxes of printer paper at the usual price, a routine transaction well within their established course of dealing. W receives the order but, distracted by other business, never sends any reply and never ships the paper. Three weeks later R sues W for breach, arguing a contract was concluded the moment the order arrived.

**Question:** Did W's silence amount to acceptance of R's order?

**Model Answer:**

**I. Claim/issue sentence.** R could have a claim against W for delivery of 300 boxes of paper under Section 433 I BGB only if W's silence exceptionally counts as acceptance under Section 362 HGB, since ordinary BGB doctrine treats silence as legally irrelevant.

**II. Requirements (as headings).**
   I. Is W a merchant to whom the Section 362 HGB silence exception can even apply?
   II. Does the order fall within the usual scope of W's commercial business?
   III. Was the offer made within an existing business relationship, giving W reason to expect it?
   IV. Did W fail to respond without undue delay?

**III. Application.**

*Requirement I.* The facts describe W as a wholesaler engaged in the ongoing trade of office supplies, which is a commercial business run in one's own name — this satisfies merchant status under Section 1 HGB, the precondition for any HGB-layer overlay on the ordinary BGB formation analysis.

*Requirement II.* The order is for printer paper, squarely within a wholesaler's ordinary stock-in-trade, so it falls within the usual scope of W's business as Section 362 HGB requires.

*Requirement III.* Questionable is whether R's order falls within Section 362 HGB's existing-relationship requirement. This is supported by the facts stating W and R have an "ongoing business relationship of several years" trading the same category of goods, and that this order is "a routine transaction well within their established course of dealing." Against this, one could argue a general trading history does not guarantee this specific order was expected — however, Section 362 HGB does not require the merchant to have anticipated this exact order, only that the offer arrives within the kind of established relationship where staying silent would reasonably be read as agreement. Consequently, the existing-relationship requirement is met.

*Requirement IV.* Nothing in the facts suggests W responded at all within the three weeks before R sued, let alone promptly — W simply "never sends any reply." Consequently, W failed to respond, and no timely rejection displaced the silence-as-acceptance consequence.

**IV. Interim results.** W is a merchant (requirement I); the order matches W's usual business (requirement II); the offer arose within an existing relationship (requirement III); and W's total silence satisfies the failure-to-respond element (requirement IV).

**V. Final answer.** As a result, W's silence is deemed acceptance under Section 362 HGB, reversing the ordinary BGB rule that silence is legally irrelevant to formation (as would otherwise apply under the general Week 03 offer/acceptance framework); a contract for 300 boxes of paper at the usual price was concluded, and R can claim delivery from W under Section 433 I BGB.

### Question 23

Compare Section 138 I BGB with Section 138 II BGB and explain why a case should not cite them interchangeably.

**Model Answer (Compare Schema):**

Section 138 I BGB is triggered whenever a legal transaction, viewed as a whole, is intolerable under public policy or good morals as recognized by the legal order — no specific disparity or exploitation needs to be shown, only that the transaction's content, purpose, or circumstances offend accepted societal values (e.g. a severely one-sided restriction on someone's economic freedom, or overcollateralization that strips a debtor of any realistic ability to conduct business). Its legal consequence is that the transaction is void from the outset. Section 138 II BGB is a specific, narrower sub-case: usury, triggered only when there is a striking, objective disproportion between performance and consideration *and* the stronger party exploited the weaker party's inexperience, need, lack of judgment, or significantly weaker bargaining position. Its legal consequence is likewise voidness from the outset. Contrast example: a contract clause that forces an employee to work for a single employer for the next twenty years regardless of pay is a Section 138 I problem (an intolerable restriction on personal and economic freedom, with no need to show price disparity or exploitation); a notarized land sale at 30% of market value pushed onto an inexperienced heir eager to settle an inheritance quickly is a Section 138 II usury problem (disparity plus exploitation). Exam trap: do not reach for Section 138 II merely because a transaction "feels unfair" — 138 II has two cumulative, provable elements (disproportion and exploitation); a transaction that is one-sided or oppressive without either element, or without both together, falls back on the general Section 138 I standard instead, if it falls under Section 138 at all.

### Question 24 (Case — integrates Week 03 + Week 10)

**Facts:** Collector K buys a rare stamp from dealer D for EUR 5,000, mistakenly believing (due to a genuine slip in reading D's price list) that the price was EUR 500 — a Section 119 I content mistake. K signs the purchase agreement, pays EUR 5,000, and D physically hands the stamp to K with both parties agreeing that ownership should pass immediately. A week later K discovers the price mistake and wants to know: (a) whether K can rescind the purchase agreement, and (b) whether rescinding it automatically makes K no longer the owner of the stamp.

**Question:** Can K rescind the purchase agreement, and if so, does that rescission by itself undo K's ownership of the stamp?

**Model Answer:**

**I. Claim/issue sentence.** K can rescind the purchase agreement for the price mistake, but whether K remains the stamp's owner afterward depends on the separate question of whether the disposition (ownership-transfer) agreement is independently valid under the principle of abstraction.

**II. Requirements (as headings).**
   I. Is there a valid ground to rescind the purchase (obligation) agreement?
   II. Does that same defect also affect the disposition agreement transferring ownership?

**III. Application.**

*Requirement I.* Questionable is whether K can rescind the purchase agreement. This is supported by K's mistake being a genuine slip in reading the price list, which is a mistake about the content of the declaration under Section 119 I BGB (K did not intend to declare agreement to a EUR 5,000 price). Against this, one could argue this is a mere miscalculation in K's own head, which does not qualify for rescission under the course's own distinction between a content mistake and an unwritten motive error — however, the facts describe a slip in *reading* the price list, meaning K's outward declaration (agreeing to EUR 5,000) did not match what K actually intended to declare, which is exactly a Section 119 I content mistake rather than a mere internal miscalculation. Consequently, K has a valid ground to rescind the purchase agreement under Section 119 I BGB, assuming timely declaration under Sections 143, 121 BGB (satisfied here, given the prompt one-week discovery-to-action gap).

*Requirement II.* This is the abstraction question: under the separation principle, the purchase agreement (obligation contract, Section 433 BGB) and the handover-plus-agreement-that-ownership-shall-pass (disposition contract, Section 929 BGB) are distinct legal transactions, and under the principle of abstraction their validity is independent. This is supported by the fact that Sections 119 I mistakes of this kind — a price/content slip in the underlying deal — typically taint only the obligation contract, not the disposition contract, because K's mistake was about the terms of the sale, not about the separate, later act of agreeing that ownership should pass and physically handing over the stamp. Against this, one could argue a single mistake should logically unwind the whole transaction in one step — however, this is precisely the "one-step thinking" the separation and abstraction principles are designed to prevent; only defects that identically taint both transactions (such as incapacity or Section 123 deceit/duress infecting the decision to hand over the item as well) reach the disposition contract, and nothing in the facts suggests K's price-reading slip infected the separate decision to transfer and accept ownership of the stamp itself. Consequently, the disposition agreement remains valid and unaffected by the rescission of the purchase agreement.

**IV. Interim results.** K validly rescinds the purchase (obligation) agreement under Section 119 I BGB (requirement I), but the disposition agreement transferring ownership of the stamp remains valid and unaffected (requirement II).

**V. Final answer.** As a result, K can rescind the purchase agreement, which is retroactively void under Section 142 I BGB and entitles D to reclaim the EUR 5,000 — but K remains the owner of the stamp, since the abstraction principle keeps the disposition agreement's validity independent of the obligation agreement's defect; D's return claim for the stamp itself must run through unjust enrichment (Section 812 BGB) against the now-legal-basis-less transfer, not through automatic reversal of ownership.
