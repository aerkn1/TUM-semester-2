# Week 03 Contract Law I Practice Questions And Model Answers

Course: Business Law

## Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 1

Define a declaration of intent and explain why it has two dimensions.

**Model Answer:** A declaration of intent is the expression of a person's will aimed at producing a legal consequence. There is no single statutory definition section for the term itself, but its effects are anchored in Sections 130, 133, and 157 BGB. It has two dimensions: an objective element (the will must be expressed outwardly, e.g. by writing, speech, or conclusive behavior) and a subjective element (the person must internally intend the relevant legal act, especially the intention to be legally bound). For example, nodding at an auction can be a declaration of intent even without words, because conclusive behavior is a valid objective expression. The trap is treating the objective element alone as sufficient — contract law asks how the declared behavior is legally understood from the recipient's perspective, not only what was outwardly done.

### Question 2

Compare an offer with an invitatio ad offerendum.

**Model Answer:** An offer is a declaration of intent requesting contract conclusion so that the contract depends only on the recipient's consent; it is triggered once a party expresses definite essentialia negotii (parties, good/service, price) with a clear intention to be bound, and its legal consequence is that the offeror is bound under Section 145 BGB unless bound-ness was excluded. An invitatio ad offerendum is an invitation to make an offer, not an offer itself; it is triggered when the declaror lacks intention to be legally bound toward every possible respondent (interpreted under Sections 133, 157 BGB), and its legal consequence is that the other party's response is the offer, requiring a further acceptance from the original declaror. Contrast example: a laptop displayed in a shop window with a price tag is normally an invitatio, so a customer saying "I'll take it" is the offer, not an acceptance. The exam trap is treating any advertisement, catalog listing, or price display as an offer simply because it states object and price.

### Question 3

List the requirements for a legal transaction to be void under Section 134 BGB, and explain why the rule exists.

**Model Answer:** The requirements are: (1) a legal transaction (e.g. a contract) exists; (2) the transaction violates a statutory prohibition, meaning a rule outside the BGB itself (criminal, administrative, or EU law) that the legal order treats as mandatory; (3) the statute does not itself provide a different legal consequence for the violation (e.g. a mere fine instead of voidness). If these are met, the consequence is that the legal transaction is void under Section 134 BGB. The rule exists to preserve the unity of the legal order — the civil law should not enforce an agreement, such as an illegal export deal or a cartel agreement violating Art. 101 II TFEU, that another branch of law has already declared prohibited.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 4 (Case)

**Facts:** Bakery owner B places a tray of pastries in the shop window with a sign reading "Croissant — EUR 2.50." Customer K walks up to the counter and says, "I'll take one croissant." The employee at the till, T, has just been told by B that the croissant price was mistakenly listed too low and should be EUR 3.50, so T tells K the price is EUR 3.50. K insists on paying EUR 2.50, citing the window sign.

**Question:** Did K and B conclude a purchase agreement for one croissant at EUR 2.50?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/Issue Sentence
K and B concluded a valid purchase agreement at EUR 2.50 if there are two matching declarations of intent, offer and acceptance, and no validity obstacle applies.

II. Requirements
1. An effective offer with essentialia negotii (parties, good, price) and intention to be legally bound.
2. A matching acceptance.

III. Application
Questionable is whether the window display with the sign "EUR 2.50" is itself an offer. This is supported by the fact that the sign names both the good (croissant) and a price. Against this, one could argue that a shop window display is normally an invitatio ad offerendum under Sections 133, 157 BGB, because the baker does not intend to be bound to sell to every possible customer regardless of stock or pricing errors — a reasonable recipient would understand the display as an invitation to make an offer, not a binding offer itself. Consequently, the display is not an offer; K's statement "I'll take one croissant" is the offer, referencing the displayed price of EUR 2.50 as its implied term.

Questionable is whether T's response of "EUR 3.50" is an acceptance of K's EUR 2.50 offer. This is supported by nothing in the facts suggesting agreement; T explicitly names a different price. Against this, there is no room to argue T accepted, since a reply naming a different price does not mirror the offer. However, under Section 150 II BGB, a response changing the terms of an offer counts as rejection of the original offer combined with a new offer at the changed terms. Consequently, T's EUR 3.50 statement is a rejection of K's EUR 2.50 offer and a new offer at EUR 3.50, which K has not accepted (K insists on EUR 2.50, which is now a renewed offer at the old price, not an acceptance of T's new offer).

IV. Interim Result
No matching declarations exist at EUR 2.50: K's offer at EUR 2.50 was rejected and countered at EUR 3.50, and K's repeated insistence on EUR 2.50 is a further offer that T/B has not accepted.

V. Final Answer
As a result, no purchase agreement was concluded between K and B at EUR 2.50; the parties are still in the offer/counteroffer stage, and no contract exists at either price unless one side accepts the other's most recent offer.

### Question 5 (Case)

**Facts:** Company S sends a letter to buyer C offering 500 units of packaging material at EUR 4 per unit, stating "This offer is valid for acceptance until Friday, 5 p.m." C's acceptance letter is posted on Thursday but, due to a postal delay, does not reach S's mailroom until the following Monday. S had already sold the units to another buyer on Saturday, believing the offer had lapsed.

**Question:** Was a valid purchase agreement concluded between S and C?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/Issue Sentence
S and C concluded a valid contract if there are two matching declarations of intent, offer and acceptance, and the acceptance was effective and timely.

II. Requirements
1. An effective offer (Section 145 BGB), here with a fixed acceptance deadline under Section 148 BGB.
2. An acceptance that reaches the offeror within the fixed deadline (Section 130 BGB reception requirement, read together with Section 148 BGB).

III. Application
Questionable is whether S's letter is an effective, binding offer. This is supported by the letter naming the goods, quantity, and price (essentialia negotii) and expressing clear intention to be bound by fixing an acceptance deadline. Against this, there is no serious argument the letter is a mere invitatio, since it is addressed to a specific buyer with definite terms. Consequently, S made an effective offer under Section 145 BGB, with a fixed deadline under Section 148 BGB of Friday 5 p.m.

Questionable is whether C's acceptance was effective in time. This is supported by C posting the letter on Thursday, before the deadline. However, under Section 130 BGB, a declaration to an absent party becomes effective only when it reaches the recipient's sphere so that notice could normally be taken — not when it is sent. Against this, one could argue C should not bear the risk of postal delay since C acted diligently, but the general rule places the risk of transmission delay on the declaring party (the sender bears the risk that a declaration arrives late), and Section 149 BGB only rescues a late-arriving acceptance if the offeror recognized (or should have recognized) the delay and failed to notify the acceptor immediately — nothing in the facts suggests S recognized any delay before selling to the other buyer. Consequently, C's acceptance reached S only on Monday, after the Friday 5 p.m. deadline, so the offer had already expired under Section 146 BGB by the time the acceptance arrived.

IV. Interim Result
The offer lapsed under Section 146 BGB before C's acceptance became effective; C's Monday-arriving acceptance is therefore a new offer, not an acceptance of S's original offer.

V. Final Answer
As a result, no purchase agreement was concluded on the original terms; C's late acceptance is a new offer that S has not accepted (S in fact acted inconsistently with it by selling to another buyer).

### Question 6 (Case)

**Facts:** Retailer R sends wholesaler W an offer to buy 100 chairs at EUR 60 each. W replies by email: "Confirmed — 100 chairs, EUR 60 each, delivery within two weeks." Two days later, before delivery, W discovers a competitor would have paid EUR 80 per chair and tells R the "agreement" was never binding because W's email was just a "preliminary confirmation."

**Question:** Is W bound to deliver 100 chairs to R at EUR 60 each?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/Issue Sentence
R could have a claim against W for delivery of 100 chairs at EUR 60 each under Section 433 I BGB if a valid purchase agreement was concluded and W cannot avoid it merely by relabeling the exchange.

II. Requirements
1. An effective offer by R.
2. A matching acceptance by W.
3. No validity obstacle, and no basis for W to unilaterally withdraw after the fact.

III. Application
Questionable is whether R's message was an effective offer. This is supported by the message stating quantity (100 chairs), object (chairs), and price (EUR 60 each) — all essentialia negotii for a purchase agreement — with clear intention to be bound, since it is directed at a specific business partner. Consequently, R made an effective offer under Section 145 BGB.

Questionable is whether W's email is a matching acceptance. This is supported by W's email repeating the same quantity, object, and price, and adding a delivery term consistent with (not contradicting) the offer. Against this, one could argue W's later description of the email as a mere "preliminary confirmation" shows no intention to be bound. However, under the objective recipient horizon (Sections 133, 157 BGB), what matters is not W's subsequent, self-serving characterization but how a reasonable recipient in R's position would have understood the word "Confirmed" together with the matching terms at the time it was sent — this reads as unconditional agreement, not as a preliminary or non-binding step. Consequently, W's email is a matching, binding acceptance.

IV. Interim Result
A valid purchase agreement was concluded when W's acceptance reached R; W's later relabeling does not retroactively remove that binding effect, since intention to be bound is judged at the time of the declaration, from the recipient's objective perspective.

V. Final Answer
As a result, R can claim delivery of 100 chairs at EUR 60 each from W under Section 433 I BGB; W's discovery of a better price after the fact does not undo the concluded contract.

### Question 7 (Case)

**Facts:** During negotiations for a custom-built delivery van, manufacturer M emails buyer B: "Here is our proposal: base price EUR 45,000 for the specification we discussed. Before we finalize anything, please come back to us once you've confirmed the exact interior layout with your logistics team, since that affects the final configuration." B replies, "Great, EUR 45,000 works for us, let's proceed," without ever confirming the interior layout.

**Question:** Did B and M conclude a contract for the delivery van at EUR 45,000?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/Issue Sentence
B and M concluded a valid contract at EUR 45,000 if M's email was an effective offer and B's reply was a matching acceptance.

II. Requirements
1. An offer containing essentialia negotii with clear intention to be bound.
2. A matching acceptance.

III. Application
Questionable is whether M's email is an offer at all. This is supported by the email naming a price (EUR 45,000) and a good (the delivery van in the discussed specification). Against this, one could argue the wording shows M did not yet intend to be bound: "before we finalize anything" and the request that B first confirm the interior layout with the logistics team, since that "affects the final configuration," indicate that a material term (the exact configuration) is still open and that M wants to review it before concluding anything. Under the objective recipient horizon (Sections 133, 157 BGB), a reasonable recipient would understand this as a letter of intent or a step within ongoing negotiations rather than a definite offer, because the essentialia negotii (the actual good to be delivered, i.e. the configuration) are not yet fixed and the parties have deliberately left the deal open pending further confirmation. Consequently, M's email is not an effective offer.

Questionable is whether B's reply could nonetheless create a contract. This is supported by nothing, since without an effective offer from M, B's reply can at most be B's own offer to contract at EUR 45,000 on the previously discussed (but unconfirmed) specification. However, M never accepted B's reply — the facts show no further response from M. Consequently, no acceptance of B's offer occurred either.

IV. Interim Result
Neither party made and had accepted an effective, fully-defined offer; the negotiations remained open pending confirmation of the interior layout.

V. Final Answer
As a result, no purchase agreement was concluded between B and M at EUR 45,000; B's reply is at most an unaccepted offer, and M remains free to negotiate further or walk away.

### Question 8 (Case — integrates Week 03 + Week 04)

**Facts:** Art dealer A offers collector K a painting for EUR 20,000, describing it in the offer letter as "an original work by the renowned painter Falkner." K accepts in writing, and the parties conclude the purchase agreement. Two weeks later, before any warranty issue is even raised, K discovers through an independent appraiser that the painting was in fact painted by an unknown apprentice in Falkner's workshop, not by Falkner himself, and neither A nor K knew this at the time of contracting. K wants to know whether the contract can be undone.

**Question:** Can K avoid the purchase agreement with A?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/Issue Sentence
The contract between K and A was validly concluded under the ordinary offer/acceptance rules (Sections 145, 130 BGB), so K's claim could be excluded or modified only if K can rely on rescission of the declaration of intent.

II. Requirements
1. A valid contract was concluded (formation check, Week 03).
2. A rescission ground exists under Section 119 II BGB (mistake about an essential characteristic).
3. Rescission is timely declared under Sections 143, 121 BGB.
4. No confirmation under Section 144 BGB bars rescission.

III. Application
On formation: the offer letter names the good (a specific painting), attributes authorship to Falkner, and states a price (EUR 20,000), satisfying the essentialia negotii; K's written acceptance mirrors these terms. Consequently, a valid purchase agreement was concluded under Sections 145, 130 BGB — this closes the Week 03 formation question and shifts the analysis to whether K can now get out of that valid contract.

On the rescission ground: questionable is whether K can rescind for a mistake about the painting's authorship. This is supported by the fact that authorship by a specific renowned artist is generally regarded in the art trade as an essential characteristic of a painting for purposes of Section 119 II BGB, since it directly affects value and identity of the object, not a peripheral detail. Against this, one could argue this should instead be treated as a warranty (defect) issue under Sections 434, 437 BGB, since the good delivered does not match the agreed description. However, the facts state neither party knew the truth at the time of contracting — this is a case of mutual, innocent mistake about a characteristic assumed at formation, which is squarely a Section 119 II mistake case, not a case where A knowingly delivered something different (which would instead push toward warranty law or, if A had lied, toward Section 123 deceit — neither applies here since the facts rule out knowledge on both sides). Consequently, K has a valid ground to rescind under Section 119 II BGB.

On timing and confirmation: K discovered the mistake and, on these facts, is raising it promptly (within the same short window), so rescission would be without culpable delay under Section 121 I BGB. Nothing in the facts suggests K did anything to confirm the transaction after discovering the truth, so Section 144 BGB does not bar rescission.

IV. Interim Result
K has an effective, timely, unconfirmed ground to rescind the declaration of intent under Section 119 II BGB.

V. Final Answer
As a result, if K declares rescission under Section 143 BGB, the purchase agreement is treated as void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB, and K must return the painting while A returns the EUR 20,000 (with A potentially owing K reliance damages under Section 122 BGB if K incurred reliance costs, since this is an error case, not a deceit case).
