## Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 1

Explain the decision rule for distinguishing public law from private law, and give one example of each.

**Model Answer:**

1. Public law and private law are the two basic domains of the German legal system.
2. No single statutory section defines the line; it is a classification test.
3. Requirements/features: (1) public law governs relationships involving a public authority acting with sovereign power, in a position of superordination over the other party (e.g. issuing an administrative act); (2) private law governs relationships between parties who face each other as legal equals, based on private autonomy and self-determination; (3) a public body is not automatically in "public law mode" — if it acts as an ordinary market participant (e.g. buying office supplies), the relationship is private law.
4. Example: a building permit refusal is public law (the state exercises sovereign authority over the applicant); a buyer refusing to pay for a delivered machine is private law (both parties stand as legal equals).
5. Trap: the presence of a public authority as a party is not itself decisive — always ask whether that authority is acting *with* sovereign power or merely *as* a market participant.

### Question 2

State the hierarchy of norms in Germany and name the constitutional anchor for the federal/state layer.

**Model Answer:**

1. The hierarchy of norms ranks which legal source controls when two applicable rules conflict.
2. Statutory anchor: Art. 31 GG for the federal/state layer.
3. Requirements/features: (1) EU law (primary and secondary) has primacy where it applies; (2) German federal law, including the Basic Law, parliamentary statutes, and federal regulations, ranks next; (3) German state law, including state constitutions, statutes, and regulations, ranks below federal law and yields to it under Art. 31 GG in case of conflict.
4. Example: if a federal statute and a Bavarian state statute conflict on the same issue, the federal statute prevails.
5. Trap: hierarchy of norms answers a *rank* question (which legal level wins), not a *specificity* question — do not reach for hierarchy language when the real issue is lex specialis between two rules at the same level.

### Question 3

Compare civil-law and common-law systems without falling into the "one side has no courts/no statutes" trap.

**Model Answer (Compare Schema):**

Civil-law systems treat legislation as the foundation: rules are laid down in written statutes and modified through the legislative process, with judges deciding within that written framework; supreme court decisions are not formally binding on lower courts the way common-law precedent is, but they carry strong practical influence because lower courts risk being overruled. Common-law systems give court decisions a stronger, formally binding role, with legal development often proceeding through case law and precedent binding lower courts under stare decisis. Contrast example: German courts are guided by BGB provisions first and case law second in a persuasive rather than strictly binding sense, while an English court applying stare decisis treats a higher court's prior ruling as binding. Exam trap: never claim civil-law systems lack case law or that common-law systems lack statutes — the systems are converging, and the real distinction is about which source has *primary structural authority*, not which source exists at all.

### Question 4

Explain the BGB's bracketing technique and lex specialis, and state how they interact.

**Model Answer:**

The requirements are:
1. The BGB is organized into five books, with Book 1 (Sections 1-240) containing a General Part.
2. The bracketing technique means Book 1's general rules apply across the later, more specific books (Book 2 Obligations, Book 3 Property, Book 4 Family, Book 5 Succession) unless a more specific provision modifies or displaces them.
3. Lex specialis means that where two provisions cover the same issue, the more specific rule takes precedence over the more general one.

If these are met, the consequence is that solving a contract-law problem typically requires reading more than one section: a specific rule (e.g. a sales-contract provision in Book 2) together with a general rule that brackets it (e.g. a declaration-of-intent rule in Book 1), with lex specialis resolving any direct conflict in favor of the more specific text.

The reason this rule exists is to let the legislator state a principle once (in the General Part) instead of repeating it in every specific chapter, while still allowing specialized areas (like HGB commercial rules) to override the general BGB baseline where business practice requires a different result.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 5 (Case — integrates Week 01-02 + Week 03)

**Facts:** Handels-GmbH, a wholesale merchant, publishes a printed catalog listing "Model X industrial shelving unit — EUR 450, while stocks last" and mails it to regular business customers. Customer K reads the catalog and sends a signed order form back stating "I hereby order one Model X shelving unit at EUR 450." Handels-GmbH's warehouse manager, noticing the catalog listed an outdated price due to a printing error, refuses to ship the unit at that price and instead demands EUR 600. K insists a contract was already concluded at EUR 450 and threatens to sue.

**Question:** Was a valid purchase contract concluded between K and Handels-GmbH at EUR 450?

**Model Answer:**

**I. Claim/issue sentence.** K and Handels-GmbH concluded a valid purchase contract under Section 433 BGB if there are two matching declarations of intent — offer and acceptance — and no validity obstacle applies.

**II. Requirements (as headings).**
   I. An offer.
   II. A matching acceptance.
   III. No obstacle to formation.

**III. Application.**

*Requirement I — offer.* Questionable is whether the catalog itself was an offer capable of being accepted. This is supported by the fact that the catalog names a specific product and a specific price. Against this, one could argue that a printed catalog sent to an open group of business customers is, under Sections 133, 157 BGB (objective recipient horizon), typically read as an invitation to make an offer (invitatio ad offerendum) rather than a binding offer, because Handels-GmbH cannot reasonably intend to bind itself to every recipient regardless of stock or pricing accuracy — the "while stocks last" qualifier reinforces that the sender did not intend unconditional bound-ness under Section 145 BGB. Consequently, the catalog is not itself an offer; K's signed order form is the offer instead.

*Requirement II — matching acceptance.* Questionable is whether Handels-GmbH accepted K's offer. Handels-GmbH's warehouse manager expressly refused to ship at EUR 450 and instead demanded EUR 600, which is not a matching declaration but a rejection combined with a new offer under Section 150 II BGB (an "acceptance" that changes the price is legally a counteroffer). Consequently, there is no acceptance of K's original EUR 450 offer.

*Requirement III — no obstacle.* Since no acceptance occurred, this requirement is not reached; there is no need to test Sections 125, 134, or 138 here because the contract never got past the offer/acceptance stage at the EUR 450 price.

**IV. Interim results.** No binding offer at EUR 450 came from Handels-GmbH's catalog (requirement I fails as to the catalog, but K's order counts as an offer). No acceptance of K's EUR 450 offer occurred (requirement II fails); Handels-GmbH's response is instead a new counteroffer at EUR 600.

**V. Final answer.** As a result, no valid purchase contract was concluded between K and Handels-GmbH at EUR 450. K's order was an offer that Handels-GmbH rejected while making a new counteroffer of EUR 600 under Section 150 II BGB; a contract will only arise if K now accepts that EUR 600 counteroffer.

### Question 6 (Case)

**Facts:** A regional government agency issues a formal order requiring a chemical plant to halt production for 48 hours after an environmental inspection reveals a safety violation. The plant's operator wants to know whether it can challenge the order using ordinary private-law contract remedies, since the agency "did not ask for the plant's consent."

**Question:** Is the relationship between the agency and the plant operator governed by public law or private law, and what follows from that classification?

**Model Answer:**

**I. Claim/issue sentence.** The plant operator's dispute with the agency is governed by public law if the agency acted with sovereign authority in a position of superordination over the plant, rather than as an equal private party.

**II. Requirements (as headings).**
   I. Is a public authority involved?
   II. Did that authority act with sovereign power (superordination/subordination), or merely as an ordinary market participant?

**III. Application.**

*Requirement I.* The order was issued by a regional government agency following an environmental inspection — a public authority is clearly involved.

*Requirement II.* Questionable is whether the agency acted with sovereign authority. This is supported by the nature of the order itself: a unilateral, binding production-halt order issued after a regulatory inspection is a classic administrative act, comparable to a building-permit decision or a regulatory closure order named in the course materials. Against this, one could argue that any government body dealing with a private company is automatically in private law — however, this argument fails because the presence of a public party is not itself decisive; what matters is whether the state exercises sovereign power over the other party rather than negotiating with it as an equal. Here, the agency did not seek the plant's consent and imposed the halt unilaterally, which is the hallmark of superordination, not equal-party negotiation.

**IV. Interim result.** Both requirements are met: a public authority is involved, and it acted with sovereign power.

**V. Final answer.** As a result, the relationship is governed by public law (administrative law), not private law. The plant operator cannot use ordinary private-law contract remedies (such as claims under the BGB) to challenge the order; it must instead pursue the public-law/administrative-law avenues for contesting an administrative act.

### Question 7 (Case)

**Facts:** An EU regulation on food-labeling standards enters into force, stating that all packaged food sold in the Union must display nutrition information in a specified uniform format "directly and in its entirety" from a given date, without requiring any further national implementing legislation. A German food producer argues it can ignore the rule until the Bundestag passes a corresponding German statute.

**Question:** Is the producer correct that no action is required until German legislation is passed?

**Model Answer:**

**I. Claim/issue sentence.** The producer's argument only succeeds if the EU food-labeling rule is a directive requiring national transposition rather than a regulation that applies directly.

**II. Requirements (as headings).**
   I. Classify the EU instrument (regulation or directive).
   II. State the legal consequence that follows from that classification.

**III. Application.**

*Requirement I.* Questionable is whether the instrument is a regulation or a directive. This is supported by the wording used: the rule states it applies "directly and in its entirety," which is the defining language of a regulation under Art. 288 TFEU — a regulation is directly applicable in Member States, binding in its entirety, and creates uniform rules without needing national transposition. Against this, one could argue any EU food-standards rule "sounds like" a directive because harmonization topics are often addressed by directives — however, this argument fails because the decisive criterion is the stated mode of application (direct and entire), not the subject matter. A directive instead sets common goals and requires Member States to transpose it into national law, leaving discretion about form and method. Consequently, this is a regulation.

*Requirement II.* Since the instrument is a regulation, no German implementing statute is needed for it to bind the producer; it takes effect directly in German law once it enters into force under EU law.

**IV. Interim results.** The instrument is a regulation, not a directive (requirement I). Its consequence is direct application without waiting for national legislation (requirement II).

**V. Final answer.** As a result, the producer is not correct. The regulation binds the producer directly and in its entirety from the stated date regardless of whether the Bundestag ever passes a corresponding statute; that "wait for national legislation" logic only applies to directives.

### Question 8 (Case)

**Facts:** A dispute arises over whether a rule in the General Part of the BGB (Book 1) or a more specific rule found in the HGB commercial-law statute governs a sale between two merchants. Student S argues that because the BGB is the "main" civil code and the HGB is a "side" statute, the BGB rule must always control.

**Question:** Is S's reasoning correct, and which principle actually resolves the conflict?

**Model Answer:**

**I. Claim/issue sentence.** Whether the BGB rule or the HGB rule controls is resolved by lex specialis, not by which statute is considered "more central" to German civil law.

**II. Requirements (as headings).**
   I. Do both rules cover the same issue?
   II. Is one of the two rules more specific to the facts at hand?

**III. Application.**

*Requirement I.* The facts state that both a general BGB sales-related rule and a specific HGB merchant rule appear to address the same transaction — a sale between two merchants — so both rules are in principle applicable to the same issue.

*Requirement II.* Questionable is whether the HGB rule is more specific. This is supported by the course's bracketing/lex-specialis method: the HGB layers business-specific rules on top of the general BGB baseline precisely for actors and transactions involving merchants, which is a narrower and more tailored category of facts than the BGB's general civil-law rule. Against this, one could argue that the BGB's status as the foundational civil code should give it automatic priority — however, this argument fails because rank/prominence of a statute is not the criterion; lex specialis asks only which rule is drafted to cover the narrower, more specific factual situation, and a merchant-to-merchant sale is exactly that situation for the HGB provision. Consequently, the HGB rule is the more specific rule and displaces the general BGB rule on this point.

**IV. Interim result.** Both rules cover the same issue, and the HGB rule is the more specific one.

**V. Final answer.** As a result, S's reasoning is incorrect. The applicable principle is lex specialis, not statutory "seniority," and the more specific HGB merchant rule controls over the general BGB rule for this sale between two merchants.

## More Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 9

Explain the distinction between law and morality, and state why an exam answer must not jump directly from "unfair" to "illegal."

**Model Answer:**

1. Morality asks whether conduct is ethically right or wrong; law asks whether a binding, enforceable norm attaches a legal consequence to that conduct.
2. No general statutory anchor defines the line; it is a conceptual distinction from the course's law/morality discussion, though Section 242 BGB (good faith) shows law can incorporate a social standard into a binding legal duty.
3. Requirements/features: (1) some immoral conduct is not illegal (e.g. refusing to help a friend); (2) some legal rules deliberately protect morally complicated behavior (e.g. bluffing about a competing offer in a negotiation); (3) law sometimes explicitly imports a social standard, such as good faith under Section 242 BGB, but the rule only binds because a statute made it a legal duty, not because it feels fair.
4. Example: lying about a pregnancy in a job interview may be morally contested, but whether it has legal consequences depends on whether a specific legal rule (e.g. an unlawful-discrimination or deceit provision) attaches to it — moral disapproval alone creates no claim.
5. Trap: never conclude "this is unfair, therefore illegal" — always identify the specific legal source and the legal consequence it attaches before treating conduct as a legal wrong.

### Question 10

Name the four statutory-interpretation lenses used in the course and illustrate them using Section 123 BGB.

**Model Answer:**

1. The four lenses structure how German lawyers determine the meaning of an unclear or ambiguous legal term.
2. No single section states the method itself; it is the course's standard interpretation toolkit, illustrated in the notes using Section 123 BGB (deceit).
3. Requirements/features: (1) wording — what the text says and how key terms are defined, including the risk that a translated term hides meaning present in the German original; (2) purpose/telos — what the norm is trying to achieve; (3) systematic context — where the norm sits in the statute and how it relates to surrounding provisions; (4) historical interpretation — what the historical legislator or legal tradition suggests.
4. Example: interpreting "deceit" under Section 123 BGB requires more than the English gloss of the word — wording, purpose (protecting free formation of the will), systematic position (within the BGB's rules on declarations of intent), and historical understanding all shape which conduct counts as deceit.
5. Trap: do not treat wording as sufficient on its own — a translated or ambiguous term can hide meaning that only purpose, system, and history reveal.

### Question 11

Explain how a legal norm is applied using the conditions/legal-consequence method, and outline the four-step case-solving process built on it.

**Model Answer:**

1. Every legal norm splits into factual conditions and the legal consequence that follows once all conditions are satisfied.
2. Statutory anchor for the illustrative example: Section 123 BGB, used in the course to introduce the conditions/consequence pattern.
3. Requirements/features (four-step method): (1) identify the applicable legal norm; (2) break the norm into its individual elements/conditions; (3) test the concrete facts against each element in turn; (4) state the legal consequence that follows once all elements are satisfied.
4. Example: to apply Section 123 BGB, first identify the norm (avoidance for deceit), break it into elements (a deceit, causation for the declaration, no exclusion), test each against the facts, and only then state the consequence (the declaration may be avoided).
5. Trap: do not skip straight to a felt "fair" outcome — a legal consequence is only available once every element of the norm has been separately tested against the facts.

### Question 12

Compare EU regulations and directives using the trigger-word method, and give the consequence that follows from each classification.

**Model Answer (Compare Schema):**

Regulations are directly applicable in all Member States, binding in their entirety, and create uniform rules without needing national transposition — the trigger language is "applies directly," "immediately," or "in its entirety." Directives instead set common goals and require Member States to transpose them into national law, leaving discretion about form and method and aiming at harmonization rather than full uniformity — the trigger language is "must implement," "must transpose," or "achieve a goal by [date]." Contrast example: a regulation stating uniform nutrition-labeling rules apply "directly and in their entirety" binds a German food producer the moment it enters into force, with no Bundestag action needed; a directive on the same topic would instead require the Bundestag to pass an implementing statute before the rule binds producers domestically. Exam trap: do not treat a directive as automatically ineffective before transposition — under specific conditions a directive can still have direct effect and grant individual rights even without timely national implementation, though this is the exception, not the rule.

### Question 13

Explain the German federal legislative procedure and the role the Bundesrat plays in it.

**Model Answer:**

1. The legislative procedure is the staged process, involving four main institutions, by which a draft becomes binding federal statute.
2. Not tied to one BGB section — it is the procedural process described in the course's legislative-procedure material (Bundestag, Bundesrat, Bundesregierung, Bundespräsident).
3. Requirements/features (staged flow): (1) initiation — a draft can come from the federal government, the Bundesrat, parliamentary groups, or 5% of Bundestag members; (2) parliamentary process — Bundestag readings, committee work, amendment discussion, adoption by vote; (3) Bundesrat involvement — for consent bills, the Bundesrat can block the statute outright; for objection bills, the Bundestag can overrule a Bundesrat objection under the applicable procedure; (4) execution — signing by the responsible minister or Chancellor, authorization by the Federal President, and promulgation in the Federal Law Gazette.
4. Example: a statute affecting state administrative competences typically needs Bundesrat consent, giving the Länder representative body a genuine veto point that a purely Bundestag-controlled objection bill would not have.
5. Trap: do not treat the Bundesrat as a mere formality — depending on whether a bill is a consent bill or an objection bill, its practical power ranges from an outright block to a surmountable objection, and mixing up the two overstates or understates its role.

## More Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 14 (Case)

**Facts:** The city of Munich's procurement office places an order with a private furniture manufacturer for 200 office chairs for a municipal building, paying the market price under an ordinary purchase agreement identical in form to one a private company would sign. When the manufacturer delivers chairs with a manufacturing defect, the city wants to know whether it must pursue a public-law administrative remedy or an ordinary private-law warranty claim.

**Question:** Is the relationship between the city and the manufacturer governed by public law or private law?

**Model Answer:**

**I. Claim/issue sentence.** The city's dispute with the manufacturer is governed by private law if the city acted as an ordinary market participant rather than exercising sovereign authority, even though a public body is a party.

**II. Requirements (as headings).**
   I. Is a public body a party?
   II. Did that body act with sovereign power/superordination, or as an ordinary market participant?

**III. Application.**

*Requirement I.* The city of Munich, a public body, is one of the two parties.

*Requirement II.* Questionable is whether the city acted with sovereign authority here. This is supported by the fact that none of the hallmarks of sovereign action are present — no unilateral order, no administrative act, no superordination over the manufacturer. Against this, one could argue any contract signed by a public body is automatically public law because of who signed it — however this fails because the presence of a public party is not decisive; ordinary purchasing on market terms puts the city in the same position as any private buyer, matching the course's example of a public body acting as a market participant when buying office supplies. Consequently, the city acted as an ordinary market participant.

**IV. Interim result.** A public body is a party, but it did not act with sovereign power.

**V. Final answer.** As a result, private law governs. The city must pursue an ordinary private-law warranty claim, not a public-law administrative remedy.

### Question 15 (Case)

**Facts:** A German statute permits a food additive that a newly enacted, directly applicable EU regulation expressly bans across all Member States "with immediate and uniform effect." A German producer continues using the additive, relying on the still-unamended German statute, which has not yet been repealed by the Bundestag.

**Question:** Can the producer rely on the German statute permitting the additive?

**Model Answer:**

**I. Claim/issue sentence.** Whether the producer may rely on the German statute depends on whether the EU regulation, as directly applicable EU law, takes precedence over the conflicting national statute even though the German statute has not been formally repealed.

**II. Requirements (as headings).**
   I. Is the EU instrument directly applicable, uniformly binding law?
   II. Does it conflict with the German statute on the same issue?
   III. What is the consequence of that conflict under the hierarchy of norms?

**III. Application.**

*Requirement I.* The instrument is described as taking "immediate and uniform effect" — direct-applicability trigger language — so it is a regulation under Art. 288 TFEU, directly applicable and binding in its entirety without national transposition.

*Requirement II.* The regulation bans what the German statute permits — same issue (use of the additive), opposite outcomes — so there is a genuine conflict.

*Requirement III.* Questionable is which rule controls. This is supported by the fact that EU law has primacy where applicable, ranking above German federal law in the hierarchy of norms; the fact that the Bundestag has not yet repealed the conflicting statute does not suspend that primacy. Against this, one could argue that an unrepealed German statute remains valid domestic law and must be followed until amended — however this fails because primacy is a hierarchy-of-norms question, not a formal-repeal question; the German statute is simply displaced to the extent of the conflict for as long as it conflicts with directly applicable EU law. Consequently, the regulation controls.

**IV. Interim results.** The instrument is a valid, directly applicable regulation (requirement I); it directly conflicts with the German statute (requirement II); EU law prevails under the hierarchy of norms regardless of formal repeal status (requirement III).

**V. Final answer.** As a result, the producer cannot rely on the German statute. The directly applicable EU regulation bans the additive with immediate effect and displaces the conflicting national rule even though the Bundestag has not yet repealed it.

### Question 16 (Case — integrates Week 01-02 + Week 07)

**Facts:** GmbH is a private limited company. Its purchasing employee, E, telephones a supplier and orders raw materials "for my company," using GmbH's account details and referencing GmbH's order number, without E holding a formal written power of attorney beyond E's ordinary purchasing role. The supplier later asks whether the resulting purchase contract binds GmbH or only E personally, and separately asks whether this is even a private-law dispute at all given that GmbH is a state-registered legal construct.

**Question:** (a) Is this a private-law or public-law matter, and (b) does the contract bind GmbH?

**Model Answer:**

**I. Claim/issue sentence.** (a) Whether the dispute is private law depends on whether GmbH and the supplier face each other as legal equals rather than GmbH exercising sovereign authority; (b) whether the contract binds GmbH depends on whether E's declaration meets the three cumulative elements of effective agency under Section 164 I BGB.

**II. Requirements (as headings).**
   I. Classification: is this a relationship between equal private actors?
   II. Agency: (a) E's own declaration of intent; (b) publicity — acting in GmbH's name; (c) power of representation.

**III. Application.**

*Requirement I.* GmbH, though created through a state registration process, is itself a private legal person under Section 13 GmbHG that deals with the supplier on ordinary commercial terms — no sovereign authority or superordination is exercised by either side. Consequently, this is a private-law relationship notwithstanding GmbH's origin in a registration procedure.

*Requirement II(a).* E made E's own declaration of intent (placing the order), not merely transmitting someone else's already-fixed declaration — so E acted as an agent, not a messenger.

*Requirement II(b).* Questionable is whether publicity is met. This is supported by the fact that E referenced GmbH's account details and order number when placing the call, which objectively signals to the supplier, under Sections 133, 157 BGB, that the order is made in GmbH's name, not E's own. Against this, one could argue that without an explicit "I am acting for GmbH" statement, publicity fails — however this fails because publicity does not require magic words; it is enough that the circumstances let the recipient recognize the principal. Consequently, publicity is met.

*Requirement II(c).* E is GmbH's purchasing employee acting within E's ordinary purchasing role, which as a matter of internal authorization supplies power of representation for routine purchase orders of this kind (declared authority under Section 167 I BGB), even without a separate formal power-of-attorney document.

**IV. Interim results.** The relationship is private law (requirement I); all three Section 164 I elements — own declaration, publicity, authority — are met (requirement II).

**V. Final answer.** As a result, this is a private-law matter, and the purchase contract binds GmbH directly under Section 164 I BGB; E drops out of the contract as a party.

### Question 17 (Case — integrates Week 01-02 + Week 12-13)

**Facts:** Founders sign notarized articles of association for a new GmbH and pay in the EUR 25,000 minimum share capital. Before the commercial register (Handelsregister) has entered the company, the registry court reviews the application and issues a formal decision refusing registration because the stated company object is described too vaguely to satisfy Section 3 I GmbHG. The founders argue the refusal is just an ordinary private-law contract dispute they can resolve directly with the registry clerk.

**Question:** (a) Is the registry court's refusal decision public law or private law, and (b) does the (refused) GmbH already exist as a full legal person at this stage?

**Model Answer:**

**I. Claim/issue sentence.** (a) The registry court's refusal is public law if the court acts with sovereign authority over the founders rather than as their contractual equal; (b) GmbH's legal personhood depends on whether registration — which is constitutive — has actually occurred.

**II. Requirements (as headings).**
   I. Classification of the registry decision: public or private law?
   II. Is registration constitutive for GmbH legal personality?
   III. Has registration occurred here?

**III. Application.**

*Requirement I.* Questionable is how to classify a registry court's refusal. This is supported by the fact that the registry court is a state body reviewing a formal application against statutory requirements (Section 3 I GmbHG) and issuing a unilateral formal decision (refusal) that the founders did not consent to and cannot simply negotiate around — this is a state authority exercising its statutory review power, not two equal private parties bargaining. Against this, one could argue that because the underlying transaction (forming a company) is itself a private-law act, the registration step must also be private law — however this fails because the classification test looks at the specific relationship in question (founders vis-à-vis the reviewing court), not at the private-law character of the company being formed; the registry court's review-and-decide function is administrative in nature. Consequently, the refusal decision is public law.

*Requirement II.* The course's GmbH-formation sequence (draft articles -> notarize -> pay share capital -> register) treats registration as constitutive under Sections 2, 3, 5, 11 GmbHG — there is no full GmbH legal person before registration.

*Requirement III.* Registration has been refused, not completed, so it has not occurred.

**IV. Interim results.** The refusal is a public-law/administrative matter (requirement I); registration is constitutive (requirement II); registration did not occur here (requirement III).

**V. Final answer.** As a result, the registry court's refusal is a public-law decision, not an ordinary private-law dispute the founders can resolve directly with the clerk; and because registration is constitutive and has not occurred, no full GmbH legal person yet exists — the founders remain, at most, a pre-company entity until a corrected application is registered.

### Question 18 (Case)

**Facts:** A Bavarian state statute regulates trade-fair permits and imposes a stricter licensing requirement than an otherwise-applicable federal statute governing the same permits nationwide. Trade-fair organizer T argues the state rule cannot apply because it is "just a state law," while state officials insist the state rule is more specific to Bavaria's fair circuit and should win.

**Question:** Which rule governs, and by what principle?

**Model Answer:**

**I. Claim/issue sentence.** Whether the federal or Bavarian state statute governs a genuine federal/state conflict is resolved by the hierarchy of norms and Art. 31 GG, not by which rule seems more locally specific.

**II. Requirements (as headings).**
   I. Do both statutes cover the same issue and genuinely conflict?
   II. Which legal level does each statute belong to, and which principle resolves a conflict between those levels?

**III. Application.**

*Requirement I.* Both statutes impose licensing requirements for the same trade-fair permits, and their requirements diverge (stricter vs. less strict) — a genuine conflict on the same issue.

*Requirement II.* Questionable is which rule should be treated as controlling. This is supported by the fact that the federal statute belongs to the federal legal level and the Bavarian statute belongs to the state legal level. State officials' "more specific to Bavaria" argument imports a lex-specialis-style analysis — however this fails because lex specialis resolves conflicts between general and specific rules at the same or overlapping level, not federal/state rank conflicts; a federal/state conflict is a hierarchy-of-norms question, resolved by Art. 31 GG, under which federal law prevails over conflicting state law regardless of how locally tailored the state rule appears. Consequently, the federal statute controls.

**IV. Interim result.** Both statutes conflict on the same issue; the conflict is a hierarchy question, and federal law prevails under Art. 31 GG.

**V. Final answer.** As a result, the federal statute governs. T is right that the state rule cannot displace the federal rule merely by being more locally tailored — this is a hierarchy-of-norms conflict, not lex specialis, and Art. 31 GG settles it in favor of federal law.

## Additional Short-Answer / Theory Question

### Question 19

Name the four sources of law identified in the course material and explain why privately set rules (such as standard business terms) still count as a "source of law" despite not being state-enacted.

**Model Answer:**

1. Sources of law are the different origins from which binding or interpretively relevant legal rules can come, beyond just "the statute book."
2. No single statutory anchor states the list; it is the course's sources-of-law overview, which foreshadows the Standard Business Terms topic later in the course.
3. Requirements/features: (1) written statutory law — BGB, HGB, AktG, GmbHG, and other statutes, the primary source in German civil-law reasoning; (2) unwritten sources — customary law and analogies, filling gaps the written text does not address; (3) case law — court decisions that guide interpretation and predictability even without formal binding force on lower courts; (4) privately set rules — standard business terms and other contractual rules that parties create themselves, but only within the space statutory law allows and subject to legal limits (e.g. content control).
4. Example: a webshop's terms and conditions are a privately set rule that can shape the parties' relationship, but they do not override mandatory statutory law and can themselves be struck down by statutory content-control rules if they unreasonably disadvantage the other party.
5. Trap: do not treat privately set rules as legally equivalent to statutes — they are a source of law only in the narrow sense that they can bind the parties who adopted them, and they always operate subject to, not above, the state-enacted legal framework.

## Additional Case / Legal-Opinion Question

### Question 20 (Case)

**Facts:** The EU adopts a directive requiring all Member States "to achieve, by [a stated deadline], a minimum two-year guarantee period for consumers buying certain digital goods." The transposition deadline passes and the Bundestag has not yet enacted any implementing statute. Consumer C, who bought a defective digital good from Trader T shortly after the deadline passed, asks whether C can already rely on the directive's two-year guarantee even though no German statute implements it yet. T argues that "a directive can never bind anyone until Germany passes its own law."

**Question:** Is T's argument correct?

**Model Answer:**

**I. Claim/issue sentence.** Whether C can rely on the directive's guarantee period depends first on correctly classifying the EU instrument, then on whether the default "no binding effect before transposition" rule is displaced by the exception the course material recognizes for a missed transposition deadline.

**II. Requirements (as headings).**
   I. Classify the EU instrument.
   II. State the default consequence for that classification.
   III. Consider whether the missed-deadline exception changes the default consequence.

**III. Application.**

*Requirement I.* The instrument uses "to achieve, by [date]" language — a goal to be reached within a set time — which is the trigger language for a directive under Art. 288 TFEU, not the "applies directly/in its entirety" language of a regulation. Consequently, this is a directive.

*Requirement II.* Directives set common goals and require Member States to transpose them into national law, leaving discretion about form and method; unlike a regulation, a directive does not, by default, create rules that bind private parties directly before that transposition happens. T's general premise reflects this default rule correctly.

*Requirement III.* Questionable is whether T's argument holds even though the transposition deadline has already passed without action. This is supported by the course material's own qualification that a directive can sometimes have direct effect and grant individual rights under specific conditions — an explicit exception to the "needs national transposition" default, most relevant precisely when a Member State has missed its deadline. Against this, one could argue the exception is irrelevant here because Germany simply has not acted yet, and a directive by definition cannot bind before implementation — however, this fails to account for the course material's express flag that a missed deadline is exactly the scenario in which the exception can be triggered. Consequently, T's blanket claim that "a directive can never bind anyone" before transposition overstates the default rule and ignores the recognized exception.

**IV. Interim results.** The instrument is a directive (requirement I); the default rule is no binding effect on private parties before transposition (requirement II); but the missed-deadline direct-effect exception may displace that default (requirement III), though the course material does not spell out its precise conditions.

**V. Final answer.** As a result, T's argument is too absolute. The general rule is that a directive needs national transposition before it creates rights directly, but the missed-deadline exception the course flags means C's claim cannot be dismissed outright — the exact scope of that exception is not detailed in the materials processed for this course, so on exam day this should be flagged as the recognized trap (do not treat "no transposition yet" as an automatic, exception-free bar) rather than resolved with invented specifics.
