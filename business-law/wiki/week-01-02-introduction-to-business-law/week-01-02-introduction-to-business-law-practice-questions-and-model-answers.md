## Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 1

Explain the decision rule for distinguishing public law from private law, and give one example of each.

**Model Answer:**

1. Public law and private law are the two basic domains of the German legal system.
2. No single statutory section defines the line; it is a classification test.
3. Requirements/features: (1) public law governs relationships involving a public authority acting with sovereign power, in a position of superordination over the other party (e.g. issuing an administrative act); (2) private law governs relationships between parties who face each other as legal equals, based on private autonomy and self-determination; (3) a public body is not automatically in "public law mode" — if it acts as an ordinary market participant (e.g. buying office supplies), the relationship is private law.
4. Example: a building permit refusal is public law (the state exercises sovereign authority over the applicant); a buyer refusing to pay for a delivered machine is private law (both parties stand as legal equals).
5. Trap: the presence of a public authority as a party is not itself decisive — always ask whether that authority is acting *with* sovereign power or merely *as* a market participant.

### Question 2

State the hierarchy of norms in Germany and name the constitutional anchor for the federal/state layer.

**Model Answer:**

1. The hierarchy of norms ranks which legal source controls when two applicable rules conflict.
2. Statutory anchor: Art. 31 GG for the federal/state layer.
3. Requirements/features: (1) EU law (primary and secondary) has primacy where it applies; (2) German federal law, including the Basic Law, parliamentary statutes, and federal regulations, ranks next; (3) German state law, including state constitutions, statutes, and regulations, ranks below federal law and yields to it under Art. 31 GG in case of conflict.
4. Example: if a federal statute and a Bavarian state statute conflict on the same issue, the federal statute prevails.
5. Trap: hierarchy of norms answers a *rank* question (which legal level wins), not a *specificity* question — do not reach for hierarchy language when the real issue is lex specialis between two rules at the same level.

### Question 3

Compare civil-law and common-law systems without falling into the "one side has no courts/no statutes" trap.

**Model Answer (Compare Schema):**

Civil-law systems treat legislation as the foundation: rules are laid down in written statutes and modified through the legislative process, with judges deciding within that written framework; supreme court decisions are not formally binding on lower courts the way common-law precedent is, but they carry strong practical influence because lower courts risk being overruled. Common-law systems give court decisions a stronger, formally binding role, with legal development often proceeding through case law and precedent binding lower courts under stare decisis. Contrast example: German courts are guided by BGB provisions first and case law second in a persuasive rather than strictly binding sense, while an English court applying stare decisis treats a higher court's prior ruling as binding. Exam trap: never claim civil-law systems lack case law or that common-law systems lack statutes — the systems are converging, and the real distinction is about which source has *primary structural authority*, not which source exists at all.

### Question 4

Explain the BGB's bracketing technique and lex specialis, and state how they interact.

**Model Answer:**

The requirements are:
1. The BGB is organized into five books, with Book 1 (Sections 1-240) containing a General Part.
2. The bracketing technique means Book 1's general rules apply across the later, more specific books (Book 2 Obligations, Book 3 Property, Book 4 Family, Book 5 Succession) unless a more specific provision modifies or displaces them.
3. Lex specialis means that where two provisions cover the same issue, the more specific rule takes precedence over the more general one.

If these are met, the consequence is that solving a contract-law problem typically requires reading more than one section: a specific rule (e.g. a sales-contract provision in Book 2) together with a general rule that brackets it (e.g. a declaration-of-intent rule in Book 1), with lex specialis resolving any direct conflict in favor of the more specific text.

The reason this rule exists is to let the legislator state a principle once (in the General Part) instead of repeating it in every specific chapter, while still allowing specialized areas (like HGB commercial rules) to override the general BGB baseline where business practice requires a different result.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 5 (Case — integrates Week 01-02 + Week 03)

**Facts:** Handels-GmbH, a wholesale merchant, publishes a printed catalog listing "Model X industrial shelving unit — EUR 450, while stocks last" and mails it to regular business customers. Customer K reads the catalog and sends a signed order form back stating "I hereby order one Model X shelving unit at EUR 450." Handels-GmbH's warehouse manager, noticing the catalog listed an outdated price due to a printing error, refuses to ship the unit at that price and instead demands EUR 600. K insists a contract was already concluded at EUR 450 and threatens to sue.

**Question:** Was a valid purchase contract concluded between K and Handels-GmbH at EUR 450?

**Model Answer:**

**I. Claim/issue sentence.** K and Handels-GmbH concluded a valid purchase contract under Section 433 BGB if there are two matching declarations of intent — offer and acceptance — and no validity obstacle applies.

**II. Requirements (as headings).**
   I. An offer.
   II. A matching acceptance.
   III. No obstacle to formation.

**III. Application.**

*Requirement I — offer.* Questionable is whether the catalog itself was an offer capable of being accepted. This is supported by the fact that the catalog names a specific product and a specific price. Against this, one could argue that a printed catalog sent to an open group of business customers is, under Sections 133, 157 BGB (objective recipient horizon), typically read as an invitation to make an offer (invitatio ad offerendum) rather than a binding offer, because Handels-GmbH cannot reasonably intend to bind itself to every recipient regardless of stock or pricing accuracy — the "while stocks last" qualifier reinforces that the sender did not intend unconditional bound-ness under Section 145 BGB. Consequently, the catalog is not itself an offer; K's signed order form is the offer instead.

*Requirement II — matching acceptance.* Questionable is whether Handels-GmbH accepted K's offer. Handels-GmbH's warehouse manager expressly refused to ship at EUR 450 and instead demanded EUR 600, which is not a matching declaration but a rejection combined with a new offer under Section 150 II BGB (an "acceptance" that changes the price is legally a counteroffer). Consequently, there is no acceptance of K's original EUR 450 offer.

*Requirement III — no obstacle.* Since no acceptance occurred, this requirement is not reached; there is no need to test Sections 125, 134, or 138 here because the contract never got past the offer/acceptance stage at the EUR 450 price.

**IV. Interim results.** No binding offer at EUR 450 came from Handels-GmbH's catalog (requirement I fails as to the catalog, but K's order counts as an offer). No acceptance of K's EUR 450 offer occurred (requirement II fails); Handels-GmbH's response is instead a new counteroffer at EUR 600.

**V. Final answer.** As a result, no valid purchase contract was concluded between K and Handels-GmbH at EUR 450. K's order was an offer that Handels-GmbH rejected while making a new counteroffer of EUR 600 under Section 150 II BGB; a contract will only arise if K now accepts that EUR 600 counteroffer.

### Question 6 (Case)

**Facts:** A regional government agency issues a formal order requiring a chemical plant to halt production for 48 hours after an environmental inspection reveals a safety violation. The plant's operator wants to know whether it can challenge the order using ordinary private-law contract remedies, since the agency "did not ask for the plant's consent."

**Question:** Is the relationship between the agency and the plant operator governed by public law or private law, and what follows from that classification?

**Model Answer:**

**I. Claim/issue sentence.** The plant operator's dispute with the agency is governed by public law if the agency acted with sovereign authority in a position of superordination over the plant, rather than as an equal private party.

**II. Requirements (as headings).**
   I. Is a public authority involved?
   II. Did that authority act with sovereign power (superordination/subordination), or merely as an ordinary market participant?

**III. Application.**

*Requirement I.* The order was issued by a regional government agency following an environmental inspection — a public authority is clearly involved.

*Requirement II.* Questionable is whether the agency acted with sovereign authority. This is supported by the nature of the order itself: a unilateral, binding production-halt order issued after a regulatory inspection is a classic administrative act, comparable to a building-permit decision or a regulatory closure order named in the course materials. Against this, one could argue that any government body dealing with a private company is automatically in private law — however, this argument fails because the presence of a public party is not itself decisive; what matters is whether the state exercises sovereign power over the other party rather than negotiating with it as an equal. Here, the agency did not seek the plant's consent and imposed the halt unilaterally, which is the hallmark of superordination, not equal-party negotiation.

**IV. Interim result.** Both requirements are met: a public authority is involved, and it acted with sovereign power.

**V. Final answer.** As a result, the relationship is governed by public law (administrative law), not private law. The plant operator cannot use ordinary private-law contract remedies (such as claims under the BGB) to challenge the order; it must instead pursue the public-law/administrative-law avenues for contesting an administrative act.

### Question 7 (Case)

**Facts:** An EU regulation on food-labeling standards enters into force, stating that all packaged food sold in the Union must display nutrition information in a specified uniform format "directly and in its entirety" from a given date, without requiring any further national implementing legislation. A German food producer argues it can ignore the rule until the Bundestag passes a corresponding German statute.

**Question:** Is the producer correct that no action is required until German legislation is passed?

**Model Answer:**

**I. Claim/issue sentence.** The producer's argument only succeeds if the EU food-labeling rule is a directive requiring national transposition rather than a regulation that applies directly.

**II. Requirements (as headings).**
   I. Classify the EU instrument (regulation or directive).
   II. State the legal consequence that follows from that classification.

**III. Application.**

*Requirement I.* Questionable is whether the instrument is a regulation or a directive. This is supported by the wording used: the rule states it applies "directly and in its entirety," which is the defining language of a regulation under Art. 288 TFEU — a regulation is directly applicable in Member States, binding in its entirety, and creates uniform rules without needing national transposition. Against this, one could argue any EU food-standards rule "sounds like" a directive because harmonization topics are often addressed by directives — however, this argument fails because the decisive criterion is the stated mode of application (direct and entire), not the subject matter. A directive instead sets common goals and requires Member States to transpose it into national law, leaving discretion about form and method. Consequently, this is a regulation.

*Requirement II.* Since the instrument is a regulation, no German implementing statute is needed for it to bind the producer; it takes effect directly in German law once it enters into force under EU law.

**IV. Interim results.** The instrument is a regulation, not a directive (requirement I). Its consequence is direct application without waiting for national legislation (requirement II).

**V. Final answer.** As a result, the producer is not correct. The regulation binds the producer directly and in its entirety from the stated date regardless of whether the Bundestag ever passes a corresponding statute; that "wait for national legislation" logic only applies to directives.

### Question 8 (Case)

**Facts:** A dispute arises over whether a rule in the General Part of the BGB (Book 1) or a more specific rule found in the HGB commercial-law statute governs a sale between two merchants. Student S argues that because the BGB is the "main" civil code and the HGB is a "side" statute, the BGB rule must always control.

**Question:** Is S's reasoning correct, and which principle actually resolves the conflict?

**Model Answer:**

**I. Claim/issue sentence.** Whether the BGB rule or the HGB rule controls is resolved by lex specialis, not by which statute is considered "more central" to German civil law.

**II. Requirements (as headings).**
   I. Do both rules cover the same issue?
   II. Is one of the two rules more specific to the facts at hand?

**III. Application.**

*Requirement I.* The facts state that both a general BGB sales-related rule and a specific HGB merchant rule appear to address the same transaction — a sale between two merchants — so both rules are in principle applicable to the same issue.

*Requirement II.* Questionable is whether the HGB rule is more specific. This is supported by the course's bracketing/lex-specialis method: the HGB layers business-specific rules on top of the general BGB baseline precisely for actors and transactions involving merchants, which is a narrower and more tailored category of facts than the BGB's general civil-law rule. Against this, one could argue that the BGB's status as the foundational civil code should give it automatic priority — however, this argument fails because rank/prominence of a statute is not the criterion; lex specialis asks only which rule is drafted to cover the narrower, more specific factual situation, and a merchant-to-merchant sale is exactly that situation for the HGB provision. Consequently, the HGB rule is the more specific rule and displaces the general BGB rule on this point.

**IV. Interim result.** Both rules cover the same issue, and the HGB rule is the more specific one.

**V. Final answer.** As a result, S's reasoning is incorrect. The applicable principle is lex specialis, not statutory "seniority," and the more specific HGB merchant rule controls over the general BGB rule for this sale between two merchants.

## Quick Answer Route Summary

| Question # | Primary Route/Section | One-line takeaway |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Public vs private law test | Sovereign authority vs. equal private actors — the public party's role, not its presence, decides. |
| 2 | Art. 31 GG hierarchy | EU law > federal law > state law; hierarchy is a rank question, not a specificity question. |
| 3 | Civil law vs common law | Statutes-as-foundation vs. precedent-as-foundation; both systems have courts and statutes. |
| 4 | BGB bracketing + lex specialis | General Part brackets specific books; specific rule wins a same-level conflict. |
| 5 | Sections 145, 150 II, 133/157 BGB | Catalog = invitatio; K's order = offer; price-changed reply = counteroffer, not acceptance. |
| 6 | Public vs private law classification | Unilateral, non-consensual order from a public body = public law, not a contract dispute. |
| 7 | Art. 288 TFEU regulation vs directive | "Direct and entire" wording = regulation; no national legislation needed. |
| 8 | Lex specialis | HGB merchant rule (specific) beats general BGB rule on the same issue. |
