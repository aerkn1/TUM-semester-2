## Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 1

What makes a person or entity a merchant (Kaufmann) under the HGB, and why does this classification matter for a business-law exam case?

**Model Answer:**
1. A merchant is a person or entity carrying on a commercial business (Section 1 I HGB), or treated as a merchant through voluntary registration (Section 2 HGB) or automatically by legal form (Section 6 I HGB, Section 13 III GmbHG).
2. Statutory anchor: Sections 1, 2, 6 HGB; Section 13 III GmbHG.
3. Requirements/features: (1) Section 1 I HGB — actually carrying on a commercially organized business, presumed unless the type/scope makes commercial organization unnecessary; (2) Section 2 HGB — a smaller business becomes a merchant only by opting into registration; (3) Section 6 I HGB / Section 13 III GmbHG — certain legal forms (GmbH, AG) are merchants automatically, regardless of actual business scale.
4. Example: a one-person online retailer running an organized accounting/logistics operation is a merchant under Section 1 I HGB without ever registering; a small part-time craft seller only becomes one by registering under Section 2 HGB; a freshly founded GmbH is a merchant from the start under Section 6 I HGB even before it makes its first sale.
5. Consequence/trap: merchant status is the gateway to the entire HGB layer — Prokura, Section 15 publicity, the Section 362/confirmation-letter silence exceptions, and the Section 377 notice duty all presuppose it. "The actor is a company" is not itself an answer — name the specific gateway (Section 1, 2, or 6 HGB).

### Question 2

Compare Prokura and Handlungsvollmacht.

**Model Answer:**
Prokura triggers when the merchant (or an organ authorized to represent the merchant) expressly grants it, anchored in Sections 48-53 HGB, and leads to a very broad, legally fixed scope covering nearly all business acts (Section 49 I HGB), with internal restrictions generally ineffective against third parties (Section 50 HGB).

Handlungsvollmacht triggers when a merchant puts someone in charge of a kind of business or specific acts without granting full Prokura, anchored in Section 54 HGB, and leads to authority scoped to what that kind of business or transaction usually entails — excluding loan-taking and court representation unless separately authorized (Section 54 II HGB) — where individually known or should-have-known limits can bind externally.

Contrast example: a Prokurist can validly sign a EUR 50,000 supply contract even against an unregistered internal EUR 20,000 cap, because a third party who doesn't know of the cap isn't bound by it; an employee with Handlungsvollmacht subject to a supplier-known EUR 20,000 cap cannot.

Exam warning: do not treat every "authorized signatory" as a Prokurist — only an actual express grant under Section 48 HGB creates Prokura; anything narrower defaults to the Handlungsvollmacht analysis instead.

### Question 3

State the effect of Section 15 I, II, and III HGB in one sentence each.

**Model Answer:**
The requirements/effects are:
1. Section 15 I HGB: if a fact requiring registration is true but was never registered, a good-faith third party may rely on the register's silence, and the merchant generally cannot assert the unregistered true fact against them.
2. Section 15 II HGB: if a fact requiring registration is true, registered, and published, it can be asserted against third parties regardless of their actual knowledge.
3. Section 15 III HGB: if the register wrongly shows a fact that is not actually true, a good-faith third party may rely on that wrong published content.

If these are met, the consequence is that the register's public face — rather than the underlying truth — controls what a good-faith third party can rely on.

This exists because the commercial register is meant to be the reliable single source of truth for legal traffic, sparing third parties from independently investigating every counterpart's true legal status.

### Question 4

List the requirements for Section 377 HGB to exclude a buyer's warranty rights.

**Model Answer:**
The requirements are:
1. A mutual commercial purchase under Section 343 HGB (both parties act as merchants within their businesses).
2. Delivery of the goods to the buyer or a third party the buyer designated.
3. A material defect under Section 434 BGB present in the delivered goods.
4. The buyer's failure to inspect within due course and notify the defect without undue delay — immediately upon discovery for defects not recognizable on ordinary inspection.
5. No fraudulent concealment of the defect by the seller.

If these are met, the consequence is that the goods are deemed approved and the buyer's warranty rights under Section 437 BGB are excluded for that defect.

This exists to give commercial sellers fast certainty about whether a transaction is closed, reflecting the higher professionalism expected of merchants compared to ordinary BGB buyers.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 5 (Case)

**Facts:** König Elektro GmbH grants Prokura to its purchasing manager, Frau Berger, registered in the commercial register. Internally, the managing director instructs Berger never to sign contracts above EUR 30,000 without his prior sign-off. Needing components urgently, Berger signs a EUR 45,000 supply contract with Kupfer AG on König Elektro GmbH's behalf, signing "ppa. Berger." Kupfer AG's sales representative has never heard of any internal limit and had no reason to suspect one. König Elektro GmbH later refuses to pay, citing the internal EUR 30,000 cap.

**Question:** Does Kupfer AG have a claim against König Elektro GmbH for payment of EUR 45,000 under Section 433 II BGB?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: Kupfer AG could have a claim against König Elektro GmbH for payment of EUR 45,000 under Section 433 II BGB if a valid purchase contract was concluded and binds the GmbH.

II. Requirements:
- I. Merchant status of König Elektro GmbH
- II. Contract conclusion between Berger (for the GmbH) and Kupfer AG
- III. Effective representation of König Elektro GmbH by Berger
- IV. No effective internal limitation defeating that representation
- V. Legal consequence

III. Application:
- I. König Elektro GmbH is a merchant by legal form under Section 6 I HGB / Section 13 III GmbHG, regardless of its actual business scale — the merchant gateway is met without the Section 1 scale test.
- II. Berger and Kupfer AG's representative exchanged a matching offer and acceptance for the EUR 45,000 supply contract.
- III. Questionable is whether Berger's declaration binds König Elektro GmbH. This is supported by the fact that Berger holds registered Prokura under Section 48 HGB, which under Section 49 I HGB covers nearly all business acts a commercial enterprise entails, including ordinary supply purchases — she acted with her own declaration and signed "ppa.," satisfying the Section 164 I BGB publicity element.
- IV. Against binding the GmbH, one could argue the internal EUR 30,000 cap should control. However, under Section 50 I HGB, internal restrictions on Prokura are ineffective against third parties unless the third party knew of the limitation or its breach was obvious. Kupfer AG's representative had no such knowledge and nothing in the facts made a limit obvious. Consequently, the internal limit does not defeat Berger's external authority.

IV. Interim results: merchant status met; contract concluded; representation effective despite the internal cap.

V. Final answer: As a result, Kupfer AG can claim payment of EUR 45,000 from König Elektro GmbH under Section 433 II BGB; the internal EUR 30,000 limit only gives the GmbH an internal claim against Berger, not a defense against Kupfer AG.

### Question 6 (Case)

**Facts:** Herr Novak revokes the Prokura of his employee, Herr Adler, after a dispute, but forgets to have the revocation entered in the commercial register. Two months later, Adler — still holding the company stamp — signs a EUR 12,000 purchase contract "ppa." with Fenster GmbH, whose purchasing agent checked the commercial register beforehand and saw Adler listed as Prokurist with no revocation noted. Novak now refuses to honor the contract, telling Fenster GmbH that Adler's Prokura had already been revoked.

**Question:** Can Fenster GmbH hold Novak's business bound to the EUR 12,000 contract?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: Fenster GmbH could have a claim against Novak's business for performance of the EUR 12,000 contract under Section 433 I BGB if the contract concluded through Adler binds the business despite the (unregistered) revocation of his Prokura.

II. Requirements:
- I. Novak's business is a merchant and the revocation is a registrable fact
- II. The revocation is true
- III. The fact was not registered
- IV. Fenster GmbH acted in good faith
- V. Legal consequence under Section 15 I HGB

III. Application:
- I. Novak's business is a merchant, so the grant and revocation of Prokura are facts Section 53 HGB requires to be registered.
- II. The revocation is true — Adler's Prokura had in fact ended.
- III. Questionable is whether the revocation can be asserted against Fenster GmbH. Against this, one could argue a principal should always be free to end an agent's authority effective immediately. However, Novak never had the revocation entered in the register, so the true fact remained unregistered.
- IV. This is supported by Fenster GmbH's agent having checked the register beforehand and found nothing indicating revocation, with no other reason to doubt Adler's status — satisfying good faith.

IV. Interim results: unregistered true fact confirmed; good faith confirmed; Section 15 I HGB protection applies.

V. Final answer: As a result, under Section 15 I HGB, Novak cannot assert the true but unregistered revocation against a good-faith third party who relied on the register's silence — Fenster GmbH can hold Novak's business bound to the EUR 12,000 contract as if Adler's Prokura were still valid.

### Question 7 (Case)

**Facts:** Merchant seller Dynamo GmbH delivers 50 industrial pumps to merchant buyer Kraft OHG on 3 March. The pumps have a visible manufacturing defect (cracked housings) that would be apparent on a routine visual check. Kraft OHG's warehouse staff sign for delivery but do not inspect the shipment until 20 March, when they notice the cracks and notify Dynamo GmbH the same day. Kraft OHG demands replacement under Section 439 BGB. Dynamo GmbH refuses, citing Section 377 HGB.

**Question:** Can Kraft OHG demand replacement of the pumps under Section 439 BGB?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: Kraft OHG could have a claim against Dynamo GmbH for replacement of the pumps under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB if the pumps are defective and Kraft OHG's warranty rights were not excluded under Section 377 HGB.

II. Requirements:
- I. Mutual commercial purchase (Section 343 HGB)
- II. Delivery
- III. Material defect (Section 434 BGB)
- IV. Timely inspection and notice under Section 377 HGB
- V. Legal consequence

III. Application:
- I. Both Dynamo GmbH and Kraft OHG are merchants acting within their businesses, so this is a mutual commercial purchase under Section 343 HGB.
- II. Delivery of the 50 pumps occurred on 3 March.
- III. The cracked housings are a material defect under Section 434 BGB — the pumps lack the quality customary for defect-free industrial pumps.
- IV. Questionable is whether Kraft OHG met its Section 377 HGB duty. This is supported by notice being given the same day the defect was actually noticed. However, the defect was recognizable on a routine inspection that should have occurred shortly after the 3 March delivery, not 17 days later. Against Kraft OHG, one could argue warehouse workload excuses the delay, but Section 377 HGB expects inspection without undue delay after delivery for defects recognizable on ordinary inspection, and a 17-day gap does not meet that standard here.

IV. Interim results: mutual commercial purchase confirmed; defect confirmed; Section 377 HGB duty breached.

V. Final answer: As a result, the pumps are deemed approved under Section 377 HGB, Kraft OHG's warranty rights are excluded, and Kraft OHG cannot demand replacement under Section 439 BGB despite the pumps being objectively defective.

### Question 8 (Case — integrates Week 11 + Week 12-13)

**Facts:** Rapid Logistik GmbH & Co. KG needs a new forklift. The KG's general partner (Komplementär) is Rapid Verwaltungs GmbH, whose sole managing director, Herr Thies, has granted Prokura to the KG's logistics manager, Frau Voss, registered in the commercial register. Voss negotiates and signs a EUR 28,000 forklift purchase contract with Gabelstapler AG, signing "ppa. Voss for Rapid Logistik GmbH & Co. KG." Gabelstapler AG later learns that Thies never personally countersigned this specific contract and questions whether the KG is bound at all, given the layered GmbH & Co. KG structure.

**Question:** Is Rapid Logistik GmbH & Co. KG bound to pay EUR 28,000 to Gabelstapler AG under Section 433 II BGB?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: Gabelstapler AG could have a claim against Rapid Logistik GmbH & Co. KG for payment of EUR 28,000 under Section 433 II BGB if a valid purchase contract was concluded and binds the KG through Voss's Prokura.

II. Requirements:
- I. Merchant status of the KG
- II. Contract conclusion
- III. Effective grant of Prokura within the layered GmbH & Co. KG structure
- IV. Effective representation by Voss under that Prokura
- V. Legal consequence

III. Application:
- I. A KG carrying on a commercial business is a merchant under Sections 1, 6 HGB in conjunction with Sections 105 ff., 161 HGB. The GmbH & Co. KG structure does not change this — the KG itself is the merchant and contracting party, with Rapid Verwaltungs GmbH acting only as its general partner.
- II. Voss and Gabelstapler AG's representative reached agreement on the EUR 28,000 forklift purchase — a valid offer and acceptance exist.
- III. Questionable is whether Voss's Prokura was validly granted. This is supported by the fact that Prokura for a KG must be granted by whoever is entitled to represent the KG — here, the general partner Rapid Verwaltungs GmbH, itself represented by its managing director Thies under Section 35 GmbHG. Thies, acting for the GmbH in its capacity as general partner, validly granted Voss Prokura, registered under Section 53 HGB. Against this, one could argue Thies's absence from the specific forklift contract matters. However, that misreads the structure: Thies's involvement was only required at the earlier stage of granting Prokura to Voss, not for each individual transaction she later concludes under it.
- IV. Once validly granted, Voss's Prokura carries the broad statutory scope of Section 49 I HGB, covering an ordinary equipment purchase for logistics operations; she signed "ppa." for the KG, satisfying the Section 164 I BGB publicity element, and nothing in the facts suggests any known or obvious internal limitation.

IV. Interim results: KG is a merchant; contract concluded; Prokura validly granted through the general-partner GmbH's managing director; representation by Voss effective.

V. Final answer: As a result, Rapid Logistik GmbH & Co. KG is bound to pay EUR 28,000 to Gabelstapler AG under Section 433 II BGB. The layered GmbH & Co. KG structure only changes who is competent to grant Prokura (the general partner's managing director) — it does not require that person's personal involvement in every transaction the Prokurist later undertakes.

### Question 9

State the requirements for shop assistant authority under Section 56 HGB.

**Model Answer:**
1. Section 56 HGB protects a good-faith customer who buys or pays at a shop/warehouse open to the public, dealt with by staff who appear authorized to sell.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 56 HGB.
3. Requirements/features: (1) a shop or warehouse open to the public; (2) staff employed there; (3) the transaction is a sale or receipt of payment of the kind customarily conducted at that location; (4) the customer's good faith that the staff member is authorized.
4. Example: a customer buying a lamp from a sales assistant at a lighting store is protected even if that assistant was never individually authorized; the same assistant "purchasing" inventory from a supplier is not covered, since Section 56 only protects sales and receipts, not purchases by the clerk.
5. Trap: Section 56 HGB is not a general authority provision — it is limited to sales and receipts made at the shop's public-facing premises; do not stretch it to cover a staff member's purchasing decisions or off-site conduct.

### Question 10

Compare the Section 362 HGB silence exception and the commercial letter of confirmation.

**Model Answer (Compare Schema):**

Section 362 HGB triggers when a merchant whose business includes conducting business for others receives an offer from someone with whom the merchant has an existing business relationship, anchored in Section 362 HGB, and leads to silence being treated as acceptance if the merchant does not reply without delay. The commercial letter of confirmation triggers when merchants who negotiated a contract exchange a letter that memorializes (and may slightly deviate from) the agreed terms, recognized through commercial custom rather than a single fixed section, and leads to silence after receipt binding the recipient to the letter's content — but only if the sender acted in good faith and could reasonably expect the (possibly deviating) content to be accepted. Contrast example: a forwarding agent who regularly handles a client's shipments and receives a new shipping request without responding is bound under Section 362 HGB by pure silence; two merchants who negotiated a bulk order and then received a follow-up letter stating quantities are bound by the letter's content only if the sender did not knowingly misstate the terms (see the 500 vs. 250 light bulbs bad-faith example). Exam trap: do not treat these as the same mechanism — Section 362 HGB needs an existing business relationship and a usual-business offer with no immediate answer, whereas the letter of confirmation needs prior negotiation and turns on the sender's good faith about the letter's content, not on any ongoing relationship.

### Question 11

Explain the difference between declaratory and constitutive commercial-register entries.

**Model Answer:**
1. This distinguishes whether commercial-register entry merely publishes an existing legal fact or actually creates the legal fact itself.
2. No single fixed section states the label; it is a doctrinal classification used throughout the trade-law material.
3. Requirements/features: (1) declaratory registration — the underlying legal fact (e.g. merchant status under Section 1 HGB, or Prokura conferral under Section 48 HGB) already exists once its real-world requirements are met, and registration only publicizes it, feeding into the Section 15 HGB good-faith rules; (2) constitutive registration — the legal fact does not exist at all until registration occurs (e.g. a GmbH or AG is not legally formed until entered in the register).
4. Example: a sole trader who meets Section 1 I HGB's commercial-scale test is already a merchant before ever registering — registration is declaratory; a founder who has signed a GmbH's articles and paid in capital does not yet have a GmbH until the register entry is made — registration is constitutive.
5. Trap: do not assume all commercial-register entries work the same way — ask whether the entry announces a fact already true (declaratory, then run Section 15 HGB) or is the very event bringing the fact into legal existence (constitutive, where no Section 15 HGB good-faith analysis is needed for the fact's existence itself).

### Question 12

Compare the ordinary BGB diligence standard with the prudent-merchant standard under Section 347 I HGB.

**Model Answer (Compare Schema):**

The ordinary BGB diligence standard triggers for any obligor generally, anchored in Section 276 II BGB, and leads to a duty of care measured by what is customarily required in dealings, without any merchant-specific elevation. The prudent-merchant standard triggers specifically for a merchant's commercial transactions, anchored in Section 347 I HGB, and leads to a heightened diligence standard reflecting the professionalism expected of someone who conducts business as their trade. Contrast example: a private individual who negligently damages a rented item is measured against ordinary Section 276 II BGB care; a merchant storing a counterparty's goods pending delivery is held to the higher prudent-merchant standard for how those goods are handled. Exam trap: do not import Section 347 HGB's heightened standard into a case where at least one side is not acting as a merchant within their business — apply Section 276 II BGB in the ordinary case, and elevate to Section 347 I HGB only once merchant status and a commercial-transaction context are both confirmed.

### Question 13

Explain the abuse-of-authority exception to Prokura's broad external scope.

**Model Answer:**
1. This is the narrow carve-out under which a third party's reliance on a Prokurist's broad statutory authority can still fail despite Section 50 HGB.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 50 HGB, with the abuse exception developed alongside it.
3. Requirements/features: (1) the Prokurist acts outside an internal restriction; (2) ordinarily Section 50 HGB makes that internal restriction ineffective against a third party; (3) the exception applies only if the third party actually knew of the internal-limit breach, or the abuse was so obvious that good faith cannot be assumed.
4. Example: a Prokurist secretly colluding with a buyer to sign a contract both know violates an internal cap defeats the buyer's reliance; a buyer who simply had no way of knowing about an unregistered internal cap remains protected even if, in hindsight, the deal looks unusually large.
5. Trap: do not treat "the deal looks like a bad business decision" as automatic proof of obvious abuse — the exception requires actual knowledge or a genuinely obvious abuse from the third party's own vantage point, not mere hindsight unusualness of the transaction.

### Question 14 (Case)

**Facts:** Spedition Nord GmbH is a freight-forwarding merchant that has handled Möbelhaus Bayer's shipments every month for two years under an ongoing arrangement. On 5 June, Möbelhaus Bayer sends Spedition Nord GmbH a routine shipping request matching its usual pattern. Spedition Nord GmbH's dispatcher receives the email but, due to a staffing shortage, never replies or acts, and the delivery deadline passes. Möbelhaus Bayer claims a binding forwarding contract was breached; Spedition Nord GmbH claims no contract ever formed because it never accepted.

**Question:** Did a binding contract form between Möbelhaus Bayer and Spedition Nord GmbH despite Spedition Nord GmbH's silence?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: Möbelhaus Bayer could have a contract claim against Spedition Nord GmbH if Spedition Nord GmbH's silence counts as acceptance under Section 362 HGB.

II. Requirements:
- I. Spedition Nord GmbH's business includes conducting business for others
- II. An existing business relationship with Möbelhaus Bayer
- III. Receipt of an offer within the usual business
- IV. No reply without delay
- V. Legal consequence

III. Application:
- I. Spedition Nord GmbH is a freight forwarder, a business whose core activity is conducting shipping business for other parties — satisfying the threshold Section 362 HGB business type.
- II. Questionable is whether an existing business relationship exists. This is supported by the two-year pattern of monthly shipments between the same two parties, which is exactly the kind of established relationship Section 362 HGB requires — not a one-off contact between strangers.
- III. The 5 June shipping request matches Spedition Nord GmbH's usual business and the established pattern, so it is an offer within the meaning of Section 362 HGB.
- IV. Against forming a contract, one could argue that a staffing shortage excuses the lack of a reply. However, Section 362 HGB does not require fault for silence to count as acceptance — it only requires that no timely reply was sent, and none was here.

IV. Interim results: business type met; existing relationship met; offer within the usual business met; no timely reply given.

V. Final answer: As a result, Spedition Nord GmbH's silence is deemed acceptance under Section 362 HGB, and a binding forwarding contract formed on the terms of Möbelhaus Bayer's 5 June request despite Spedition Nord GmbH never actively responding.

### Question 15 (Case — integrates Week 11 + Week 07)

**Facts:** Herr Krause has worked for years at Baustoffe Halle GmbH, repeatedly signing supply contracts "im Auftrag" on company letterhead with the company stamp, in full view of the managing director, who knew of this practice and never objected — even though Krause was never granted Prokura or any formal Vollmacht. A new supplier, Ziegel AG, reasonably relies on this consistent pattern and Krause's use of company materials to sign a EUR 15,000 supply deal. Baustoffe Halle GmbH later refuses to pay, stating Krause was never authorized to represent it at all.

**Question:** Is Baustoffe Halle GmbH bound to the EUR 15,000 contract with Ziegel AG?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: Ziegel AG could have a claim against Baustoffe Halle GmbH under Section 433 II BGB if Krause had power of representation despite never being formally granted Prokura or Vollmacht.

II. Requirements:
- I. Own declaration by Krause
- II. Publicity (acting in the GmbH's name)
- III. Power of representation
- IV. Legal consequence

III. Application:
- I. Krause made his own declaration when signing the supply contract, rather than merely transmitting someone else's fixed declaration — this is agency, not a messenger scenario.
- II. Krause signed "im Auftrag" on company letterhead with the company stamp, clearly showing he acted in Baustoffe Halle GmbH's name — publicity is met.
- III. Questionable is whether Krause had power of representation, since no Prokura was ever registered under Section 48 HGB and no explicit Vollmacht was granted under Section 167 I BGB. This is supported by the reliance-based (agency-by-estoppel) route: the managing director actually knew of Krause's repeated pattern of signing contracts this way and tolerated it over time — the conscious-tolerance fact pattern that distinguishes estoppel from ostensible agency's negligent-failure-to-notice version. Against granting Krause authority, one could argue the absence of any formal Prokura or Vollmacht should end the analysis. However, that would ignore that reliance-based authority exists specifically to protect a third party who reasonably relied on a pattern the principal itself allowed to continue, and Ziegel AG's reliance on the repeated conduct and company materials was in good faith.

IV. Interim results: own declaration and publicity met; power of representation established through agency by estoppel given the managing director's knowing tolerance of the pattern.

V. Final answer: As a result, Baustoffe Halle GmbH is bound to the EUR 15,000 contract under Section 433 II BGB. Krause never held Prokura in the Section 48 HGB sense, but the managing director's knowing tolerance of his repeated authorized-looking conduct created real power of representation through agency by estoppel, on which Ziegel AG was entitled to rely in good faith.

### Question 16 (Case — integrates Week 11 + Week 08)

**Facts:** Präzision Maschinenbau OHG (merchant) sells a batch of CNC-machined parts to Werkzeug Schmidt GmbH (merchant). The parts pass a routine visual and dimensional check at delivery on 10 April. A latent metallurgical flaw only becomes apparent three weeks later when the parts fail under stress testing during Werkzeug Schmidt GmbH's own production run. Werkzeug Schmidt GmbH notifies Präzision Maschinenbau OHG the same day the flaw becomes apparent and demands replacement under Section 439 BGB. Präzision Maschinenbau OHG argues the three-week gap since delivery already excludes the claim under Section 377 HGB.

**Question:** Can Werkzeug Schmidt GmbH still demand replacement under Section 439 BGB?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: Werkzeug Schmidt GmbH could have a claim for replacement under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB if the parts are defective under Section 434 BGB and its warranty rights were not excluded under Section 377 HGB.

II. Requirements:
- I. Mutual commercial purchase (Section 343 HGB)
- II. Delivery
- III. Material defect (Section 434 BGB)
- IV. Timely inspection and notice under Section 377 HGB
- V. Legal consequence

III. Application:
- I. Both parties are merchants acting within their businesses, so this is a mutual commercial purchase under Section 343 HGB.
- II. Delivery occurred on 10 April.
- III. The latent metallurgical flaw is a material defect under Section 434 BGB — the parts lack the quality customary for defect-free CNC parts, even though this was not visible at delivery.
- IV. Questionable is whether Werkzeug Schmidt GmbH met its Section 377 HGB duty despite the three-week gap since delivery. This is supported by the rule that Section 377 HGB only requires prompt inspection and notice for defects recognizable on an ordinary inspection at delivery; for defects not recognizable that way, the duty is only to notify without undue delay once the defect is actually discovered. The routine visual and dimensional check at delivery was the ordinary inspection expected at that stage, and the metallurgical flaw was not something that check could reveal. Against Werkzeug Schmidt GmbH, Präzision Maschinenbau OHG could argue that three weeks is simply too long a gap from delivery. However, the relevant clock for a latent defect runs from discovery, not from delivery, and notice was given the same day the flaw became apparent during stress testing.

IV. Interim results: mutual commercial purchase confirmed; defect confirmed; Section 377 HGB duty satisfied because the defect was latent and notice followed discovery without delay.

V. Final answer: As a result, Werkzeug Schmidt GmbH's warranty rights are not excluded under Section 377 HGB, and it can demand replacement of the parts under Section 439 BGB despite the three-week gap between delivery and notice.

### Question 17 (Case)

**Facts:** Herr Feld's Prokura for Tischlerei Roth GmbH was validly revoked and the revocation was properly filed for registration, but due to a clerical error at the registry itself, the revocation was never actually entered — the register wrongly continues to list Feld as Prokurist. Relying on this entry, Baumarkt Ost checks the register before dealing, sees Feld listed, and signs a EUR 9,000 supply contract with Feld "ppa." Tischlerei Roth GmbH refuses to pay, pointing out Feld's Prokura was genuinely revoked.

**Question:** Is Tischlerei Roth GmbH bound to the EUR 9,000 contract?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: Baumarkt Ost could have a claim against Tischlerei Roth GmbH under Section 433 II BGB if Feld's declaration binds the GmbH despite his actually-revoked Prokura, based on the wrongly published register entry.

II. Requirements:
- I. Merchant status of Tischlerei Roth GmbH and registrability of Prokura
- II. The register wrongly shows Feld as holding Prokura
- III. Baumarkt Ost's good faith
- IV. Legal consequence under Section 15 III HGB

III. Application:
- I. Tischlerei Roth GmbH is a merchant, and Prokura and its revocation are facts Section 53 HGB requires to be registered.
- II. Feld's Prokura was validly revoked, yet due to the registry's own clerical error the revocation was never entered — the register wrongly continues to show Feld as Prokurist, which is the "wrong but published content" fact pattern of Section 15 III HGB, distinct from the mere silence covered by Section 15 I HGB.
- III. Questionable is whether Baumarkt Ost acted in good faith. This is supported by Baumarkt Ost having checked the register beforehand and found Feld listed with no indication of any revocation, with nothing in the facts suggesting it knew or should have known the entry was wrong. Against this, one could argue the registry's own error, rather than the GmbH's conduct, caused the wrong entry, and so the GmbH should not bear the consequence. However, Section 15 III HGB protects good-faith reliance on the register's published content regardless of who caused the inaccuracy — the risk of a wrong register entry is allocated to whoever the register concerns, not to the good-faith third party relying on it.

IV. Interim results: registrable fact confirmed; register wrongly shows Feld as Prokurist; Baumarkt Ost's good faith confirmed; Section 15 III HGB protection applies.

V. Final answer: As a result, under Section 15 III HGB, Tischlerei Roth GmbH cannot assert Feld's true (revoked) status against good-faith Baumarkt Ost, and remains bound to the EUR 9,000 contract as if Feld's Prokura were still valid.

### Question 18 (Case — integrates Week 11 + Week 07)

**Facts:** Herr Bauer holds registered Prokura for Sanitär Vogel GmbH. Without obtaining prior permission from the managing director and without any later ratification, Bauer uses his Prokura to sign a EUR 6,000 purchase contract on behalf of Sanitär Vogel GmbH — buying fittings from his own separately owned hardware business, Bauer Baumarkt e.K., effectively representing both the buyer (Sanitär Vogel GmbH, as Prokurist) and the seller (his own business, as owner) in the same transaction. Sanitär Vogel GmbH later refuses to pay, arguing the contract is invalid.

**Question:** Is Sanitär Vogel GmbH bound to pay Bauer Baumarkt e.K. EUR 6,000 under Section 433 II BGB?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: Bauer Baumarkt e.K. could have a claim against Sanitär Vogel GmbH for payment of EUR 6,000 under Section 433 II BGB if Bauer's self-contracting as Prokurist validly bound Sanitär Vogel GmbH.

II. Requirements:
- I. Contract conclusion via self-contracting
- II. Bauer's Prokura otherwise covers the transaction
- III. No effective exception to the Section 181 BGB self-dealing restriction
- IV. Legal consequence

III. Application:
- I. Bauer, acting for Sanitär Vogel GmbH as Prokurist, and Bauer personally as owner of Bauer Baumarkt e.K., are the same natural person on both sides of the transaction — this is self-contracting in the Section 181 BGB sense.
- II. Bauer's registered Prokura under Section 48 HGB otherwise covers an ordinary supply purchase of this kind under Section 49 I HGB.
- III. Questionable is whether Section 181 BGB defeats the transaction despite Bauer's broad Prokura. This is supported by the fact that self-dealing is analytically stronger than an ordinary internal-instruction breach: because there is no independent counterparty bargaining against Bauer's own interest, the transaction is provisionally invalid unless one of four exceptions applies — prior permission, later ratification, mere performance of an existing obligation, or a transaction advantageous only to Sanitär Vogel GmbH. Against invalidity, one could argue Prokura's broad Section 49 I HGB scope should cover any ordinary supply purchase regardless of counterparty identity. However, none of the four exceptions are met here: no prior permission was given, no later ratification occurred, this was a new purchase rather than performance of an existing obligation, and the facts show an ordinary purchase, not one legally advantageous only to Sanitär Vogel GmbH. Consequently, Section 181 BGB is not displaced and restricts Bauer's external power for this specific transaction.

IV. Interim results: self-contracting confirmed; Prokura otherwise sufficient; no Section 181 BGB exception applies, so the restriction stands.

V. Final answer: As a result, the contract is provisionally invalid under Section 181 BGB, and Sanitär Vogel GmbH is not bound to pay Bauer Baumarkt e.K. EUR 6,000 under Section 433 II BGB unless the GmbH later ratifies the transaction under Section 177 BGB.

## Short-Answer / Theory Questions (continued)

### Question 19

List two additional commercial-contract modifications the HGB makes to BGB defaults, beyond agency scope, register publicity, and the Section 377 HGB notice duty.

**Model Answer:**
1. Beyond agency, register publicity, and Section 377 HGB, two further BGB default rules are adjusted specifically for merchants in ordinary commercial dealings.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 350 HGB; Section 354 I HGB.
3. Requirements/features: (1) Section 350 HGB — a suretyship/guarantee given by a merchant in a commercial transaction does not need the written form Section 766 BGB otherwise requires; (2) Section 354 I HGB — where a merchant renders services in the course of their business, payment is presumed owed even without an explicit price agreement, reversing the ordinary assumption that services might be gratuitous.
4. Example: a merchant who orally guarantees a supplier's invoice is bound despite no signed document, whereas a private individual's oral guarantee for a friend's debt fails Section 766 BGB's form requirement; a freight forwarder who moves a regular client's goods without discussing a fee can still claim payment because Section 354 I HGB presumes merchant services are not free.
5. Trap: do not treat these as "the same idea as Section 377" — they modify different BGB defaults (form requirements and gratuitousness assumptions), not the notice/inspection regime, and each needs its own statutory anchor when cited.

### Question 20

Which businesspeople are usually NOT merchants despite running an active business, and why does this matter for HGB analysis?

**Model Answer:**
1. Even someone clearly "in business" can fall outside HGB merchant status if their activity does not meet the Section 1 I HGB commercial-business threshold or another merchant gateway.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 1 I, II HGB (by exclusion); Section 2 HGB (opt-in for the small-business subset).
3. Requirements/features — categories usually NOT merchants: (1) liberal professions (doctors, lawyers, notaries, auditors, tax consultants, architects, writers, interpreters) — professional/personal-skill-based activity, not a commercially organized business in the Section 1 sense; (2) purely asset-managing activity, e.g. merely renting out apartments — passive asset management, not an operated commercial business; (3) employees — acting under direction, not independently, so they cannot be merchants in their own right; (4) charities — not profit-directed; (5) very small business activities that do not require commercial organization, unless the operator opts in under Section 2 HGB.
4. Example: a solo architect running a design practice with an assistant and steady client billing is not automatically a merchant, however busy the practice looks, because architecture is a liberal profession; a landlord renting out three apartments is not a merchant even though rent is collected regularly, because letting property is asset management, not an operated commercial business.
5. Trap: "runs a business" and "is a merchant" are not synonyms — always test the actual Section 1 I HGB criteria (or the Section 2 opt-in, or the Section 6 legal-form gateway) rather than assuming professional activity or steady income automatically triggers HGB status; a wrong call here breaks every downstream HGB rule (Prokura, Section 15, Section 377), since none of them apply without a merchant.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions (continued, part 2)

### Question 21 (Case)

**Facts:** Merchant Fassbinder Getränke GmbH grants joint Prokura to two employees, Herr Lenz and Frau Wick, registered as "gemeinsame Prokura" under Section 48 II HGB. On Monday, Lenz signs a EUR 40,000 keg-supply contract "ppa." on behalf of the GmbH after discussing and agreeing the deal with Wick by phone; Wick countersigns the same contract document the following Wednesday, two days later, once back in the office. The supplier, Getränke Kraft AG, later argues the contract is invalid because the two Prokurists did not sign at the exact same time.

**Question:** Is Fassbinder Getränke GmbH bound to the EUR 40,000 contract despite the two-day gap between Lenz's and Wick's signatures?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: Getränke Kraft AG could have a claim against Fassbinder Getränke GmbH under Section 433 II BGB if the joint Prokura of Lenz and Wick was validly exercised despite their signatures being two days apart.

II. Requirements:
- I. Merchant status and valid grant of joint Prokura
- II. Both joint Prokurists' involvement in the act
- III. Whether simultaneity is required
- IV. Legal consequence

III. Application:
- I. Fassbinder Getränke GmbH is a merchant by legal form under Section 6 I HGB / Section 13 III GmbHG, and validly granted joint Prokura to Lenz and Wick, registered under Section 48 II HGB in conjunction with Section 53 HGB.
- II. Both Lenz and Wick were substantively involved: Lenz agreed the deal terms with Wick by phone before signing, and Wick then countersigned the same document.
- III. Questionable is whether joint Prokura requires the Prokurists to act at the exact same moment. This is supported by the point of joint Prokura being control through mutual involvement in the decision, not a formal simultaneity requirement — the two Prokurists discussed and agreed the deal together before either signature was finalized, and Wick's countersignature completed the joint act. Against validity, one could argue a two-day gap between signatures shows the two never truly acted "jointly." However, joint Prokura only requires that both Prokurists participate in bringing about the act — sequential signing of the same agreed document, following a shared decision, satisfies this without needing simultaneous physical signing.

IV. Interim results: joint Prokura validly granted and registered; both Prokurists substantively involved in the shared decision and act; simultaneity not required.

V. Final answer: As a result, Fassbinder Getränke GmbH is bound to the EUR 40,000 contract under Section 433 II BGB; joint Prokura under Section 48 II HGB requires joint participation in the act, not simultaneous signing, so the two-day gap does not invalidate Lenz's and Wick's joint exercise of their Prokura.

### Question 22 (Case)

**Facts:** Merchant Ziegel Bau GmbH grants ordinary (non-restricted) Prokura to its site manager, Herr Dorn, registered in the commercial register. Without any additional authorization for real-estate transactions, Dorn signs a contract "ppa." selling a warehouse plot owned by Ziegel Bau GmbH to Grundstück Nord AG for EUR 300,000. Grundstück Nord AG's representative assumed Dorn's registered Prokura was sufficient for any business transaction, including real estate, and did not ask further questions. Ziegel Bau GmbH now refuses to transfer the plot, stating Dorn was never separately authorized for real-estate deals.

**Question:** Is Ziegel Bau GmbH bound to sell the plot to Grundstück Nord AG for EUR 300,000?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: Grundstück Nord AG could have a claim against Ziegel Bau GmbH for conclusion of the sale under Section 433 I BGB if Dorn's Prokura covered the real-estate transaction.

II. Requirements:
- I. Merchant status and valid Prokura grant
- II. Whether Section 49 I HGB's general scope extends to real-estate dispositions
- III. Whether separate authorization under Section 49 II HGB exists
- IV. Legal consequence

III. Application:
- I. Ziegel Bau GmbH is a merchant by legal form and validly granted Dorn registered Prokura under Section 48 HGB.
- II. Questionable is whether Dorn's Prokura, though broad under Section 49 I HGB, extends to selling real estate. This is supported by Section 49 II HGB carving real-estate dispositions out of Prokura's otherwise near-unlimited statutory scope, requiring separate, specific authorization for exactly this category of transaction — unlike the near-blanket coverage Prokura gives for ordinary commercial acts.
- III. Against Grundstück Nord AG's claim, the facts show no additional authorization was ever given to Dorn for real-estate transactions specifically; his registered Prokura alone does not satisfy Section 49 II HGB's separate-empowerment requirement. However, one could argue Grundstück Nord AG relied in good faith on Dorn's registered Prokura status generally. This does not help here, because Section 49 II HGB is a statutory scope limitation built into Prokura itself, not an internal instruction that Section 50 HGB's third-party-protection rule could override — a third party is expected to know that Prokura's statutory scope excludes real-estate dispositions absent separate authorization, unlike an undisclosed internal cap on an otherwise-covered transaction.

IV. Interim results: Prokura validly granted but does not, by itself, extend to real-estate dispositions; no separate Section 49 II HGB authorization was given; the scope gap is not curable by Grundstück Nord AG's good faith.

V. Final answer: As a result, Ziegel Bau GmbH is not bound to sell the plot under Section 433 I BGB; Dorn's ordinary Prokura did not cover the real-estate sale absent the separate authorization Section 49 II HGB requires, and this statutory scope limit is not defeated by a third party's good-faith assumption the way an internal Section 50 HGB restriction would be.

### Question 23 (Case)

**Facts:** Büromöbel Stein GmbH grants its purchasing clerk, Frau Thal, Handlungsvollmacht under Section 54 HGB to order office furniture and supplies on the company's behalf — the kind of business she is put in charge of. Facing a temporary cash-flow gap, Thal — without any special authorization for financing matters — approaches Kredit Schnell AG and signs a EUR 25,000 short-term loan agreement "i.V." for Büromöbel Stein GmbH, intending to use the funds to pay a supplier invoice. Kredit Schnell AG assumed Thal's role as an authorized company representative was enough to bind the GmbH to the loan.

**Question:** Is Büromöbel Stein GmbH bound to repay the EUR 25,000 loan to Kredit Schnell AG?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: Kredit Schnell AG could have a claim against Büromöbel Stein GmbH for repayment of EUR 25,000 under the loan agreement if Thal's Handlungsvollmacht covered concluding the loan.

II. Requirements:
- I. Valid grant of Handlungsvollmacht to Thal
- II. Whether loan-taking falls within Section 54 I HGB's usual-business scope
- III. Whether Section 54 II HGB's carve-out applies and was separately authorized
- IV. Legal consequence

III. Application:
- I. Büromöbel Stein GmbH validly granted Thal Handlungsvollmacht under Section 54 HGB, scoped to ordering office furniture and supplies — the kind of business she was put in charge of.
- II. Questionable is whether taking out a EUR 25,000 loan falls within that scope. This is supported by Section 54 I HGB extending Handlungsvollmacht only to transactions the specific business or transaction type Thal was put in charge of usually entails — ordering furniture and supplies for resale or company use, not arranging outside financing.
- III. Against binding the GmbH, one could argue Thal's general role as an authorized company representative should cover a routine cash-flow loan. However, Section 54 II HGB expressly excludes loan-taking (and court representation) from Handlungsvollmacht's scope unless the holder is specifically authorized for it, and the facts state no such special authorization was given to Thal. Consequently, even Kredit Schnell AG's assumption that Thal's role was broad enough does not help, since Section 54 II HGB is a statutory scope exclusion inherent to Handlungsvollmacht, not merely an internal instruction a third party could reasonably overlook.

IV. Interim results: Handlungsvollmacht validly granted but scoped to furniture/supply purchasing; loan-taking falls outside that scope under Section 54 II HGB; no special authorization for financing was given.

V. Final answer: As a result, Büromöbel Stein GmbH is not bound to repay the EUR 25,000 loan; Thal's Handlungsvollmacht did not extend to loan-taking under Section 54 II HGB absent specific authorization for that category of transaction.

### Question 24 (Case)

**Facts:** Teppich Welt (a merchant-run carpet showroom) employs Frau Oymen as a sales assistant on the showroom floor. Customers and colleagues casually refer to her as having "full authority to run the floor," and she has a business card printed with the title "Authorized Representative." Relying on the title and the showroom setting, a customer, Herr Falk, buys a EUR 4,000 carpet from Oymen during normal business hours, paying by bank transfer to the account she provides — a customary way of completing sales at that showroom. Teppich Welt's owner later discovers Oymen was never granted Prokura or Handlungsvollmacht in any formal sense — she is simply a shop-floor sales employee — and argues the sale is not binding because Oymen lacked "real" authority.

**Question:** Is Teppich Welt bound to the EUR 4,000 carpet sale to Herr Falk?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: Herr Falk could have a claim against Teppich Welt for delivery of the carpet under Section 433 I BGB if Oymen had power of representation for the sale despite lacking Prokura or Handlungsvollmacht.

II. Requirements:
- I. Own declaration and publicity
- II. Whether Prokura or Handlungsvollmacht applies
- III. Whether Section 56 HGB shop assistant authority applies instead
- IV. Legal consequence

III. Application:
- I. Oymen made her own declaration selling the carpet to Falk, and the showroom setting and payment-collection role made clear she acted for Teppich Welt's business — publicity is met.
- II. Questionable is whether Oymen holds Prokura or Handlungsvollmacht. This is not supported by the facts: no express grant under Section 48 HGB was ever made, and there is no showing the owner put her formally "in charge of" a defined kind of business the way Section 54 HGB requires — the business card title and colleagues' casual description are not a substitute for an actual grant of either authority type.
- III. This is supported instead by Section 56 HGB: Oymen is employed in a store open to the public (the showroom), the EUR 4,000 carpet sale and the bank-transfer payment method are transactions customary for that type of showroom, and Falk had no reason to doubt her authority to sell and receive payment on the spot. Against Section 56 HGB applying, one could argue the "Authorized Representative" title suggests something broader than ordinary shop-floor authority. However, an inflated business-card title does not itself grant Prokura or Handlungsvollmacht, and none of their formal grant requirements are met here — the case resolves at the narrower, correctly-matched Section 56 HGB level, which is enough on its own to bind the sale.

IV. Interim results: no Prokura or Handlungsvollmacht established; Section 56 HGB shop assistant authority requirements are met.

V. Final answer: As a result, Teppich Welt is bound to the EUR 4,000 carpet sale to Herr Falk under Section 433 I BGB — not because Oymen held Prokura or Handlungsvollmacht, which she never had, but because Section 56 HGB's narrower shop-assistant authority independently covers exactly this kind of customary showroom sale.

### Question 25 (Case — integrates Week 11 + Week 06)

**Facts:** Stahlwerk Ost GmbH (merchant) and Bau Komplett AG (merchant) conclude a steel-beam supply contract that incorporates Stahlwerk Ost GmbH's pre-printed standard purchase terms, which state: "DAP named destination per Incoterms 2020; Buyer waives any claim for delay damages regardless of cause." Delivery arrives eleven days late due to Stahlwerk Ost GmbH's own scheduling error, causing Bau Komplett AG a EUR 18,000 loss from a stalled construction site. Stahlwerk Ost GmbH points to the delay-damages waiver clause. Bau Komplett AG argues the clause is invalid, but concedes both parties are merchants, so the consumer-protection "black list" cannot apply directly.

**Question:** Can Stahlwerk Ost GmbH rely on the delay-damages waiver clause to defeat Bau Komplett AG's EUR 18,000 damages claim?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: Bau Komplett AG could have a claim against Stahlwerk Ost GmbH for EUR 18,000 in delay damages under Sections 280 I, II, 286 BGB unless the SBT waiver clause validly excludes it.

II. Requirements:
- I. The waiver clause is Stahlwerk Ost GmbH's SBT under Section 305 I BGB and was incorporated
- II. Whether Sections 308/309 BGB apply directly
- III. Whether the clause fails content control under Section 307 BGB via Section 310 I BGB
- IV. Legal consequence

III. Application:
- I. The pre-printed purchase terms, including the DAP/Incoterms reference and the delay-damages waiver, are pre-formulated contract terms Stahlwerk Ost GmbH presents for a multitude of contracts — SBT under Section 305 I BGB — and were validly incorporated into this steel-beam contract; the Incoterms reference is not law by itself and only becomes relevant because it was agreed as a contract term.
- II. Questionable is whether the "black list" under Section 309 BGB, which would ordinarily void a blanket exclusion of liability for a serious breach, applies directly here. This is not supported: both Stahlwerk Ost GmbH and Bau Komplett AG are merchants acting within their businesses, and Section 310 I BGB excludes Sections 308 and 309 BGB from direct application to B2B contracts between entrepreneurs.
- III. This is supported by Section 307 BGB continuing to apply indirectly through Section 310 I BGB even though 308/309 do not apply directly — the clause purports to waive delay damages "regardless of cause," including Stahlwerk Ost GmbH's own scheduling errors, which unreasonably disadvantages Bau Komplett AG contrary to good faith by stripping away the core remedy for the supplier's own fault-based delay. Against invalidity, one could argue commercial parties should be free to allocate delay risk however they choose, especially where Incoterms language signals a sophisticated negotiated framework. However, the clause's own wording — a "regardless of cause" waiver — goes beyond ordinary risk allocation (such as fixing a delivery point under DAP) and instead blanket-excludes liability even for the supplier's own fault, which the Section 307 general clause treats as a core disadvantage even without the mechanical 309 black-list trigger being directly available in B2B.

IV. Interim results: clause is SBT, validly incorporated; Sections 308/309 do not apply directly between merchants; the clause nonetheless fails the Section 307 general clause via Section 310 I BGB because it blanket-excludes fault-based delay liability.

V. Final answer: As a result, Stahlwerk Ost GmbH cannot rely on the delay-damages waiver clause; it is invalid under Section 307 BGB via Section 310 I BGB despite Sections 308/309 not applying directly in this B2B relationship, and Bau Komplett AG's EUR 18,000 damages claim under Sections 280 I, II, 286 BGB survives, with the rest of the contract — including the DAP delivery-point allocation — remaining intact under Section 306 BGB.
