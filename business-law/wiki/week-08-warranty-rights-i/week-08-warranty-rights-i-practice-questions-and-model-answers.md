## Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 1

Explain what "transfer of risk" means in warranty law and why it functions as the decisive timing checkpoint.

**Model Answer:** Transfer of risk is the moment — usually handover/delivery of the thing under Section 446 sentence 1 BGB — that separates general impaired-performance law from warranty law. Before transfer of risk, any performance problem is handled under ordinary contract-breach rules; after it, the buyer bears the risk of accidental deterioration and warranty law governs whether the seller answers for a defect. The requirement matters because the buyer must show the defect already existed at transfer of risk, not merely that it was discovered later — a laptop battery defect present at handover is a seller's problem, but coffee spilled on the laptop after handover is not. The exam trap is conflating transfer of risk with the later cure deadline: transfer of risk decides whether the defect belongs to the seller's warranty responsibility at all, while the cure deadline decides only whether the buyer may escalate from cure to secondary remedies.

### Question 2

Compare material defect and legal defect under German sales law.

**Model Answer:** A material defect (Section 434 BGB) exists when the thing's actual condition deviates from its legally required condition — assessed through subjective agreement, objective baseline expectations, assembly requirements, or aliud (wrong-item) delivery — and is triggered by something being physically or functionally wrong with the thing itself. A legal defect (Section 435 BGB) exists when a third party can assert rights against the buyer in relation to the thing that were not assumed under the contract, triggered by an undisclosed encumbrance such as a lien or a lease burdening the item. Both open the same Section 437 BGB remedy gateway (cure, revocation, reduction, damages). Contrast example: a used car with a faulty transmission is a material defect; the same car secretly still subject to a bank's registered lien is a legal defect. The exam trap is defaulting to "material defect" for every fact pattern — a problem caused by someone else's right over the thing is a legal defect even though the same remedy provisions apply afterward.

### Question 3

Explain the tiered relationship between cure, revocation, and reduction, and state the Section 441 III reduction formula.

**Model Answer:** The requirements are tiered: (1) cure under Section 439 BGB has priority as the seller's second chance, because the buyer generally cannot skip straight to revocation, reduction, or damages; (2) only once cure fails, is refused, is impossible, is unreasonable for the buyer, or a set deadline expires does the buyer gain access to revocation (Sections 437 No. 2, 440, 323) or, in the alternative, reduction (Sections 437 No. 2, 441); (3) reduction differs from revocation in that the insignificance exclusion of Section 323 V sentence 2 BGB does not bar it, so even a minor defect can support a price reduction even where it could not support revocation. If reduction is chosen, the formula under Section 441 III BGB is: reduced price = agreed price × value of the thing with the defect / value of the thing without the defect. This tiering exists because warranty law is built around giving the seller a genuine second chance before the contract is disturbed further, and reduction (unlike revocation) preserves the contract entirely, only recalibrating the price.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 4 (Case)

**Facts:** K orders a specific "ThermoPro X200" espresso machine from online retailer B. B ships a similar-looking but different, cheaper "ThermoPro X100" model instead, without telling K. K notices only after unpacking. Both models function properly on their own terms, but K wants the model she actually ordered.

**Question:** Does K have a claim against B for delivery of the correct X200 model under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB?

**Model Answer:**

I. K could have a claim against B for cure by delivery of the X200 machine under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB if the requirements of a warranty defect claim are met.

II. This requires: first, a valid purchase agreement (Section 433 BGB); second, a defect existing at transfer of risk (Sections 434/435, 446 BGB); third, no exclusion of warranty rights; fourth, a remedy available under the Section 437 BGB gateway.

III. Application:
1. Purchase agreement: K and B validly concluded a purchase contract for an X200 machine through K's order and B's confirmation; no formation obstacle is apparent.
2. Defect: Questionable is whether receiving a different, functioning model counts as a defect. This is supported by Section 434 V BGB, under which delivery of a different thing than agreed (aliud delivery) is treated as a defect. Against this, one could argue the X100 is not "broken" and works fine on its own. However, Section 434 V BGB does not require malfunction — it protects the buyer's contractual choice of the specific agreed item, not merely delivery of some working substitute. Consequently, a defect exists.
3. Transfer of risk: the wrong item was itself the thing handed over at delivery, so the defect (being the wrong item) necessarily existed at transfer of risk under Section 446 BGB.
4. No exclusion: nothing in the facts suggests K knew of the substitution (Section 442 BGB) or that a valid exclusion clause exists; this is not a merchant-to-merchant sale, so Section 377 HGB does not apply.

IV. A material defect under Section 434 V BGB exists at transfer of risk, and no exclusion applies, so the Section 437 BGB gateway opens.

5. Remedy: K may choose cure under Section 439 I BGB; since the correct item is what B always owed under the contract, replacement delivery of the X200 is the natural cure choice, and nothing in the facts suggests this would be disproportionately expensive for B under Section 439 IV BGB.

V. As a result, K can demand delivery of the correct X200 machine from B under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB, against return of the X100 machine under Sections 439 VI, 346 I BGB.

### Question 5 (Case)

**Facts:** Manufacturer M advertises its "AquaFlow" garden hose nationally as "kink-resistant for its entire 20-meter length." Retailer R sells an AquaFlow hose to buyer P without repeating or altering the claim, but had displayed M's advertising poster in-store and never corrected or distanced itself from it. After two weeks of ordinary use, the hose kinks badly at the 12-meter mark. R and P never discussed kink-resistance specifically at the point of sale.

**Question:** Does P have a claim against R for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB?

**Model Answer:**

I. P could have a claim against R for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB if the hose is defective, the defect existed at transfer of risk, and no exclusion applies.

II. This requires: first, a valid purchase agreement; second, a material or legal defect at transfer of risk; third, no exclusion; fourth, a remedy under Section 437 BGB.

III. Application:
1. Purchase agreement: validly concluded; no issue raised on the facts.
2. Subjective defect: no explicit individual agreement on kink-resistance was made between R and P, so a subjective-quality defect under Section 434 II 1 No. 1 BGB is not established on its own.
3. Objective defect: Questionable is whether the manufacturer's advertising creates a defect anyway. This is supported by Section 434 III 1 No. 2(b) BGB, under which public statements by the manufacturer about specific qualities of the goods can shape the buyer's legitimate objective expectations, provided the seller knew or should have known of the statement, it was not corrected, and it was capable of influencing the purchase decision. Against this, one could argue R never personally repeated the claim to P. However, Section 434 III 3 BGB only releases the seller where the seller neither knew nor could have known of the statement, or it was corrected, or it did not influence the decision — none apply here, since R displayed the poster uncorrected in its own store. Consequently, the hose's failure to be kink-resistant along its full length is an objective-requirement defect.
4. Transfer of risk: the weakness in the hose's construction was inherent from manufacture, so it existed at handover even though it only manifested after two weeks of ordinary use.
5. No exclusion: nothing indicates P knew of the weakness or that a valid exclusion clause exists; this is a consumer sale, so Section 377 HGB is not relevant.

IV. A material (objective-requirement) defect under Section 434 III 1 No. 2(b) BGB exists at transfer of risk, unexcluded, so the Section 437 BGB gateway opens.

6. Remedy: cure under Section 439 BGB — repair of a structural weave defect is unlikely to be practicable, so replacement is the natural cure choice, and nothing suggests it would be disproportionate for an ordinary garden hose.

V. As a result, P can demand a replacement AquaFlow hose from R under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB.

### Question 6 (Case)

**Facts:** Buyer L purchases a designer office chair for EUR 1,200 from seller F. After delivery, L discovers one armrest has a visible manufacturing scratch that does not affect function. Repairing/refinishing the armrest costs EUR 80 and takes one day, fully restoring the chair. L instead demands a brand-new replacement chair, which would cost F EUR 1,200 plus return shipping and disposal of the scratched chair.

**Question:** Can L insist on replacement, or is F entitled to perform repair instead?

**Model Answer:**

I. L could have a claim against F for replacement under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 I BGB, unless F may validly refuse this cure type under Section 439 IV BGB.

II. This requires: first, that L has a valid cure choice under Section 439 I BGB; second, that F's refusal right under Section 439 IV BGB for disproportionate cost is examined; third, the resulting remedy for L.

III. Application:
1. Buyer's choice: L is entitled to choose between repair and replacement under Section 439 I BGB and has chosen replacement.
2. Disproportion: Questionable is whether F may refuse the chosen cure type. This is supported by the stark cost gap: replacement costs roughly fifteen times more than repair (EUR 1,200 plus shipping and disposal versus EUR 80), and repair fully restores the chair to a flawless, functionally unaffected condition within one day. Against this, one could argue L bought a "new, unblemished" chair and should not be forced to accept a repaired one. However, Section 439 IV BGB weighs the value of the item free of defects, the significance of the defect, and whether the alternative cure works without substantial disadvantage to the buyer — a purely cosmetic scratch, fully repairable in a day with no functional impact, does not create a disadvantage for L comparable to F's disproportionate replacement cost. Consequently, F may refuse replacement as disproportionate.

IV. Replacement can be validly refused; repair remains available to L as the reasonable alternative cure type.

V. As a result, L cannot insist on replacement; L is restricted to demanding repair of the armrest from F under Section 439 I, IV BGB.

### Question 7 (Case)

**Facts:** At a clearance sale, seller D expressly tells buyer Q, "this printer's scanner unit is broken, only the printing function works," and marks the price down accordingly. Q buys the printer anyway. A week later, Q confirms the scanner does not work and demands that D repair it under warranty.

**Question:** Does Q have a claim against D for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB?

**Model Answer:**

I. Q could have a claim against D for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB if the printer is defective and no exclusion applies.

II. This requires: first, a valid purchase agreement; second, a defect; third, no exclusion, in particular under Section 442 BGB; fourth, a remedy under Section 437 BGB.

III. Application:
1. Purchase agreement: validly concluded.
2. Defect: a non-functioning scanner unit is a deviation from ordinary expected quality, so a material defect under Section 434 III BGB would exist in the abstract.
3. Exclusion: Questionable is whether Section 442 BGB excludes Q's rights for this defect. This is supported by the fact that D expressly disclosed the scanner defect before sale and Q bought the printer anyway, meaning Q positively knew of the defect at contract conclusion. Against this, one could argue Q still paid money for "a printer" generally. However, Section 442 I BGB excludes the buyer's warranty rights precisely for defects the buyer knew of at conclusion, and the discounted price in fact confirms both parties priced the known defect into the deal. Consequently, the "no exclusion" requirement fails as to the scanner defect specifically.

IV. Warranty rights for the scanner defect are excluded under Section 442 I BGB.

V. As a result, Q has no claim against D for cure of the scanner under Sections 437, 439 BGB, because Q knew of that specific defect at contract conclusion.

### Question 8 (Case — integrates Week 08 + Week 11)

**Facts:** Wholesaler W (a registered merchant) sells 500 packaged units of industrial-grade sealant to hardware retailer H (also a registered merchant) for H's shop inventory. The sealant is delivered to H's warehouse on a Monday. H's warehouse staff sign for the delivery but do not open or inspect any cartons, simply stacking them in storage. Three weeks later, when H finally opens cartons to restock shelves, staff discover roughly a third of the units have hardened and become unusable — a defect that would have been visible immediately upon opening any carton. H immediately calls W and demands replacement units under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB.

**Question:** Does H have a claim against W for replacement of the defective units?

**Model Answer:**

I. H could have a claim against W for replacement under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB if the sealant is defective, the defect existed at transfer of risk, and no exclusion applies — including the Section 377 HGB commercial inspection and notice duty, since both parties are merchants.

II. This requires: first, a valid purchase agreement (Section 433 BGB); second, a material defect at transfer of risk (Sections 434, 446 BGB); third, that the sale is a mutual commercial purchase between merchants triggering Section 377 HGB; fourth, that H complied with the Section 377 HGB inspection and notice duty; fifth, no other exclusion; sixth, a remedy under Section 437 BGB.

III. Application:
1. Purchase agreement: W and H validly concluded a sale of 500 sealant units for H's business; no formation issue is apparent.
2. Defect: hardened, unusable sealant deviates from the ordinary usable quality expected of the product under Section 434 III 1 No. 1/2 BGB — a material defect — and nothing suggests the hardening happened after delivery rather than being inherent in a third of the batch, so it is treated as existing at transfer of risk under Section 446 BGB absent contrary facts.
3. Merchant/commercial-purchase gateway: Questionable is whether the ordinary BGB warranty analysis is modified here. This is supported by both W and H being registered merchants transacting for their respective businesses, making this a mutual commercial purchase under Section 343 HGB, which in turn triggers the Section 377 HGB notice regime on top of the BGB warranty gateway.
4. Section 377 HGB inspection/notice: Section 377 HGB requires H to inspect the goods without undue delay after delivery and notify any defect without undue delay after discovery. This is supported by the fact that the defect would have been visible immediately upon opening any carton, meaning a reasonable inspection at or shortly after delivery would have caught it. Against this, one could argue three weeks is not obviously excessive for a busy warehouse. However, H's staff did not inspect at all — they simply stacked the unopened cartons — and their conduct is attributed to H under Section 278 BGB; a multi-week delay before any inspection of a readily discoverable defect exceeds "without undue delay." Consequently, H failed the Section 377 HGB inspection duty.

IV. Because H did not inspect and notify without undue delay, the sealant units are deemed approved under Section 377 HGB, and H's warranty rights — including the claim for replacement under Sections 437, 439 BGB — are excluded, regardless of how serious or genuine the underlying material defect actually was.

V. As a result, H has no claim against W for replacement of the defective sealant units, because H's own failure to timely inspect and notify under Section 377 HGB bars the Section 437 BGB warranty claim.

## Quick Answer Route Summary

| Question # | Primary Route/Section | One-line takeaway |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Section 446 BGB | Transfer of risk, not discovery, is the decisive timing test. |
| 2 | Sections 434, 435 BGB | Material = thing's own condition; legal = a third party's right over the thing. |
| 3 | Sections 439, 440/323, 441 III BGB | Cure first, then revocation or reduction; reduction alone survives insignificance. |
| 4 (Case) | Section 434 V BGB | Aliud delivery is a defect even if the wrong item works fine. |
| 5 (Case) | Section 434 III 1 No. 2(b) BGB | Uncorrected manufacturer advertising can create an objective defect. |
| 6 (Case) | Section 439 IV BGB | Seller can refuse a disproportionate cure type, buyer then limited to the other type. |
| 7 (Case) | Section 442 I BGB | Buyer's pre-conclusion knowledge of a specific defect excludes rights for that defect. |
| 8 (Case, integrative) | Section 377 HGB + Section 437 BGB | Merchant buyer's late inspection/notice bars an otherwise valid warranty claim entirely. |
