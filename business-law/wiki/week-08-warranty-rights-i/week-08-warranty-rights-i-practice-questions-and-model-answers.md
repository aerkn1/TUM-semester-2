## Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 1

Explain what "transfer of risk" means in warranty law and why it functions as the decisive timing checkpoint.

**Model Answer:** Transfer of risk is the moment — usually handover/delivery of the thing under Section 446 sentence 1 BGB — that separates general impaired-performance law from warranty law. Before transfer of risk, any performance problem is handled under ordinary contract-breach rules; after it, the buyer bears the risk of accidental deterioration and warranty law governs whether the seller answers for a defect. The requirement matters because the buyer must show the defect already existed at transfer of risk, not merely that it was discovered later — a laptop battery defect present at handover is a seller's problem, but coffee spilled on the laptop after handover is not. The exam trap is conflating transfer of risk with the later cure deadline: transfer of risk decides whether the defect belongs to the seller's warranty responsibility at all, while the cure deadline decides only whether the buyer may escalate from cure to secondary remedies.

### Question 2

Compare material defect and legal defect under German sales law.

**Model Answer:** A material defect (Section 434 BGB) exists when the thing's actual condition deviates from its legally required condition — assessed through subjective agreement, objective baseline expectations, assembly requirements, or aliud (wrong-item) delivery — and is triggered by something being physically or functionally wrong with the thing itself. A legal defect (Section 435 BGB) exists when a third party can assert rights against the buyer in relation to the thing that were not assumed under the contract, triggered by an undisclosed encumbrance such as a lien or a lease burdening the item. Both open the same Section 437 BGB remedy gateway (cure, revocation, reduction, damages). Contrast example: a used car with a faulty transmission is a material defect; the same car secretly still subject to a bank's registered lien is a legal defect. The exam trap is defaulting to "material defect" for every fact pattern — a problem caused by someone else's right over the thing is a legal defect even though the same remedy provisions apply afterward.

### Question 3

Explain the tiered relationship between cure, revocation, and reduction, and state the Section 441 III reduction formula.

**Model Answer:** The requirements are tiered: (1) cure under Section 439 BGB has priority as the seller's second chance, because the buyer generally cannot skip straight to revocation, reduction, or damages; (2) only once cure fails, is refused, is impossible, is unreasonable for the buyer, or a set deadline expires does the buyer gain access to revocation (Sections 437 No. 2, 440, 323) or, in the alternative, reduction (Sections 437 No. 2, 441); (3) reduction differs from revocation in that the insignificance exclusion of Section 323 V sentence 2 BGB does not bar it, so even a minor defect can support a price reduction even where it could not support revocation. If reduction is chosen, the formula under Section 441 III BGB is: reduced price = agreed price × value of the thing with the defect / value of the thing without the defect. This tiering exists because warranty law is built around giving the seller a genuine second chance before the contract is disturbed further, and reduction (unlike revocation) preserves the contract entirely, only recalibrating the price.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 4 (Case)

**Facts:** K orders a specific "ThermoPro X200" espresso machine from online retailer B. B ships a similar-looking but different, cheaper "ThermoPro X100" model instead, without telling K. K notices only after unpacking. Both models function properly on their own terms, but K wants the model she actually ordered.

**Question:** Does K have a claim against B for delivery of the correct X200 model under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB?

**Model Answer:**

I. K could have a claim against B for cure by delivery of the X200 machine under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB if the requirements of a warranty defect claim are met.

II. This requires: first, a valid purchase agreement (Section 433 BGB); second, a defect existing at transfer of risk (Sections 434/435, 446 BGB); third, no exclusion of warranty rights; fourth, a remedy available under the Section 437 BGB gateway.

III. Application:
1. Purchase agreement: K and B validly concluded a purchase contract for an X200 machine through K's order and B's confirmation; no formation obstacle is apparent.
2. Defect: Questionable is whether receiving a different, functioning model counts as a defect. This is supported by Section 434 V BGB, under which delivery of a different thing than agreed (aliud delivery) is treated as a defect. Against this, one could argue the X100 is not "broken" and works fine on its own. However, Section 434 V BGB does not require malfunction — it protects the buyer's contractual choice of the specific agreed item, not merely delivery of some working substitute. Consequently, a defect exists.
3. Transfer of risk: the wrong item was itself the thing handed over at delivery, so the defect (being the wrong item) necessarily existed at transfer of risk under Section 446 BGB.
4. No exclusion: nothing in the facts suggests K knew of the substitution (Section 442 BGB) or that a valid exclusion clause exists; this is not a merchant-to-merchant sale, so Section 377 HGB does not apply.

IV. A material defect under Section 434 V BGB exists at transfer of risk, and no exclusion applies, so the Section 437 BGB gateway opens.

5. Remedy: K may choose cure under Section 439 I BGB; since the correct item is what B always owed under the contract, replacement delivery of the X200 is the natural cure choice, and nothing in the facts suggests this would be disproportionately expensive for B under Section 439 IV BGB.

V. As a result, K can demand delivery of the correct X200 machine from B under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB, against return of the X100 machine under Sections 439 VI, 346 I BGB.

### Question 5 (Case)

**Facts:** Manufacturer M advertises its "AquaFlow" garden hose nationally as "kink-resistant for its entire 20-meter length." Retailer R sells an AquaFlow hose to buyer P without repeating or altering the claim, but had displayed M's advertising poster in-store and never corrected or distanced itself from it. After two weeks of ordinary use, the hose kinks badly at the 12-meter mark. R and P never discussed kink-resistance specifically at the point of sale.

**Question:** Does P have a claim against R for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB?

**Model Answer:**

I. P could have a claim against R for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB if the hose is defective, the defect existed at transfer of risk, and no exclusion applies.

II. This requires: first, a valid purchase agreement; second, a material or legal defect at transfer of risk; third, no exclusion; fourth, a remedy under Section 437 BGB.

III. Application:
1. Purchase agreement: validly concluded; no issue raised on the facts.
2. Subjective defect: no explicit individual agreement on kink-resistance was made between R and P, so a subjective-quality defect under Section 434 II 1 No. 1 BGB is not established on its own.
3. Objective defect: Questionable is whether the manufacturer's advertising creates a defect anyway. This is supported by Section 434 III 1 No. 2(b) BGB, under which public statements by the manufacturer about specific qualities of the goods can shape the buyer's legitimate objective expectations, provided the seller knew or should have known of the statement, it was not corrected, and it was capable of influencing the purchase decision. Against this, one could argue R never personally repeated the claim to P. However, Section 434 III 3 BGB only releases the seller where the seller neither knew nor could have known of the statement, or it was corrected, or it did not influence the decision — none apply here, since R displayed the poster uncorrected in its own store. Consequently, the hose's failure to be kink-resistant along its full length is an objective-requirement defect.
4. Transfer of risk: the weakness in the hose's construction was inherent from manufacture, so it existed at handover even though it only manifested after two weeks of ordinary use.
5. No exclusion: nothing indicates P knew of the weakness or that a valid exclusion clause exists; this is a consumer sale, so Section 377 HGB is not relevant.

IV. A material (objective-requirement) defect under Section 434 III 1 No. 2(b) BGB exists at transfer of risk, unexcluded, so the Section 437 BGB gateway opens.

6. Remedy: cure under Section 439 BGB — repair of a structural weave defect is unlikely to be practicable, so replacement is the natural cure choice, and nothing suggests it would be disproportionate for an ordinary garden hose.

V. As a result, P can demand a replacement AquaFlow hose from R under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB.

### Question 6 (Case)

**Facts:** Buyer L purchases a designer office chair for EUR 1,200 from seller F. After delivery, L discovers one armrest has a visible manufacturing scratch that does not affect function. Repairing/refinishing the armrest costs EUR 80 and takes one day, fully restoring the chair. L instead demands a brand-new replacement chair, which would cost F EUR 1,200 plus return shipping and disposal of the scratched chair.

**Question:** Can L insist on replacement, or is F entitled to perform repair instead?

**Model Answer:**

I. L could have a claim against F for replacement under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 I BGB, unless F may validly refuse this cure type under Section 439 IV BGB.

II. This requires: first, that L has a valid cure choice under Section 439 I BGB; second, that F's refusal right under Section 439 IV BGB for disproportionate cost is examined; third, the resulting remedy for L.

III. Application:
1. Buyer's choice: L is entitled to choose between repair and replacement under Section 439 I BGB and has chosen replacement.
2. Disproportion: Questionable is whether F may refuse the chosen cure type. This is supported by the stark cost gap: replacement costs roughly fifteen times more than repair (EUR 1,200 plus shipping and disposal versus EUR 80), and repair fully restores the chair to a flawless, functionally unaffected condition within one day. Against this, one could argue L bought a "new, unblemished" chair and should not be forced to accept a repaired one. However, Section 439 IV BGB weighs the value of the item free of defects, the significance of the defect, and whether the alternative cure works without substantial disadvantage to the buyer — a purely cosmetic scratch, fully repairable in a day with no functional impact, does not create a disadvantage for L comparable to F's disproportionate replacement cost. Consequently, F may refuse replacement as disproportionate.

IV. Replacement can be validly refused; repair remains available to L as the reasonable alternative cure type.

V. As a result, L cannot insist on replacement; L is restricted to demanding repair of the armrest from F under Section 439 I, IV BGB.

### Question 7 (Case)

**Facts:** At a clearance sale, seller D expressly tells buyer Q, "this printer's scanner unit is broken, only the printing function works," and marks the price down accordingly. Q buys the printer anyway. A week later, Q confirms the scanner does not work and demands that D repair it under warranty.

**Question:** Does Q have a claim against D for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB?

**Model Answer:**

I. Q could have a claim against D for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB if the printer is defective and no exclusion applies.

II. This requires: first, a valid purchase agreement; second, a defect; third, no exclusion, in particular under Section 442 BGB; fourth, a remedy under Section 437 BGB.

III. Application:
1. Purchase agreement: validly concluded.
2. Defect: a non-functioning scanner unit is a deviation from ordinary expected quality, so a material defect under Section 434 III BGB would exist in the abstract.
3. Exclusion: Questionable is whether Section 442 BGB excludes Q's rights for this defect. This is supported by the fact that D expressly disclosed the scanner defect before sale and Q bought the printer anyway, meaning Q positively knew of the defect at contract conclusion. Against this, one could argue Q still paid money for "a printer" generally. However, Section 442 I BGB excludes the buyer's warranty rights precisely for defects the buyer knew of at conclusion, and the discounted price in fact confirms both parties priced the known defect into the deal. Consequently, the "no exclusion" requirement fails as to the scanner defect specifically.

IV. Warranty rights for the scanner defect are excluded under Section 442 I BGB.

V. As a result, Q has no claim against D for cure of the scanner under Sections 437, 439 BGB, because Q knew of that specific defect at contract conclusion.

### Question 8 (Case — integrates Week 08 + Week 11)

**Facts:** Wholesaler W (a registered merchant) sells 500 packaged units of industrial-grade sealant to hardware retailer H (also a registered merchant) for H's shop inventory. The sealant is delivered to H's warehouse on a Monday. H's warehouse staff sign for the delivery but do not open or inspect any cartons, simply stacking them in storage. Three weeks later, when H finally opens cartons to restock shelves, staff discover roughly a third of the units have hardened and become unusable — a defect that would have been visible immediately upon opening any carton. H immediately calls W and demands replacement units under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB.

**Question:** Does H have a claim against W for replacement of the defective units?

**Model Answer:**

I. H could have a claim against W for replacement under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB if the sealant is defective, the defect existed at transfer of risk, and no exclusion applies — including the Section 377 HGB commercial inspection and notice duty, since both parties are merchants.

II. This requires: first, a valid purchase agreement (Section 433 BGB); second, a material defect at transfer of risk (Sections 434, 446 BGB); third, that the sale is a mutual commercial purchase between merchants triggering Section 377 HGB; fourth, that H complied with the Section 377 HGB inspection and notice duty; fifth, no other exclusion; sixth, a remedy under Section 437 BGB.

III. Application:
1. Purchase agreement: W and H validly concluded a sale of 500 sealant units for H's business; no formation issue is apparent.
2. Defect: hardened, unusable sealant deviates from the ordinary usable quality expected of the product under Section 434 III 1 No. 1/2 BGB — a material defect — and nothing suggests the hardening happened after delivery rather than being inherent in a third of the batch, so it is treated as existing at transfer of risk under Section 446 BGB absent contrary facts.
3. Merchant/commercial-purchase gateway: Questionable is whether the ordinary BGB warranty analysis is modified here. This is supported by both W and H being registered merchants transacting for their respective businesses, making this a mutual commercial purchase under Section 343 HGB, which in turn triggers the Section 377 HGB notice regime on top of the BGB warranty gateway.
4. Section 377 HGB inspection/notice: Section 377 HGB requires H to inspect the goods without undue delay after delivery and notify any defect without undue delay after discovery. This is supported by the fact that the defect would have been visible immediately upon opening any carton, meaning a reasonable inspection at or shortly after delivery would have caught it. Against this, one could argue three weeks is not obviously excessive for a busy warehouse. However, H's staff did not inspect at all — they simply stacked the unopened cartons — and their conduct is attributed to H under Section 278 BGB; a multi-week delay before any inspection of a readily discoverable defect exceeds "without undue delay." Consequently, H failed the Section 377 HGB inspection duty.

IV. Because H did not inspect and notify without undue delay, the sealant units are deemed approved under Section 377 HGB, and H's warranty rights — including the claim for replacement under Sections 437, 439 BGB — are excluded, regardless of how serious or genuine the underlying material defect actually was.

V. As a result, H has no claim against W for replacement of the defective sealant units, because H's own failure to timely inspect and notify under Section 377 HGB bars the Section 437 BGB warranty claim.

## Additional Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 9

Explain who bears the costs of cure, and how those costs are treated if the seller cures by replacement rather than repair.

**Model Answer:** Section 439 II BGB places the necessary expenses of cure — transport, labour, and material costs — squarely on the seller, and Section 439 III BGB extends this to removal and reinstallation costs where the buyer has already installed or attached the defective item elsewhere, so the buyer is never required to advance or absorb the cost of the seller's own repair or replacement. Where the seller cures by replacement rather than repair, Section 439 V BGB (via Section 346 I BGB) gives the seller a right to have the original defective item returned, mirroring the restitution logic used elsewhere when a remedy step unwinds part of the exchange. The requirement matters because it keeps cure genuinely cost-free for the buyer — a seller cannot condition free replacement on the buyer separately funding removal of the old unit. The exam trap is assuming the buyer must simply hand back the defective item without a statutory basis for that duty; Section 439 V's cross-reference to Section 346 I is exactly that basis, and forgetting it leaves the seller's restitution claim unsupported.

### Question 10

Compare how the burden of proving a defect's timing is allocated in an ordinary sale versus a consumer sale within one year of delivery.

**Model Answer (Compare Schema):** In an ordinary sale, the buyer bears the burden of proving that a defect already existed at transfer of risk under Section 446 BGB — showing only that a problem exists today is not enough, since a defect arising after handover is not the seller's responsibility. In a consumer sale under Sections 474-479 BGB, Section 477 BGB reverses this: if the defect becomes apparent within one year of delivery, it is presumed to have already existed at transfer of risk, and the burden shifts to the seller to disprove it. Contrast example: a self-employed carpenter buying a saw for his workshop is an ordinary sale, so he must prove the defect predates delivery; a private individual buying the same saw for home use is a consumer sale, so a fault discovered ten months later is presumed pre-existing unless the seller disproves it. Exam trap: the one-year presumption is a burden-of-proof rule inside the ordinary limitation period, not a one-year warranty extension, and it never applies outside a genuine business-to-consumer sale.

### Question 11

State the main limits on a seller's ability to contractually exclude warranty rights.

**Model Answer:** Sellers may in principle exclude or limit warranty rights by agreement, but several limits prevent this from swallowing buyer protection entirely. Section 444 BGB blocks reliance on an exclusion clause where the seller fraudulently concealed the defect or gave a guarantee covering it — a seller cannot both promise a quality and contract out of liability for its absence. Section 309 No. 8 BGB restricts standard-business-terms exclusions specifically for newly manufactured goods, so a blanket SBT clause cutting out warranty rights on new goods is often invalid regardless of its wording. Good faith under Section 242 BGB further bars a seller from agreeing to a specific quality and then excluding liability for exactly that quality, since the exclusion would contradict the seller's own contractual commitment. The requirement matters because it stops exclusion clauses from stripping away protections the seller itself created through express assurances. Trap: an exclusion clause is not automatically valid merely because the parties "agreed" to it — always check fraud, guarantee, SBT rules, and good-faith contradiction before accepting it at face value.

### Question 12

List the limitation periods for warranty claims relating to movable goods and buildings, and explain what they run from.

**Model Answer (List-Requirements Schema):** (1) For movable goods, Section 438 I No. 3 BGB sets a two-year limitation period; (2) for buildings, Section 438 I No. 2 BGB sets a five-year period, reflecting the greater complexity and cost of construction defects; (3) both periods run from delivery of the thing (movables) or the equivalent handover/acceptance event, not from when the buyer actually discovers the defect. Trap: students often assume the clock starts at discovery, by analogy to tort-law "knew or should have known" starting points — warranty limitation periods under Section 438 BGB instead start from an objective handover event regardless of when the buyer actually notices the problem, so a defect noticed at month eleven and one noticed at month twenty-three of a two-year movable-goods period face the very same cutoff measured from delivery.

### Question 13

Beyond a failed or refused cure, list the other grounds on which revocation specifically can be excluded even though the general warranty gateway is open.

**Model Answer (List-Requirements Schema):** (1) Insignificance: Section 323 V sentence 2 BGB excludes revocation where the breach of duty is insignificant — a trivial defect cannot support unwinding the whole contract, even though it can still support a price reduction, since Section 441's reduction route is not subject to the same insignificance bar; (2) buyer-caused defect: Section 323 VI BGB excludes revocation where the buyer is solely or predominantly responsible for the circumstance that would otherwise entitle them to revoke; (3) buyer in default of acceptance: Section 323 VI BGB also excludes revocation where the entitling circumstance occurred while the buyer was in default of accepting the goods. Trap: these exclusions attach specifically to revocation — they do not block reduction (insignificance) and do not automatically block a damages claim, which runs its own separate responsibility test under Section 280 I BGB; always identify which specific remedy is actually being excluded rather than assuming one exclusion closes every route at once.

## Additional Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 14 (Case)

**Facts:** Buyer T tells seller S before purchase, "I need this paint to be exactly RAL 5010 gentian blue for a corporate branding job," and S confirms in writing, "yes, this can 100% matches RAL 5010." After delivery, independent testing shows the paint is actually RAL 5013 — a visibly different, darker blue — though S genuinely believed the match was correct.

**Question:** Does T have a claim for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB, even though the paint would pass ordinary market-quality expectations for "blue paint" generally?

**Model Answer:**

I. T could have a claim against S for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB if the paint is defective under the parties' individual agreement and no exclusion applies.

II. This requires: first, a valid purchase agreement; second, a material defect at transfer of risk, tested first against the parties' subjective agreement; third, no exclusion; fourth, a remedy under Section 437 BGB.

III. Application:
1. Purchase agreement: validly concluded via T's order and S's written confirmation.
2. Defect: Questionable is whether ordinary "blue paint" quality suffices, given the delivered shade is an objectively normal, saleable color. This is supported instead by Section 434 II 1 No. 1 BGB, under which an individual agreement on quality — here S's express written confirmation that the paint "100% matches RAL 5010" — controls ahead of any objective baseline expectation. Against this, one could argue S genuinely believed the match was accurate and acted in good faith. However, Section 434 BGB's defect test is not fault-based; it asks only whether the delivered condition deviates from the individually agreed condition, and RAL 5013 is a distinct, visibly different shade from the agreed RAL 5010. Consequently, a subjective-quality defect exists regardless of S's honest belief.
3. Transfer of risk: the pigment formulation was fixed at mixing, so the mismatch existed at handover under Section 446 BGB.
4. No exclusion: nothing indicates T knew of the mismatch or that a valid exclusion clause exists.

IV. A subjective-quality defect under Section 434 II 1 No. 1 BGB exists at transfer of risk and is unexcluded, so the Section 437 BGB gateway opens.

5. Remedy: cure under Section 439 I BGB — replacement with correctly mixed RAL 5010 paint is the natural cure.

V. As a result, T can demand cure from S under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB; S's honest belief in the match is irrelevant to whether a defect exists, since Section 434 BGB is not a fault-based test.

### Question 15 (Case)

**Facts:** Buyer N buys a flat-pack wardrobe from furniture retailer M. The wardrobe panels themselves are flawless, but the included assembly instructions are missing a page, causing N to attach the back panel incorrectly so that the wardrobe leans and the doors do not close properly. N calls M and demands cure.

**Question:** Does N have a claim for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB despite the physical panels being defect-free?

**Model Answer:**

I. N could have a claim against M for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB if the wardrobe is defective on account of the faulty assembly instructions and no exclusion applies.

II. This requires: first, a valid purchase agreement; second, a material defect at transfer of risk under the assembly-requirements route; third, no exclusion; fourth, a remedy under Section 437 BGB.

III. Application:
1. Purchase agreement: validly concluded; M owed a flat-pack item that includes assembly by the buyer, so assembly requirements are relevant on these facts.
2. Defect: Questionable is whether a defect exists at all, given the panels themselves are flawless. This is supported by Section 434 IV BGB, under which a defect also arises where assembly was carried out incorrectly, including where incorrect assembly resulted from defective assembly instructions supplied with the item ("IKEA clause" logic) — even though nothing is physically wrong with the components themselves. Against this, one could argue N caused the problem by assembling the wardrobe incorrectly. However, N's assembly followed the instructions M supplied, and it was the missing page — not N's own error — that caused the incorrect assembly. Consequently, a material defect under Section 434 IV BGB exists.
3. Transfer of risk: the instructions were incomplete as supplied at delivery, so the defect existed at transfer of risk under Section 446 BGB.
4. No exclusion: nothing indicates N knew the instructions were incomplete or that a valid exclusion clause exists.

IV. A material defect under Section 434 IV BGB exists at transfer of risk and is unexcluded, so the Section 437 BGB gateway opens.

5. Remedy: cure under Section 439 I BGB — M can cure by supplying the missing instruction page and, if needed, arranging correct reassembly; this is neither impossible nor disproportionate.

V. As a result, N can demand cure from M under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB, notwithstanding that the wardrobe's physical panels are entirely defect-free.

### Question 16 (Case — integrates Week 08 + Week 10)

**Facts:** A sewing machine is stolen from true owner O's workshop. The thief sells it at a flea market to trader S, who buys it in good faith, unaware of the theft. S then sells and delivers the machine to hobbyist buyer B, who also buys and takes delivery in good faith. Two weeks later, O traces the machine to B via its serial number and demands its return under Section 985 BGB. Separately, B has discovered that the machine's bobbin-tension mechanism was broken even before S ever possessed it, and wants cure from S.

**Question:** (1) Can O reclaim the machine from B despite B's good faith? (2) Independently, does B still have a warranty claim against S for the broken mechanism?

**Model Answer:**

I. Two separate claims arise: O's ownership-based recovery claim against B under Section 985 BGB, and B's contractual warranty claim against S under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB. The separation and abstraction principles mean the outcome of one does not automatically decide the other.

II. Requirements: for claim 1 (O v. B), O must be owner and B a possessor without a right to possess; for claim 2 (B v. S), a valid purchase agreement, a material defect at transfer of risk, no exclusion, and a remedy under Section 437 BGB — none of which require B to actually be the owner, since this is an obligation-law claim, not a property-law one.

III. Application

*Claim 1 — O v. B under Section 985 BGB.* Questionable is whether O remains owner despite the chain of sales down to B. This is supported by Section 929 sentence 1 BGB, which requires the transferor's authorization to pass ownership — the thief had no authorization from O, so the thief could not transfer ownership to S under ordinary rules. Against this, one could argue S and B nonetheless acquired ownership in good faith under Section 932 BGB despite the thief's lack of authorization. However, Section 935 I BGB blocks good-faith acquisition entirely where the thing was stolen from the true owner, and this block travels through the entire chain of subsequent good-faith buyers, not merely the first one. Consequently, neither S nor B ever acquired ownership, however honestly each believed otherwise, and B currently possesses the machine with no right to possess it as against O.

*Claim 2 — B v. S under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB.* Questionable is whether B has any claim at all against S given that B never became owner. This is supported by the separation principle: the purchase contract between S and B (Section 433 BGB) is an obligation contract distinct from the disposition that was supposed to follow it, and under the abstraction principle the validity of the obligation contract does not depend on whether the disposition actually succeeded. Against this, one could argue that since B never became owner, "B's purchase" was somehow void. However, nothing in the facts suggests the sales contract itself suffered any formation defect between S and B — S validly promised to sell and deliver the machine, and that promise stands regardless of S's own defective title. Consequently, a valid purchase agreement exists between B and S; the bobbin-tension mechanism was broken before S ever possessed the machine, so a material defect under Section 434 III BGB existed at the Section 446 BGB transfer of risk from S to B, and nothing indicates an exclusion.

IV. Interim results: O remains owner and can recover the machine from B under Section 985 BGB; separately, the Section 437 BGB warranty gateway is open between B and S.

V. As a result, O can reclaim the machine from B under Section 985 BGB despite B's good faith, because Section 935 BGB blocks good-faith acquisition for stolen goods down the whole chain of buyers — but B's contractual warranty claim against S for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB survives entirely independently of O's property-law claim, since the two transactions' validity is governed separately under the separation and abstraction principles.

### Question 17 (Case — integrates Week 08 + Week 09)

**Facts:** Buyer K orders custom-fitted industrial shelving from manufacturer P for EUR 8,000 for a warehouse expansion. On delivery, several shelf units are bent and unusable — a defect existing at transfer of risk. K demands cure and sets P a two-week deadline. P attempts a rushed repair that still leaves the units unusable and misses the deadline. K then buys equivalent replacement shelving from a competitor for EUR 9,500 to keep the project on schedule, and wants to recover the EUR 1,500 difference from P.

**Question:** Can K recover the EUR 1,500 price difference from P?

**Model Answer:**

I. K could have a claim against P for "big damages" (damages instead of the entire performance) under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB for the EUR 1,500 difference between the contract price and the substitute purchase.

II. This requires: first, that the Section 437 BGB warranty gateway is open; second, that cure was attempted and failed, or that a reasonable additional period for cure expired without result; third, an obligation, breach of duty, responsibility (presumed), and causal damage under Section 280 I BGB; fourth, that K gave up the defective performance entirely, distinguishing big damages from small damages.

III. Application:
1. Gateway: the bent, unusable shelf units deviate from the ordinary usable quality required under Section 434 III BGB, and nothing suggests the bending occurred after delivery, so a material defect existed at transfer of risk under Section 446 BGB, with no exclusion apparent.
2. Additional period: K set a two-week deadline, a reasonable additional period under Section 281 I BGB, and P's rushed attempt still left the units unusable within that period. Questionable is whether K needed to grant a further period. This is supported by Section 281 I BGB requiring only that a reasonable period expire without proper cure, not that the seller receive unlimited further attempts. Against this, one could argue a "rushed" attempt is not a genuine attempt and P deserves another chance. However, the facts state the deadline was missed entirely and the repair still left the units unusable, so the statutory precondition is satisfied regardless of how P characterizes its own effort. Consequently, K may proceed to damages instead of performance without granting a further period.
3. Section 280 I elements: there is an obligation (defect-free delivery under Section 433 I 2 BGB), a breach (delivery of bent, unusable units), responsibility presumed under Section 280 I sentence 2 BGB and not rebutted on the facts, and causal damage (the EUR 1,500 extra cost incurred specifically to replace P's failed performance).
4. Big vs. small damages: Questionable is whether K is limited to small damages. This is supported by K's conduct: K sourced replacement shelving from a competitor rather than keeping P's units, meaning K gave up the defective performance entirely. Against this, one could argue K should first have to prove the returned units are worthless. However, giving up the performance for a genuine substitute purchase is exactly the fact pattern big damages is built for, and K is returning P's defective units rather than retaining them. Consequently, K's claim is properly analyzed as big damages, not small damages or reduction.

IV. Interim result: the warranty gateway is open, cure failed after a reasonable additional period, and the Section 280 I BGB damages requirements are met on a big-damages basis.

V. As a result, K can recover the EUR 1,500 price difference from P as big damages under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB, against return of the defective shelf units to P.

### Question 18 (Case)

**Facts:** Buyer G buys a vintage watch for EUR 5,000 from dealer V. G later discovers the watch's movement was previously serviced with a non-original part, which is below the ordinary quality expected of an all-original vintage piece of this kind and lowers its value. An appraisal values the watch at EUR 4,000 with the non-original part and EUR 5,000 if all-original; no equivalent original replacement part is realistically available, so repair to a fully original state is impracticable. G does not want revocation and instead demands a price reduction.

**Question:** What is G entitled to under Section 441 III BGB?

**Model Answer:**

I. G could have a claim against V for a reduced price under Sections 437 No. 2, 441 BGB if the watch is defective, cure is unavailable or dispensable, and the reduction formula is applied.

II. This requires: first, a valid purchase agreement; second, a material defect at transfer of risk; third, that the preconditions otherwise required for revocation (cure failed, impossible, or dispensable) are met, since reduction shares those preconditions but is not barred by insignificance; fourth, correct application of the Section 441 III BGB formula.

III. Application:
1. Purchase agreement: validly concluded for EUR 5,000.
2. Defect: the non-original movement part falls below the ordinary quality customary for an all-original vintage watch of this kind, a material defect under Section 434 III BGB, and the part was fitted before the sale, so it existed at transfer of risk under Section 446 BGB.
3. Cure availability: Questionable is whether cure via repair could resolve this. This is supported by no equivalent original replacement part being realistically available, and further disassembly/servicing of a vintage piece risking further loss of collector value. Against this, one could argue V should still get a chance to search for a part. However, the facts state no original replacement is realistically available, so cure is impracticable. Consequently, the cure-first requirement is dispensed with and G may proceed directly to reduction.

IV. Interim result: the requirements of Sections 437 No. 2, 441 BGB are met.

4. Reduction calculation: reduced price = agreed price × value with defect / value without defect = EUR 5,000 × (EUR 4,000 / EUR 5,000) = EUR 4,000.

V. As a result, G is entitled to a reduced price of EUR 4,000 — a refund of EUR 1,000 of the EUR 5,000 already paid — under Sections 437 No. 2, 441 III BGB.

## Further Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 19

Explain the limit Section 476 BGB places on agreements that deviate from warranty rights in a consumer purchase, and when it applies.

**Model Answer:** Section 476 BGB restricts the parties' freedom, in a consumer purchase under Sections 474 ff. BGB, to agree on terms that deviate from the buyer's statutory warranty rights to the buyer's disadvantage before the seller has been notified of a defect — protecting the consumer from pre-emptively signing away rights to protections the seller has not yet had to honor. The statutory anchor is Section 476 BGB, layered on top of the general warranty regime in Sections 434-441 BGB once the sale qualifies as a consumer purchase (trader selling to a consumer, Sections 13, 14, 474 I BGB). The requirement matters because it prevents traders from using standard forms or pre-sale waivers to strip away statutory protections before a consumer even knows a defect exists, closing an obvious end-run around the warranty regime. Example: a trader's sale contract stating "buyer waives all warranty claims regardless of defect" is ineffective under Section 476 BGB against a consumer buyer, even if the consumer signed it, because it deviates from statutory warranty rights before any defect notification. The exam trap is assuming this consumer-protective limit applies to B2B sales as well — Section 476 BGB is specific to consumer purchases; between two merchants or businesses, pre-sale warranty-limiting agreements face a different, generally more permissive analysis (subject still to SBT content control and Section 444 BGB).

## Further Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 20 (Case)

**Facts:** Buyer J tells retailer R's sales assistant, "I need this external hard drive because I want to back up my professional video-editing files, which involve very large 4K project files." The assistant says nothing to contradict this and completes the sale. The hard drive works perfectly for ordinary storage, but its write speed is too slow to handle the large 4K files without constant dropped frames, making it unsuitable for J's stated purpose. A drive marketed as an "everyday backup drive" would ordinarily be expected to perform fine for typical, non-professional use.

**Question:** Does J have a claim for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB even though the drive works fine for ordinary backup purposes?

**Model Answer:**

I. J could have a claim against R for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB if the drive is defective measured against J's disclosed intended use and no exclusion applies.

II. This requires: first, a valid purchase agreement; second, a material defect at transfer of risk, tested first against the parties' subjective agreement including intended contractual use; third, no exclusion; fourth, a remedy under Section 437 BGB.

III. Application:
1. Purchase agreement: validly concluded via J's purchase from R.
2. Defect: Questionable is whether an ordinarily-functioning backup drive can be defective. This is supported by Section 434 II 1 No. 2 BGB, under which a defect exists where the thing is unsuitable for the use intended under the contract, where that intended use was made recognizable to the seller and not objected to. Against this, one could argue the drive works perfectly for its objectively marketed purpose as an "everyday backup drive," so no defect exists under ordinary market expectations. However, J disclosed the specific professional 4K video-editing purpose to R's own sales assistant before purchase, and R raised no objection or caveat about the drive's suitability for that use — under Section 434 II 1 No. 2 BGB, this disclosed and unopposed intended use sets a subjective requirement that displaces the merely objective baseline. Consequently, the drive's inability to reliably handle large 4K files without dropped frames is a defect measured against the agreed intended use, even though it performs adequately for generic backup tasks.
3. Transfer of risk: the write-speed limitation was an inherent property of the drive from manufacture, so it existed at handover under Section 446 BGB.
4. No exclusion: nothing indicates J knew of the speed limitation or that a valid exclusion clause exists.

IV. A material defect under Section 434 II 1 No. 2 BGB exists at transfer of risk and is unexcluded, so the Section 437 BGB gateway opens.

5. Remedy: cure under Section 439 I BGB — replacement with a drive suited to J's disclosed professional use is the natural cure, and nothing suggests disproportion.

V. As a result, J can demand cure from R under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB; the drive's fitness for ordinary generic backup use does not defeat the claim, because Section 434 II 1 No. 2 BGB tests suitability against the disclosed intended use, not against generic market expectations.

### Question 21 (Case)

**Facts:** Seller V sells buyer K a used industrial sewing machine "free of any third-party claims." Unknown to K, V had earlier pledged the machine as security for a business loan, and the bank's security interest was never released or disclosed to K. Two months after delivery, the bank contacts K asserting its security right and threatening to seize the machine.

**Question:** Does K have a claim against V for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB, even though the machine itself functions flawlessly?

**Model Answer:**

I. K could have a claim against V for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB if the machine suffers from a legal defect and no exclusion applies.

II. This requires: first, a valid purchase agreement; second, a legal defect at transfer of risk under Section 435 BGB; third, no exclusion; fourth, a remedy under Section 437 BGB.

III. Application:
1. Purchase agreement: validly concluded between V and K.
2. Defect: Questionable is whether a fully-functioning machine can be defective. This is supported by Section 435 BGB, under which a legal defect exists where a third party can assert rights against the buyer in relation to the thing that were not assumed under the contract — the mechanical condition of the item is irrelevant to this route. Against this, one could argue the machine works perfectly and nothing is physically wrong with it. However, the bank's undisclosed, unreleased security interest is exactly the kind of third-party right Section 435 BGB targets, and the parties' express agreement that the machine be delivered "free of any third-party claims" confirms K never assumed this encumbrance under the contract. Consequently, a legal defect under Section 435 BGB exists.
3. Transfer of risk: the security interest was created before the sale and never released, so it burdened the machine already at handover under Section 446 BGB.
4. No exclusion: nothing indicates K knew of the pledge or that a valid exclusion clause exists; V's express assurance of freedom from third-party claims cuts against any implied exclusion.

IV. A legal defect under Section 435 BGB exists at transfer of risk and is unexcluded, so the Section 437 BGB gateway opens.

5. Remedy: cure under Section 439 I BGB — V can cure by procuring release of the bank's security interest, clearing the machine of the third-party right; this is neither impossible nor disproportionate on the facts.

V. As a result, K can demand cure from V under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB despite the machine's flawless physical condition, because the undisclosed bank security interest is a legal defect under Section 435 BGB, a route distinct from material defects under Section 434 BGB.

### Question 22 (Case)

**Facts:** Buyer H inspects a used delivery van before purchase from dealer T. T invites H to "look it over as thoroughly as you like" and gives H unrestricted access for an hour. A large rust perforation is visible on the underside of the van, plainly noticeable to anyone who glances underneath, but H does not bother to look underneath at all before signing the purchase contract. After delivery, H discovers the rust damage and demands cure from T.

**Question:** Does H have a claim for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB?

**Model Answer:**

I. H could have a claim against T for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB unless H's rights are excluded under Section 442 I BGB.

II. This requires: first, a valid purchase agreement and material defect at transfer of risk; second, examination of whether Section 442 I BGB excludes the claim, including its grossly-negligent-ignorance branch; third, a remedy under Section 437 BGB if no exclusion applies.

III. Application:
1. Purchase agreement and defect: validly concluded; the rust perforation deviates from the ordinary quality expected of a used van under Section 434 III BGB and existed at transfer of risk under Section 446 BGB, since rust of that extent develops well before a single sale.
2. Exclusion: Questionable is whether Section 442 I BGB excludes H's rights despite H not actually knowing of the rust. This is supported by Section 442 I sentence 2 BGB, which excludes rights not only for defects the buyer actually knew of, but also for defects the buyer did not know of only as a result of gross negligence, unless the seller fraudulently concealed the defect or gave a guarantee. Against this, one could argue H never saw the rust and so cannot be treated as "knowing." However, T expressly invited H to inspect the van as thoroughly as desired and gave H an hour of unrestricted access, and the rust perforation was plainly visible to anyone who glanced underneath — failing to perform even this basic, invited check on a highly visible defect is gross negligence, not ordinary buyer inattention. Consequently, H's ignorance of the rust falls within the gross-negligence branch of Section 442 I BGB.

IV. Because H's ignorance of the rust resulted from gross negligence and nothing indicates fraudulent concealment or a guarantee by T, H's warranty rights for the rust defect are excluded under Section 442 I BGB.

V. As a result, H has no claim against T for cure of the rust damage under Sections 437, 439 BGB, because H's failure to notice a plainly visible defect during an invited, unrestricted inspection amounts to gross negligence within Section 442 I BGB — distinct from Question 7's scenario of actual disclosed knowledge, but producing the same exclusion.

### Question 23 (Case)

**Facts:** Caterer C orders a custom commercial oven from manufacturer M for a new kitchen build-out, paying EUR 15,000. Before delivery, C spends EUR 2,000 having a specialized ventilation duct installed specifically to fit this oven model. The oven arrives with a defect that M cannot cure — repair is impossible and no replacement unit of that model exists. C revokes the contract and, rather than trying to prove lost profit from a working oven, wants to recover the EUR 2,000 ventilation cost as wasted expenditure.

**Question:** Can C recover the EUR 2,000 from M?

**Model Answer:**

I. C could have a claim against M for reimbursement of futile expenses under Sections 437 No. 3, 284 BGB for the EUR 2,000 ventilation cost.

II. This requires: first, the Section 437 BGB warranty gateway is open and cure is excluded; second, C would otherwise have been entitled to damages instead of performance; third, C reasonably incurred expenses in reliance on receiving performance; fourth, the expenses' purpose was not going to fail regardless of the breach.

III. Application:
1. Gateway: the oven's defect deviates from the ordinary quality expected under Section 434 III BGB and existed at transfer of risk under Section 446 BGB; cure is impossible since no replacement exists and repair cannot fix it, dispensing with the cure-first requirement under Section 440 BGB.
2. Reliance expenditure: Questionable is whether C can recover the ventilation cost without proving lost profit. This is supported by Section 284 BGB, which allows a creditor to demand reimbursement of expenses reasonably incurred in reliance on receiving the seller's performance, as an alternative to damages instead of performance, precisely so the creditor need not quantify a speculative profit figure. Against this, one could argue C should instead have to prove what profit the working oven would have generated. However, Section 284 BGB exists exactly to spare the creditor that speculative proof burden where reliance expenditure is easier to show, and the EUR 2,000 duct installation was reasonably incurred specifically to accommodate this oven model in reliance on M's performance. Consequently, C may elect reimbursement under Section 284 BGB instead of proving lost profit.
3. Purpose not otherwise doomed: nothing in the facts suggests the ventilation duct would have been wasted expenditure even if the oven had been defect-free — it was built specifically to fit this model and would have served its purpose had M performed properly.

IV. Interim result: the Section 437 BGB gateway is open, cure is impossible, and the Section 284 BGB reimbursement requirements are met.

V. As a result, C can recover the EUR 2,000 ventilation cost from M under Sections 437 No. 3, 284 BGB as reimbursement of futile expenses, without needing to prove the oven's hypothetical lost profit.

### Question 24 (Case)

**Facts:** Manufacturer M runs a national ad campaign claiming its "SolarMax" garden light charges "fully in two hours of sunlight." Retailer R sells a SolarMax light to buyer P but, before the sale, R's shop clearly displays its own printed correction stating "Note: SolarMax charging times vary; the manufacturer's 'two hours' claim is not guaranteed and should be disregarded." P notices this correction sign but buys the light anyway because he liked its design, not because of the charging-time claim, and later complains that the light actually takes four hours to charge.

**Question:** Does P have a claim against R for cure based on the discrepancy between the advertised and actual charging time?

**Model Answer:**

I. P could have a claim against R for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB only if the advertised two-hour charging claim shapes an objective-quality defect; this is questionable given R's correction.

II. This requires: first, a valid purchase agreement; second, a material defect at transfer of risk, including whether manufacturer advertising shapes objective requirements under Section 434 III 1 No. 2(b) BGB, unless the seller is released under Section 434 III 3 BGB; third, no other exclusion; fourth, a remedy under Section 437 BGB.

III. Application:
1. Purchase agreement: validly concluded between R and P.
2. Objective defect via advertising: Questionable is whether M's "two hours" claim became part of the objectively expected quality. This is supported in principle by Section 434 III 1 No. 2(b) BGB, under which public statements by the manufacturer about specific product qualities can shape a buyer's legitimate objective expectations. Against this, one could argue R is not the one who made the claim. However, that alone would not release R, since Section 434 III 1 No. 2(b) BGB reaches statements by manufacturers displayed by the seller too — the decisive question is instead whether R is released under Section 434 III 3 BGB, which excuses the seller where the statement was corrected in a comparable manner to how it was made, or where it did not influence the purchase decision. Both release grounds are met here: R displayed a clear, comparably prominent correction disclaiming the two-hour figure, and the facts state P bought the light for its design, not because of the charging-time claim. Consequently, R is released under Section 434 III 3 BGB, and the advertised charging time never became part of the objectively expected quality.

IV. No defect arises from the discrepancy between the advertised and actual charging time, because R's correction and P's independent purchase motive both satisfy the Section 434 III 3 BGB release.

V. As a result, P has no claim against R for cure based on the charging-time discrepancy — unlike Question 5, where the seller failed to correct the manufacturer's claim and the claim did influence the buyer, here both release conditions under Section 434 III 3 BGB are met and no defect arises from the advertising.

### Question 25 (Case — integrates Week 08 + Week 06)

**Facts:** Online furniture retailer F's standard terms and conditions, presented to every buyer at checkout via a clearly labeled "Terms and Conditions" checkbox that must be ticked before payment, include the clause: "All items are sold with warranty rights fully excluded; the buyer accepts all goods 'as is.'" Buyer D ticks the box and buys a newly manufactured dining table from F, which arrives with a cracked leg — a material defect existing at transfer of risk. D demands cure; F points to the clause.

**Question:** Can F rely on the "as is" clause to deny D's cure claim?

**Model Answer:**

I. D could have a claim against F for cure under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB unless F's "as is" clause validly excludes it — which requires both that the clause is validly incorporated and passes SBT content control, and that no overriding rule such as Section 444 BGB blocks reliance on it regardless.

II. This requires: first, a valid purchase agreement and material defect at transfer of risk (established); second, that the clause was validly incorporated as a standard business term under Section 305 BGB; third, that the clause survives content control under Sections 307-309 BGB, in particular Section 309 No. 8 BGB for newly manufactured goods; fourth, that Section 444 BGB does not independently override even a valid exclusion.

III. Application:
1. Defect: the cracked leg is a material defect under Section 434 III BGB, existing at transfer of risk under Section 446 BGB; no dispute on this point.
2. Incorporation: the clause was clearly presented and D affirmatively accepted it via the checkbox before payment, satisfying Section 305 II BGB's incorporation requirements (clear reference, reasonable opportunity to review, agreement); it is a pre-formulated term used for a multitude of contracts, so Section 305 I BGB applies and it is a standard business term.
3. Content control: Questionable is whether a blanket "as is" exclusion of all warranty rights survives content control. This is supported by Section 309 No. 8 BGB, part of the "black list" of clauses invalid without any room for evaluation, which specifically restricts clauses excluding or limiting warranty rights for newly manufactured goods sold as new — exactly this transaction, since the table is newly manufactured. Against this, one could argue F is free to define what it sells "as is" through ordinary contract terms. However, Section 309 No. 8 BGB exists precisely to stop blanket exclusions like this one from being imposed via standard terms on new-goods sales, and a clause that fails content control is invalid under Section 306 I, II BGB, with statutory law filling the resulting gap rather than the whole contract failing. Consequently, the "as is" exclusion clause is invalid under Section 309 No. 8 BGB.
4. Section 444 overlay: even if the clause had somehow survived content control, nothing in the facts suggests fraud or a guarantee here, so Section 444 BGB is not independently needed to defeat the clause on these particular facts — the SBT content-control failure alone is dispositive.

IV. The "as is" exclusion clause is invalid under Section 309 No. 8 BGB; the rest of the contract remains valid under Section 306 BGB, and statutory warranty law fills the gap left by the invalid clause.

V. As a result, F cannot rely on the "as is" clause to deny D's claim; D retains a cure claim against F under Sections 437 No. 1, 439 BGB for the cracked table leg, because the blanket warranty-exclusion clause fails SBT content control under Section 309 No. 8 BGB for newly manufactured goods.
