## Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 1

What is a GmbH, and what makes it the central limited-liability form for small and medium-sized businesses?

**Model Answer:**

1. A GmbH is an independent legal person and limited-liability company governed by the GmbHG, in which shareholders are generally not personally liable once their capital contribution duties are fulfilled.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 13 GmbHG (legal personality and asset separation); Section 5 GmbHG (minimum share capital).
3. Requirements/features: (1) formed through articles of association, notarization, capital payment, and constitutive registration; (2) minimum share capital of EUR 25,000; (3) represented by one or more managing directors under Section 35 GmbHG.
4. Example: two engineers found "BuildTech GmbH" to limit their personal exposure to product-liability risk from selling construction software.
5. Consequence/trap: "limited liability" means the company remains fully liable with its own assets — shareholders are not partially liable, they are not personally liable at all once contribution duties are met.

### Question 2

Compare GmbH governance and AG governance.

**Model Answer:**

GmbH governance triggers when a small/medium business wants flexible, more directly shareholder-controlled management: the shareholder meeting can instruct directors, and directors (Section 35 GmbHG) both manage and represent the company, subject only to internal limits that do not bind third parties (Section 37 GmbHG). Leading to: a comparatively fast, cheap, adaptable governance structure suited to closely held businesses.

AG governance triggers when a company wants broad, transferable investor participation and capital-market readiness: it uses a mandatory dualistic system (Sections 84, 90, 101, 111, 119 AktG) — the general assembly elects/dismisses the supervisory board and makes fundamental decisions, the supervisory board appoints/dismisses and supervises the management board, and the management board manages and represents the AG independently. Leading to: a standardized, rigid governance structure that protects dispersed shareholders and supports share trading, at the cost of direct shareholder control over daily management.

Contrast example: a GmbH shareholder meeting can vote to directly instruct a director to reject a specific contract; an AG general assembly cannot instruct the management board the same way — its lever is electing the supervisory board, which in turn supervises (not instructs) the management board.

Exam trap: do not give AG shareholders GmbH-style instruction power over daily management decisions.

### Question 3

List the requirements for forming a GmbH.

**Model Answer:**

The requirements are:

1. Draft the articles of association (mandatory content under Section 3 I GmbHG: business name, company object, registered office, managing-director appointment, share capital).
2. Notarize the articles of association before a notary; any later amendment needs notarization again.
3. Pay the share capital (minimum EUR 25,000; a portion, commonly at least EUR 12,500, is typically paid in before registration).
4. Register the GmbH in the commercial register — this step is constitutive.

If these are met, the consequence is that a fully formed GmbH legal person exists from registration onward; before registration, there is no full GmbH even with notarized articles and paid-in capital.

The rule exists because each step protects a different interest: articles set internal governance certainty, notarization ensures deliberate and evidenced formation, capital payment protects creditors, and constitutive registration gives third parties a reliable public marker of when the limited-liability shield actually starts.

### Question 4

Three university friends want to launch a specialty coffee-roasting company. They expect to import green coffee (supply risk), sign multi-year lease and equipment-financing contracts, and hope to bring in an outside investor within two years, but currently have only about EUR 8,000 combined in savings. Recommend a legal form.

**Model Answer:**

1. Business scale and risk: capital-intensive early operations (equipment, leases, import contracts), meaningful contractual and supply risk, and an anticipated future capital raise from an outside investor — this points away from an unlimited-liability form.
2. Default/simple form: given only EUR 8,000 in savings, a full GmbH (EUR 25,000 minimum share capital) is not immediately affordable — the natural default at this capital level is a GbR, but that leaves all three founders personally, unlimitedly liable for lease and import-contract risk.
3. Alternative(s) considered: an oHG would formalize the commercial partnership but keeps full personal liability, a poor fit given the leasing/import risk; a UG (Unternehmergesellschaft) allows formation with far less capital while still shielding personal assets.
4. Liability/governance/formation cost/financing comparison: the UG limits liability to company assets and matches the low starting capital (cash-only contribution, paid in full immediately), but carries a mandatory 25% annual-surplus reserve requirement (Section 5a III GmbHG) until it can convert into a full GmbH, has lower reputation with lenders/investors, and banks may still request personal guarantees.
5. Recommendation: form a UG now to secure liability protection immediately, with an explicit plan to convert to a full GmbH once the reserve (plus any future investor capital injection) reaches EUR 25,000 — rejecting a bare GbR (unacceptable personal liability given import/lease risk) and an immediate GmbH (unaffordable at EUR 8,000 combined capital).

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 5 (Case)

**Facts:** A and B jointly run a mid-size furniture import and wholesale business under the name "Nordwood Handel." The business generates significant recurring revenue, employs five staff, and is entered in the commercial register as an oHG. A signs a EUR 40,000 supply contract with a Polish timber mill on behalf of the business. The business later becomes insolvent and cannot pay. The timber mill wants to know if it can pursue B's personal assets even though B never personally signed anything.

**Question:** Does the timber mill have a claim against B personally for the EUR 40,000?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: The timber mill could have a claim against B personally for EUR 40,000 under the oHG personal-liability rule if B is a partner in an oHG whose partner validly bound the partnership to the supply contract.

II. Requirements:
- I. Nordwood Handel qualifies as an oHG.
- II. A validly bound the oHG to the EUR 40,000 contract.
- III. oHG partners are personally liable for partnership debts.
- IV. Legal consequence for B.

III. Application:

- I. Questionable is whether Nordwood Handel is an oHG. This is supported by the facts stating it is a commercial business (furniture import/wholesale, recurring revenue, five staff) entered in the commercial register — matching the requirement of a registered commercial general partnership. Consequently, this requirement is met.
- II. Questionable is whether A's signature validly bound the partnership. As a partner in a commercial partnership, A ordinarily has representation power for the partnership's business; nothing in the facts suggests A lacked authority to sign an ordinary supply contract for the business. Consequently, this requirement is met.
- III. Questionable is whether B, who never personally signed, is nonetheless liable. Against this, one could argue B's lack of personal involvement should shield them. However, the defining feature of an oHG is that every partner is personally liable for partnership obligations, regardless of which partner concluded the specific transaction — unlike a GmbH, there is no built-in liability shield. Consequently, this requirement is met.
- IV. Since the oHG itself owes EUR 40,000 and cannot pay, the timber mill may pursue any partner's private assets, including B's.

IV. Interim result: all requirements met.

V. Final answer: As a result, the timber mill can claim the EUR 40,000 from B personally, because B is a personally, unlimitedly liable partner in the oHG that validly incurred the debt.

### Question 6 (Case — integrates Week 12-13 + Week 07)

**Facts:** C is the sole managing director of Bright Ideas GmbH. The GmbH's articles of association state that any contract above EUR 50,000 requires prior approval from the shareholder meeting. Without seeking that approval, C signs a EUR 120,000 consulting contract on behalf of the GmbH with "Bright Ideas GmbH" as one party — and C personally as the other party, since C also operates a private consulting practice on the side. The shareholders were never asked and never learned of the arrangement until after the work began.

**Question:** (a) Is the GmbH bound to the EUR 120,000 contract despite the missing shareholder approval? (b) Is the contract between C-as-director and C-as-private-consultant valid?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: Bright Ideas GmbH could be bound to the EUR 120,000 contract under Section 35 GmbHG if C had representation power as managing director, and the contract could additionally be invalid under Section 181 BGB if C contracted with themselves without a valid exception.

II. Requirements:
- I. C's general representation power as GmbH director (Section 35 GmbHG).
- II. Effect of the internal EUR 50,000 approval requirement (Section 37 GmbHG / can-do vs. may-do).
- III. Self-dealing under Section 181 BGB.
- IV. Legal consequence.

III. Application:

- I. Questionable is whether C, as sole managing director, has power to represent the GmbH externally. This is supported by Section 35 GmbHG, under which the GmbH is represented by its director(s); as sole director, C holds full external representation power — this mirrors the Section 164 I BGB agency requirement of acting within a power of representation (Week 07), except here the power is organ-based rather than an ordinary Vollmacht. Consequently, this requirement is met.
- II. Questionable is whether the missing shareholder approval defeats the contract's validity toward the consulting counterparty. Against this, one could argue that a EUR 50,000 threshold set in the articles should control any larger transaction. However, Section 37 GmbHG provides that internal limitations on a director's representation power generally do not affect its external validity — the same can-do/may-do split used in ordinary agency (Week 07, Section 164 I framework): C's external "can do" stays intact, and the shareholder-approval breach only creates internal recourse against C (e.g., under the Section 43 I GmbHG duty of care/loyalty), not external invalidity. Consequently, this requirement (validity toward the outside contract) is met — the GmbH remains bound in principle on this point alone.
- III. Questionable is whether the contract is nonetheless invalid because C appears on both sides — as the GmbH's representative and as the private consultant. This is supported by Section 181 BGB, which by default bars a representative from contracting with themselves (self-contracting) unless an exception applies (prior permission, later ratification, performance of an existing obligation, or a transaction legally advantageous only to the principal) — the same self-dealing rule tested in ordinary agency (Week 07). None of these exceptions is shown on the facts: the shareholders gave no prior permission and did not ratify, this is a new obligation rather than performance of an existing one, and a EUR 120,000 consulting contract is not purely beneficial to the GmbH. Consequently, this requirement (a Section 181 exception applying) is not met, so the self-dealing prohibition applies and the contract is provisionally invalid.
- IV. Because Section 181 independently blocks the transaction, the internal-limit analysis under II does not save it — self-dealing is a stronger defect than an ordinary internal-instruction breach, since it removes the independent counterparty bargain the can-do/may-do split assumes (Week 07 nuance: Section 181 can restrict external power itself, not just create internal liability).

IV. Interim result: representation power exists (I, II), but Section 181 self-dealing is not excused (III) — the contract is provisionally invalid.

V. Final answer: As a result, (a) the missing shareholder approval alone would not have stopped the GmbH from being bound, because internal limits under Section 37 GmbHG do not defeat external representation power; but (b) the contract is nonetheless provisionally invalid under Section 181 BGB because C contracted with themselves without any applicable exception, unless the shareholders now ratify it.

### Question 7 (Case)

**Facts:** Solaris AG's supervisory board, frustrated with slow decision-making, passes a resolution directly instructing the management board to reject a specific EUR 2 million supply contract with a named vendor, and further instructs the management board to instead sign with a vendor the supervisory board prefers. The management board complies without independently assessing the decision, believing it is bound to follow supervisory board instructions the way a GmbH director would follow shareholder instructions.

**Question:** Was the supervisory board entitled to instruct the management board this way, and does the management board's compliance affect its own responsibility for the decision?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: The management board's independent management authority could be violated by the supervisory board's resolution if AG governance does not permit the supervisory board to give binding instructions on ordinary business decisions.

II. Requirements:
- I. Allocation of functions in the AG dualistic system.
- II. Scope of supervisory board powers.
- III. Consequence of following an instruction the supervisory board was not entitled to give.

III. Application:

- I. Questionable is whether the supervisory board may direct specific business decisions the way a GmbH shareholder meeting can direct a director. This is supported by the AG's dualistic structure: the general assembly elects/dismisses the supervisory board and makes fundamental decisions, the supervisory board appoints/dismisses and supervises the management board, and the management board manages and represents the AG. Consequently, management authority and representation sit with the management board, not the supervisory board.
- II. Questionable is whether "supervision" includes issuing direct operational instructions like choosing a specific vendor. Against this, one could argue a supervisory body naturally has some steering power. However, the AG structure is deliberately more rigid than the GmbH precisely because AG shareholders — and, by extension, the supervisory board they elect — do not manage through instructions; supervision means oversight within its own competences, not substituting the supervisory board's business judgment for the management board's day-to-day decisions. Consequently, the supervisory board's resolution exceeds its proper role.
- III. Questionable is whether the management board is nonetheless shielded because it merely followed the resolution. This is supported by the general principle that the management board manages the AG under its own responsibility; it cannot delegate that responsibility to the supervisory board by simply deferring to an instruction the supervisory board was not entitled to give. Consequently, the management board is not excused and remains responsible for the underlying business decision.

IV. Interim result: the supervisory board's resolution exceeded its role in the dualistic system, and the management board's compliance does not shift responsibility for the decision back to the supervisory board.

V. Final answer: As a result, the supervisory board was not entitled to instruct the management board on this specific contract choice, and the management board remains responsible for its own decision to comply, because AG governance keeps management authority with the management board, supervised — not instructed — by the supervisory board.

### Question 8 (Case)

**Facts:** D forms "QuickFix UG (haftungsbeschränkt)" with EUR 500 in share capital, paid in cash immediately as required. In its first profitable year, QuickFix UG earns EUR 40,000 in annual surplus. D wants to distribute the entire EUR 40,000 to themselves as the sole shareholder, arguing that since the UG only required EUR 500 to start, there are no further capital-related restrictions once the company is running.

**Question:** Can D lawfully distribute the entire EUR 40,000 surplus?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: D's entitlement to distribute the full EUR 40,000 surplus could be limited if the UG's statutory reserve obligation restricts how much of the annual surplus may be paid out.

II. Requirements:
- I. QuickFix UG's legal form and applicable capital rules.
- II. Statutory reserve requirement under Section 5a III GmbHG.
- III. Consequence for the distribution.

III. Application:

- I. Questionable is whether the low EUR 500 starting capital means no further capital rules apply. This is supported by the fact that a UG can be formed with as little as EUR 1 and only cash contributions — but this describes formation, not the ongoing capital regime. Consequently, formation capital alone does not answer the distribution question.
- II. Questionable is what ongoing obligation applies to annual profits. This is supported by Section 5a III GmbHG, under which a UG must place at least 25% of its annual surplus into a statutory reserve until, combined with existing share capital, it reaches the EUR 25,000 GmbH threshold. Against this, D could argue full owner discretion applies once the company is profitable. However, the reserve duty exists specifically to compensate for the UG's low entry capital and protect creditors, and it is mandatory, not optional. Consequently, this requirement is met — the reserve obligation applies to QuickFix UG's EUR 40,000 surplus.
- III. Applying the 25% figure, at least EUR 10,000 of the EUR 40,000 surplus must be retained in the statutory reserve; only the remaining EUR 30,000 is available for distribution to D, subject to any other statutory or articles-based restrictions.

IV. Interim result: the reserve requirement applies and caps the distributable amount below the full surplus.

V. Final answer: As a result, D cannot lawfully distribute the entire EUR 40,000 — at least 25% (EUR 10,000) must be retained in the statutory reserve under Section 5a III GmbHG, leaving at most EUR 30,000 available for distribution.

## Short-Answer / Theory Questions (Continued)

### Question 9

Compare partnerships and corporations as the two basic categories of German business associations.

**Model Answer (Compare Schema):**

Partnerships (GbR, oHG, KG, GmbH & Co. KG) trigger when a business stays close to its individual members: the entity is person-centered, representation is usually tied to the partners themselves, and — with the partial exception of GmbH & Co. KG — at least one partner is personally liable with private assets alongside the entity, while partners are taxed individually rather than the entity itself. Leading to: flexible, low-formality structures well suited to smaller, closely held ventures where the partners are willing to accept personal exposure in exchange for governance simplicity.

Corporations (GmbH, UG, AG) trigger when a business wants the entity to stand more independently from its members: they are separate legal persons represented by dedicated organs (directors for GmbH/UG, a management board for AG) rather than by the members directly, liability is generally limited to the entity's own assets once capital duties are fulfilled, and the entity itself is taxed rather than the members. Leading to: more structured, formality-heavy setups (notarization, minimum capital, registration) that in exchange give members a liability shield and, for GmbH/AG, easier change of ownership than in a person-centered partnership.

Contrast example: an oHG partner who never personally signed a contract can still be personally sued by a creditor for the partnership's full debt (Question 5); a GmbH shareholder who never personally signed anything cannot be pursued for the GmbH's debts once their capital contribution is paid in, because the corporation, not the shareholder, is the liable legal person.

Exam trap: do not assume every partnership lacks any liability shield at all — GmbH & Co. KG is a partnership by category (KG) that nonetheless shifts practical liability risk onto a corporation (the GmbH general partner) rather than a natural person; classify by legal consequence, not just by the partnership/corporation label.

### Question 10

Explain the three categories of GmbH director duties under Section 43 I GmbHG and the consequence of breaching them.

**Model Answer:**

1. Section 43 I GmbHG imposes a bundle of duties on GmbH managing directors because directors control company assets and decisions without being the residual risk-bearers — that role belongs to the shareholders.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 43 I GmbHG; insolvency filing duty under Section 15a InsO.
3. Requirements/features: (1) duty of loyalty — put company interests first, avoid competition with the company and undisclosed conflicts of interest; (2) duty of care — proper day-to-day management and compliance with legal/regulatory obligations; (3) insolvency-crisis duties — once the company is in financial crisis, heightened duties arise, including the duty to file for insolvency under Section 15a InsO.
4. Example: a director who quietly diverts a lucrative supply contract to their own side business rather than the GmbH breaches the duty of loyalty even if the GmbH suffers no other operational failure.
5. Trap: breach of any of these duties can expose the director to personal liability toward the company — do not assume the GmbH's separate legal personality (Section 13 GmbHG) also shields the director's own conduct; the liability shield protects shareholders from the company's debts, not directors from their own duty breaches.

### Question 11

List the requirements a business advisor must work through before recommending a legal form, and state the consequence of skipping this analysis.

**Model Answer (List-Requirements Schema):**

The requirements are:

1. Liability exposure the founders are willing to accept.
2. Formation process burden (notary, registration, timing).
3. Capital requirements the venture can realistically raise.
4. Governance flexibility needed (person-centered control vs. structured organs).
5. Disclosure duties the business is willing to bear.
6. Financing sources and how they weigh reputation, liability, capital, and transferability.
7. Tax aspects (entity-level vs. partner-level taxation).
8. Ease of changing shareholders/partners over time.
9. Reputation signal the form sends to counterparties.
10. Liability pitfalls that survive formation (insolvency duties, director duties, capital rules).

If these are met — i.e., all factors are actually weighed against the specific venture's facts — the consequence is a defensible, fact-driven legal-form recommendation that explicitly rejects at least one plausible alternative.

The rule exists because the "best" legal form is not the one with the fewest formalities or the most familiar name; it is the one whose liability, governance, financing, and cost profile match the business risk — a bare "recommend GmbH" answer without running this list and rejecting an alternative scores poorly precisely because it skips the analysis the multi-factor test is meant to force.

### Question 12

Explain the GmbH & Co. KG structure and why a business might choose it over a plain KG.

**Model Answer:**

1. GmbH & Co. KG is a KG (limited partnership) in which the general partner (Komplementaer) role — which normally carries unlimited personal liability — is filled by a GmbH rather than a natural person.
2. Statutory anchor: Sections 161, 105 ff. HGB (KG/oHG partnership structure); Section 13 GmbHG (GmbH liability limited to company assets).
3. Requirements/features: (1) the KG still needs at least one general partner (here, the GmbH) and one limited partner (Kommanditist, liable only up to their agreed contribution); (2) the GmbH general partner itself bears unlimited liability for the KG's debts, but since the GmbH is a limited-liability company, the practical liability risk for the natural persons behind it is shifted to the GmbH's own company assets; (3) the structure keeps partnership-style features (person-centered governance, partner-level taxation for the KG's share) while functionally capping the exposure of the humans involved.
4. Example: a family logistics business structures itself as "Familie Weber GmbH & Co. KG," where Weber GmbH (owned by the family) is the general partner and the family members hold Kommanditist shares — a creditor pursuing the KG's unpaid debt can reach Weber GmbH's assets as general partner, but not the family members' personal assets directly.
5. Trap: do not describe a GmbH & Co. KG as eliminating unlimited liability altogether — the general-partner role's unlimited liability still exists in law, it is simply borne by a corporation (the GmbH) instead of a natural person; the KG itself remains a partnership, not a corporation.

### Question 13 (Theory — integrates Week 12-13 + Week 11)

Explain why a GmbH is always classified as a merchant under trade law regardless of its actual business activity, and contrast this with the general merchant test.

**Model Answer:**

1. Merchant status under the HGB normally depends on running a commercial business of a certain type or scale (the general Week 11 gateway), but corporations such as the GmbH are merchants automatically, without that scale/type inquiry.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 6 I HGB together with Section 13 III GmbHG (merchant by legal form); contrast anchor: Section 1 HGB (general merchant gateway) and Section 2 HGB (small-business opt-in registration).
3. Requirements/features: (1) under the general Section 1 HGB route, a business becomes a merchant only if it runs a commercial business, presumed unless its type or scope does not require commercial organization; (2) a small business below that threshold can still become a merchant by choosing to register (Section 2 HGB); (3) a GmbH skips both inquiries entirely — Sections 6 I HGB and 13 III GmbHG deem it a merchant purely because of its legal form, regardless of whether its actual activity is small, large, commercial, or even non-commercial in character.
4. Example: a one-person GmbH that only manages a single rental apartment as a passive asset-holding vehicle is still a merchant by legal form, even though the same activity run by a natural person would likely fail the Section 1 HGB commercial-scale test.
5. Trap: do not run the Section 1 HGB scale/type test on a GmbH or AG — that test is for natural persons and non-corporate business operators; for any GmbH, AG, or similar corporate form, merchant status is automatic and the analysis should go straight to Section 6 HGB / Section 13 III GmbHG.

### Question 14 (Case)

**Facts:** "Seaside Charters KG" operates a boat-rental business. K is the sole Komplementaer (general partner) who manages daily operations and signs contracts. L is a Kommanditist who contributed EUR 15,000 as agreed in the partnership agreement and takes no part in management. K signs a EUR 60,000 contract for a new boat on behalf of the KG, and the business later cannot pay. The supplier wants to know whether it can pursue L personally beyond the EUR 15,000 L already contributed.

**Question:** Can the supplier recover the shortfall from L's personal assets beyond the EUR 15,000 contribution?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: The supplier could have a claim against L for the shortfall beyond EUR 15,000 only if L's status in Seaside Charters KG exposes L to liability beyond the agreed contribution, which the KG's liability split generally does not allow for a Kommanditist who has paid in full.

II. Requirements:
- I. Seaside Charters KG qualifies as a KG with K as Komplementaer and L as Kommanditist.
- II. K validly bound the KG to the EUR 60,000 contract.
- III. Scope of L's liability as Kommanditist.
- IV. Legal consequence for L.

III. Application:

- I. Questionable is whether the partnership is validly structured as a KG. This is supported by the facts stating K manages and signs for the business (matching the Komplementaer role) while L only contributed capital and takes no part in management (matching the Kommanditist role). Consequently, this requirement is met.
- II. Questionable is whether K's signature bound the KG. As Komplementaer, K holds the management and representation role for the KG; nothing in the facts suggests K lacked authority to sign an ordinary supply contract for the boat-rental business. Consequently, this requirement is met.
- III. Questionable is whether L can be pursued beyond the EUR 15,000 already contributed. Against this, the supplier could argue that KG partners generally face liability for partnership debts as a class, so any partner's personal assets should be available for the shortfall. However, the defining feature of a Kommanditist under the KG liability split is that liability is capped at the agreed contribution — L's role is precisely designed to separate the unlimited exposure of the Komplementaer from the capped exposure of the Kommanditist, and the facts show L's EUR 15,000 was already paid in as agreed. Consequently, this requirement (liability beyond EUR 15,000) is not met.
- IV. Because L's liability is capped and already satisfied by the paid-in contribution, the shortfall must instead be pursued against the KG's own assets and against K, whose liability as Komplementaer remains unlimited.

IV. Interim result: the KG/Komplementaer/Kommanditist structure is validly formed and the contract validly binds the KG (I, II), but L's liability is capped at the EUR 15,000 already contributed and not exceeded (III).

V. Final answer: As a result, the supplier cannot recover the shortfall from L's personal assets beyond the EUR 15,000 contribution; it must instead pursue the KG's remaining assets and K personally, since K bears unlimited liability as Komplementaer while L's liability as Kommanditist is capped at the agreed contribution already paid.

### Question 15 (Case — integrates Week 12-13 + Week 11)

**Facts:** "Hansen Logistik GmbH & Co. KG" runs a freight business; its general partner is Hansen Verwaltungs-GmbH, whose sole director is H. H, acting as director of the GmbH general partner, grants Prokura to employee P for the KG's freight operations, and P is duly entered in the commercial register as Prokurist with the signature "ppa." P then signs a EUR 90,000 fuel-supply contract on behalf of Hansen Logistik GmbH & Co. KG, exceeding an internal instruction from H that all fuel contracts above EUR 50,000 first be cleared with H personally — a limit P knew about but ignored. The fuel supplier had no knowledge of this internal instruction.

**Question:** (a) Is Hansen Logistik GmbH & Co. KG bound to the EUR 90,000 contract? (b) Whose assets can the supplier ultimately reach if the KG cannot pay?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: Hansen Logistik GmbH & Co. KG could be bound to the EUR 90,000 contract under the Prokura granted to P if that grant was valid and P's internal instruction breach does not defeat the supplier's reliance, with the further consequence question of which assets stand behind the KG's liability given its GmbH & Co. KG structure.

II. Requirements:
- I. Valid grant of Prokura to P for the KG.
- II. Effect of P's exceeding the internal EUR 50,000 instruction (Section 50 HGB).
- III. Whether the supplier's good faith is relevant/decisive.
- IV. Which assets stand behind the KG's resulting liability.

III. Application:

- I. Questionable is whether Prokura was validly granted to P. This is supported by the facts: H, as director of Hansen Verwaltungs-GmbH, held representation power for that GmbH under Section 35 GmbHG, and since Hansen Verwaltungs-GmbH is the Komplementaer with management/representation authority over the KG, H's grant of Prokura on the KG's behalf, followed by P's registration with the "ppa." signature (Section 51 HGB), matches the Sections 48-53 HGB Prokura-grant and publicity requirements. Consequently, this requirement is met.
- II. Questionable is whether P's breach of the internal EUR 50,000 clearance instruction defeats the KG's binding to the contract. Against this, one could argue an internal instruction that P personally knew about should control, since P was aware of the limit and ignored it anyway. However, Section 50 HGB provides that internal restrictions on a Prokurist's power are generally ineffective externally regardless of the Prokurist's own knowledge of them — what matters for defeating the third party's reliance is the third party's knowledge or the abuse being obvious, not the Prokurist's own awareness of the internal rule. Consequently, this requirement (external validity despite the breach) is met.
- III. Questionable is whether the supplier's good faith matters here. This is supported by the facts stating the supplier had no knowledge of the internal instruction — under the Section 50 HGB logic (echoing the Section 37 GmbHG can-do/may-do split used for GmbH directors, Question 6), a good-faith third party unaware of an internal limit can rely on Prokura's broad statutory scope (Section 49 I HGB). Consequently, this requirement is met, and P's breach only creates internal consequences for P (e.g., toward H/the GmbH), not external invalidity.
- IV. Since Hansen Verwaltungs-GmbH is the Komplementaer, it bears unlimited liability for the KG's debts as general partner — but because that Komplementaer is itself a GmbH, the practical liability risk for the EUR 90,000 shortfall lands on the GmbH's own company assets (Section 13 GmbHG), not on H's or any Kommanditist's personal assets, which is precisely the liability-shifting function of the GmbH & Co. KG structure.

IV. Interim result: Prokura was validly granted and its broad statutory scope survives P's breach of the internal instruction given the supplier's good faith (I-III); the KG is bound, and unlimited liability for the shortfall falls on the Komplementaer GmbH's own assets, not on H personally (IV).

V. Final answer: As a result, (a) Hansen Logistik GmbH & Co. KG is bound to the EUR 90,000 contract despite P's breach of the internal clearance instruction, because Section 50 HGB keeps that limit ineffective against a good-faith supplier; and (b) if the KG cannot pay, the supplier can reach the KG's own assets and then Hansen Verwaltungs-GmbH's company assets as the unlimited-liability Komplementaer, but not H's or any Kommanditist's personal assets, because the GmbH & Co. KG structure shifts that exposure onto the GmbH.

### Question 16 (Case — integrates Week 12-13 + Week 01-02)

**Facts:** "MedTech Solutions GmbH" sells a batch of laboratory equipment to the State University of Freiberg, a public university run directly by the state (a public-law institution). The University's purchasing office places the order through an ordinary written purchase order and purchase-price agreement, identical in form to how the University buys equipment from any private vendor. A dispute arises over a EUR 15,000 balance the University has not paid. A junior staff member at MedTech Solutions argues the company cannot sue in an ordinary civil court because "the other side is a public authority," and the matter must instead be a public-law dispute.

**Question:** Is the staff member correct that this is a public-law matter?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: MedTech Solutions GmbH's claim against the University could be a public-law matter only if the University acted with sovereign authority in this transaction, rather than as an ordinary market participant, which the classification test resolves independently of MedTech Solutions' own status as a GmbH.

II. Requirements:
- I. MedTech Solutions GmbH's capacity to contract as a legal person.
- II. Whether the University acted with sovereign authority or as an equal market participant.
- III. Legal consequence for the classification of the dispute.

III. Application:

- I. Questionable is whether MedTech Solutions GmbH, as a corporation rather than a natural person, can validly be a contracting party at all. This is supported by Section 13 GmbHG, under which the GmbH is an independent legal person with its own rights and obligations, fully capable of entering contracts through its director's representation power (Section 35 GmbHG); the GmbH's corporate status raises no barrier to the transaction being an ordinary sale. Consequently, this requirement is met.
- II. Questionable is whether the University's role in this purchase makes the dispute public law. Against this, the staff member's argument rests on the University being a public body and therefore "always" public law. However, the public/private law test does not turn on whether a public authority is a party, but on whether that authority acted with sovereign power in a position of superordination over the other party, or instead acted as an ordinary market participant; here, the University placed a purchase order and agreed a price exactly as any private buyer would, exercising no sovereign authority over MedTech Solutions GmbH — the two parties face each other as legal equals in an ordinary sales relationship. Consequently, this requirement (sovereign-authority action) is not met.
- III. Because the University acted as a market participant rather than with sovereign power, the transaction falls on the private-law side of the classification test regardless of the University's public-law status as an institution.

IV. Interim result: MedTech Solutions GmbH validly contracted as a legal person (I), and the University's conduct in this purchase does not meet the sovereign-authority requirement for public law (II), so the dispute is classified as private law (III).

V. Final answer: As a result, the staff member is incorrect — this is a private-law matter despite the University's public-law status as an institution, because the University acted as an ordinary market participant rather than exercising sovereign authority, and MedTech Solutions GmbH may pursue the EUR 15,000 balance in the ordinary civil courts like any other sales dispute.

### Question 17 (Case)

**Facts:** Three founders form "PrintCraft GmbH." One founder, F, contributes a used industrial printing press as an in-kind contribution instead of cash, declaring its value as EUR 20,000 toward the EUR 25,000 minimum share capital (the other two founders pay the remaining EUR 5,000 in cash). No independent expert valuation is obtained; F simply estimates the press's value based on its original purchase price years ago. An independent appraisal obtained later, after registration, shows the press was actually worth only EUR 9,000 at the time of contribution.

**Question:** Does the overvaluation of F's in-kind contribution affect whether PrintCraft GmbH's minimum share capital requirement was genuinely satisfied?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: PrintCraft GmbH's minimum share capital requirement could fail to be genuinely satisfied if F's in-kind contribution was overvalued, since in-kind contributions must reflect real value to serve the creditor-protection purpose of the capital rule.

II. Requirements:
- I. GmbH minimum share capital requirement (Section 5 GmbHG).
- II. Conditions for a valid in-kind contribution toward that capital.
- III. Effect of an overvalued in-kind contribution on the capital requirement.

III. Application:

- I. Questionable is whether PrintCraft GmbH's capital structure meets Section 5 GmbHG on its face. This is supported by the facts: EUR 20,000 (in kind) plus EUR 5,000 (cash) nominally reaches the EUR 25,000 minimum. Consequently, on paper this requirement appears met.
- II. Questionable is whether F's contribution was validly made as an in-kind contribution. This is supported by the rule that contributions in kind require specification of value and may require expert valuation, precisely because an asset (unlike cash) does not carry a self-evident value — the facts show no independent expert valuation was obtained and F relied on an outdated purchase price rather than current value. Consequently, this requirement (a properly evidenced in-kind valuation) is questionable and, on these facts, not solidly met.
- III. Questionable is whether the later-discovered true value (EUR 9,000, not EUR 20,000) undermines the capital requirement. Against this, one could argue that registration already occurred, so the formal capital requirement should be treated as settled. However, the entire point of requiring value specification and expert valuation for in-kind contributions is to prevent the minimum capital figure from being watered down on paper while creditors are told a company has EUR 25,000 backing it — an overvaluation by EUR 11,000 (EUR 20,000 claimed vs. EUR 9,000 actual) means the company never actually received real value up to the stated minimum, defeating the creditor-protection purpose the rule exists to serve. Consequently, this requirement (genuine satisfaction of the capital minimum) is not met.

IV. Interim result: the capital structure appears to meet EUR 25,000 formally (I), but the in-kind contribution was not properly evidenced (II) and, once its true value is known, does not actually provide real value up to the stated minimum (III).

V. Final answer: As a result, the overvaluation means PrintCraft GmbH's minimum share capital requirement was not genuinely satisfied in substance — only EUR 14,000 in real value (EUR 9,000 press plus EUR 5,000 cash) actually backs the company despite the EUR 25,000 stated on paper, undermining exactly the creditor-protection function that the in-kind valuation requirement is designed to guarantee.

### Question 18 (Case)

**Facts:** N is the sole managing director of "GreenBuild GmbH," a construction-materials supplier. Without informing the shareholders, N quietly starts a side business, "N Baustoffe," that sells the same construction materials to several of GreenBuild GmbH's existing customers at a slightly lower price, using contacts and pricing knowledge N gained through the director role. GreenBuild GmbH loses EUR 35,000 in revenue over six months as customers shift to N Baustoffe. The shareholders only discover the arrangement after review of customer records.

**Question:** Does GreenBuild GmbH have a claim against N for the lost revenue?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: GreenBuild GmbH could have a claim against N for the lost revenue if N's side business breaches the duty of loyalty owed by a GmbH director under Section 43 I GmbHG.

II. Requirements:
- I. N owed director duties to GreenBuild GmbH.
- II. N's conduct breaches the duty of loyalty specifically.
- III. Consequence of the breach.

III. Application:

- I. Questionable is whether N owed duties to GreenBuild GmbH at all. This is supported by the facts stating N is the sole managing director; Section 43 I GmbHG imposes duties of loyalty, care, and (in crisis) insolvency-related duties on exactly this role. Consequently, this requirement is met.
- II. Questionable is whether operating a competing side business breaches the duty of loyalty specifically, rather than merely being an unrelated personal venture. This is supported by the facts: N used contacts and pricing knowledge gained through the director role to sell the same materials to GreenBuild GmbH's own existing customers, undisclosed to the shareholders. Against this, N might argue that running a side business on personal time is not automatically disloyal. However, the duty of loyalty requires a director to put the company's interests first and avoid competition and undisclosed conflicts of interest — directly competing for the company's own customer base using role-derived knowledge, without disclosure or shareholder consent, is squarely the kind of conduct the duty of loyalty is designed to prevent. Consequently, this requirement is met.
- III. Because N is personally liable for breaches of Section 43 I GmbHG duties, and the facts show a quantifiable EUR 35,000 revenue loss traceable to N's competing business, GreenBuild GmbH has a basis to hold N personally liable for that loss.

IV. Interim result: N owed director duties (I), N's undisclosed competing business breaches the duty of loyalty (II), and personal liability for the resulting loss follows (III).

V. Final answer: As a result, GreenBuild GmbH has a claim against N for the EUR 35,000 in lost revenue, because N breached the Section 43 I GmbHG duty of loyalty by secretly competing for the company's own customers using role-derived knowledge, and directors are personally liable for such breaches.

## Short-Answer / Theory Questions (Coverage-Gap Fill)

### Question 19

Explain the GbR as the basic partnership form and state what pushes a partnership out of GbR status into oHG.

**Model Answer:**

1. The GbR (Gesellschaft bürgerlichen Rechts) is the default, most basic partnership form in German law, and functions as the general partnership model that oHG and KG borrow from when no more specific rule exists.
2. Statutory anchor: Sections 705 ff. BGB.
3. Requirements/features: (1) it often arises tacitly, simply through two or more people jointly pursuing a common purpose, without any formal founding act; (2) it is person-centered rather than entity-centered; (3) it is not a corporation, though it is legally independent enough to participate in legal relations in its own right; (4) personal liability of the partners is the default risk, since a GbR carries no built-in liability shield.
4. Example: two people jointly run a small gardening service for private customers without commercial scale — nothing more than that joint activity is needed for a GbR to exist.
5. Trap: do not assume a joint venture needs oHG's formalities (commercial-register registration) to exist as a partnership — a GbR requires no registration and can arise purely from conduct; it only becomes an oHG once the business reaches commercial type or scope, at which point registration in the commercial register becomes required.

### Question 20

List the GmbH shareholder meeting's core competences under Section 46 GmbHG and state the applicable voting-majority rule.

**Model Answer (List-Requirements Schema):**

The requirements are:

1. Appointment and dismissal of directors (Section 46 GmbHG; dismissal specifically operates through revocation of the appointment under Section 38 GmbHG).
2. Appointment of Prokuristen.
3. Assertion of the GmbH's claims against directors.
4. Decisions on the use of profits.

If these are met — i.e., a decision falls within one of these competences — the consequence is that a simple majority resolution generally suffices for ordinary shareholder decisions, while a higher majority is required for more crucial decisions, such as amending the articles of association.

The rule exists because the shareholder meeting is the GmbH's ownership-level decision body, separate from the directors who handle day-to-day management and representation (Section 35 GmbHG) — Section 46 draws the line between what the owners decide directly (appointments, profit use, claims against management) and what is left to the directors to run; treating director appointment/dismissal as anything other than a shareholder-meeting competence, or assuming every shareholder decision needs the higher articles-amendment majority, are both common errors.

### Question 21 (Case)

**Facts:** R and S jointly offer private gardening and lawn-care services to individual homeowners in their neighborhood. They split profits informally, have no written partnership agreement, and are not entered in the commercial register. Their combined annual revenue is modest, and they occasionally hire a part-time helper for larger jobs. A supplier who sold them gardening equipment on credit wants to sue "the oHG" for the unpaid EUR 3,000 invoice, asserting that any two people running a joint business together automatically form an oHG.

**Question:** Is the supplier correct that R and S's joint venture is an oHG?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: The supplier's characterization of R and S's venture as an oHG is only correct if their joint activity meets the oHG's commercial-business requirement, rather than defaulting to the basic GbR form.

II. Requirements:
- I. A partnership exists between R and S.
- II. Whether the partnership qualifies as an oHG (commercial business) or remains a GbR.
- III. Legal consequence for the supplier's claim.

III. Application:

- I. Questionable is whether R and S have formed a partnership at all. This is supported by the facts: they jointly pursue a common purpose (offering gardening services) and split profits, which is all a partnership requires to arise — no written agreement or registration is needed. Consequently, this requirement is met.
- II. Questionable is whether this partnership is an oHG, as the supplier asserts, or a GbR. Against this, the supplier argues that any two people operating a business together form an oHG. However, an oHG requires the business to be commercial in type or scope — the facts show a modest-revenue, private-customer gardening service with only occasional part-time help, which does not read as reaching commercial scale, and R and S are not entered in the commercial register. Consequently, this requirement (oHG classification) is not met; the venture is the basic GbR form under Sections 705 ff. BGB instead.
- III. Because the venture is a GbR rather than an oHG, the supplier's claim must be analyzed and pursued on that basis; this does not mean the supplier is without a remedy, since GbR partners also carry personal liability as the default risk — but the legal characterization "oHG" itself is incorrect, and any argument that hinges specifically on oHG-only mechanics (e.g., commercial-register-based registration effects) would fail.

IV. Interim result: R and S formed a partnership (I), but it is a GbR, not an oHG, because the business does not reach commercial type or scope (II), which affects how the supplier's claim must be framed (III).

V. Final answer: As a result, the supplier is incorrect that any joint business automatically forms an oHG — R and S's venture is a GbR under Sections 705 ff. BGB given its non-commercial scale, though the supplier may still be able to pursue R and S personally under GbR's own default personal-liability risk, just not under oHG-specific doctrine.

### Question 22 (Case)

**Facts:** T is the sole managing director of "Vertex Trading GmbH." The company becomes unable to pay its debts as they fall due, a state that persists for several weeks. T is aware of the situation but continues operating the business as usual, hoping sales will recover, and does not file for insolvency. Two months later, the company still cannot pay, and a creditor who extended credit to Vertex Trading GmbH during those two months — credit that would not have been extended had insolvency proceedings already been opened — suffers a loss when the company's remaining assets prove insufficient.

**Question:** Does T's failure to act expose T to personal liability?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: T could be personally liable to the creditor if T breached the insolvency-crisis duties owed by a GmbH director under Section 43 I GmbHG, specifically the duty to file for insolvency under Section 15a InsO.

II. Requirements:
- I. T owed director duties to Vertex Trading GmbH, including insolvency-crisis duties.
- II. The insolvency-filing duty was triggered and breached.
- III. Consequence of the breach for T.

III. Application:

- I. Questionable is whether T, as sole managing director, owed insolvency-crisis duties. This is supported by Section 43 I GmbHG, under which directors face heightened duties once the company is in financial crisis, including the duty to file for insolvency under Section 15a InsO. Consequently, this requirement is met.
- II. Questionable is whether the filing duty was actually triggered and breached. This is supported by the facts: the company was unable to pay its debts as they fell due for a sustained period (several weeks, continuing into a second month), and T was aware of this but did not file. Against this, T might argue that hoping sales would recover justified delay. However, the insolvency-filing duty is triggered by the company's factual inability to pay, not by the director's optimism about a turnaround, and the facts show T had actual awareness of the persisting shortfall. Consequently, this requirement is met — T breached the Section 15a InsO filing duty.
- III. Because T is personally liable for breaches of Section 43 I GmbHG duties, and the facts trace a specific loss to a creditor who extended credit during the period T should have already filed, T's continued operation without filing exposes T to personal liability for that loss.

IV. Interim result: T owed insolvency-crisis duties (I), breached the filing duty by continuing to operate without filing despite awareness of the persisting inability to pay (II), and personal liability for the resulting creditor loss follows (III).

V. Final answer: As a result, T's failure to file for insolvency exposes T to personal liability under Section 43 I GmbHG (via the Section 15a InsO filing duty) for the loss suffered by the creditor who extended credit during the period the filing should already have been made — this is a distinct duty-breach from an ordinary loyalty or care violation, tied specifically to the company's financial-crisis state.

### Question 23 (Case — integrates Week 12-13 + Week 10)

**Facts:** "Fleet Solutions GmbH" decides to sell a used delivery van it has owned and used for two years. Its sole director, V, negotiates the sale with buyer W and signs the sales contract on the GmbH's behalf; an employee then hands the van's keys and documents to W at the agreed handover. Months later, it emerges that the van had been stolen from its original owner, O, five years earlier — before Fleet Solutions GmbH ever acquired it — by an unrelated third party who then sold it through several hands until it reached the GmbH. W had no reason to suspect anything was wrong and paid full market price.

**Question:** Does W acquire ownership of the van?

**Model Answer:**

I. Claim/issue sentence: W could acquire ownership of the van under Section 929 sentence 1 BGB if the four cumulative elements of a movable-property transfer are met, but this requires examining whether Fleet Solutions GmbH's own lack of authorization can be cured by good-faith acquisition under Section 932 BGB, and whether Section 935 BGB blocks that route given the van's theft history.

II. Requirements:
- I. A disposition agreement between Fleet Solutions GmbH (acting through its director V) and W that ownership shall pass.
- II. Delivery of the van to W.
- III. The disposition agreement still in force at the time of delivery.
- IV. Authorization to dispose (or, failing that, good-faith acquisition under Section 932 BGB).
- V. Whether Section 935 BGB blocks good-faith acquisition.

III. Application:

- I. Questionable is whether a valid disposition agreement was reached given that Fleet Solutions GmbH is a corporation, not a natural person. This is supported by Section 13 GmbHG, under which the GmbH is an independent legal person that can only form and communicate intent through its organs — here, director V, who holds representation power under Section 35 GmbHG; V's negotiation and signing of the sale on the GmbH's behalf functions as the GmbH's own declaration of intent to transfer ownership, just as it would for the underlying sales contract. Consequently, this requirement is met.
- II. Questionable is whether delivery occurred. This is supported by the facts: an employee handed over the keys and documents to W, completing the real act of handover; delivery is a factual act, not itself a declaration of intent, so it can validly be carried out by an employee rather than requiring the director personally. Consequently, this requirement is met.
- III. Nothing in the facts suggests the agreement was revoked before delivery. Consequently, this requirement is met.
- IV. Questionable is whether Fleet Solutions GmbH was authorized to dispose of the van. Since the van was stolen from O before the GmbH ever acquired it, the GmbH itself never validly became owner through the chain of prior transfers, and so it was not authorized to dispose of the van as owner. This requirement is not met on the ordinary route, so the analysis must move to good-faith acquisition under Section 932 BGB — W's good faith is supported by the facts stating W had no reason to suspect anything was wrong and paid full market price.
- V. Questionable is whether Section 935 BGB nonetheless blocks W's good-faith acquisition. This is supported by the fact that O lost possession of the van involuntarily, through theft, five years before it reached Fleet Solutions GmbH — Section 935 kills good-faith acquisition for the true owner's stolen property regardless of how many honest hands it passes through afterward, and regardless of how honest and diligent the current buyer (W) is. Consequently, this requirement (Section 935 blocking good faith) is met against W.

IV. Interim result: a valid disposition agreement and delivery occurred through the GmbH's organ representation (I-III), Fleet Solutions GmbH lacked authorization to dispose (IV), and W's good faith cannot cure that defect because Section 935 blocks good-faith acquisition of stolen goods regardless of how far removed the current possessor is from the theft (V).

V. Final answer: As a result, W does not acquire ownership of the van — despite W's complete good faith and Fleet Solutions GmbH validly forming and communicating its intent to sell through director V's organ representation, Section 935 BGB's lost/stolen block defeats good-faith acquisition, and O remains the true owner; W's remedy lies against Fleet Solutions GmbH under the sales contract, not in ownership of the van itself.
