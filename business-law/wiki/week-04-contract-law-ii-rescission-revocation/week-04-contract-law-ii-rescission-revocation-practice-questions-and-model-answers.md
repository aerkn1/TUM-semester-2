## Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 1

Compare rescission and revocation: what triggers each, what section anchors each, and what consequence follows?

**Model Answer:**

Rescission attacks a declaration of intent that was flawed at the moment it was made, anchored in Sections 119, 120, or 123 BGB, and leads to voidness from the beginning (ex tunc) once effectively declared under Section 142 I BGB. Revocation reacts to a performance problem in an otherwise valid reciprocal contract, anchored in Sections 323, 324, or 326 V BGB, and transforms the original obligations into restitution duties under Sections 346-348 BGB rather than erasing the contract retroactively.

Contrast example: a buyer who typed the wrong quantity in an order (Section 119 I Alt. 2) rescinds the declaration itself; a buyer who never receives the goods on time (Section 323 I) revokes a contract that was validly formed.

Exam trap: do not use revocation for a flawed declaration or rescission for a late/defective performance — the two routes answer different questions (was the declaration itself broken, or did a valid deal go wrong later) and mixing them up misroutes the entire answer.

### Question 2

Explain deceit and duress under Section 123 BGB.

**Model Answer:**

1. Deceit (Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB) is the intentional creation or maintenance of an error about facts in another person, done to induce a declaration of intent; duress (Section 123 I Alt. 2 BGB) is an unlawful threat of a future evil that the threatening party claims to influence, made to induce a declaration.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 123 I BGB, with the one-year time limit for rescission under Section 124 I BGB.
3. Requirements: (1) for deceit, an intentional false representation or concealment of a fact and double causality (the deceit causes the error, the error causes the declaration); (2) for duress, an unlawful threat, tested through the threatened evil, the desired success, and the means-purpose relationship between them; (3) for both, the deceived/coerced party must not yet have confirmed the transaction under Section 144 BGB.
4. Example: a seller falsely claiming a rival offer is lower to pressure a buyer into a discount is deceit; a supplier threatening physical harm unless a contract is signed is duress. Ordinary commercial pressure, such as threatening to end a business relationship over unattractive terms, is usually lawful and not duress.
5. Consequence/trap: unlike error-based rescission under Section 119/120, Section 122 reliance damages do not protect a deceiver or a party using duress — the exam trap is assuming Section 122 always follows a successful rescission.

### Question 3

State the five-step structure required to establish effective rescission.

**Model Answer:**

The requirements are:
1. A ground for rescission and causality between that ground and the declaration issued (Sections 119, 120, or 123 BGB).
2. A declaration of rescission addressed to the correct recipient, generally the contracting partner (Section 143 I, II BGB).
3. Compliance with the applicable time limit — without undue delay for Sections 119/120 (Section 121 I BGB), or within one year for Section 123 (Section 124 I BGB).
4. No exclusion, in particular no confirmation of the transaction after learning of the defect (Section 144 BGB).
5. The legal effect: the declaration is void from the beginning, ex tunc (Section 142 I BGB).

If these are met, the transaction is treated as if it never existed, subject only to possible restitution under Sections 812 ff. BGB and, in error cases, reliance damages under Section 122 BGB. The rule exists because private autonomy should not bind a party to a declaration they never actually intended, provided the flaw is proven and asserted promptly.

### Question 4

Compare revocation under Section 323 BGB and revocation under Section 324 BGB.

**Model Answer:**

Section 323 BGB triggers when a party fails to render, or improperly renders, a primary performance duty in a reciprocal contract (e.g., the seller does not deliver, the buyer does not pay); it generally requires the creditor to set an additional period (Nachfrist) for performance or cure, unless that period is dispensable under Section 323 II BGB (serious and definitive refusal, a fixed-date transaction, or special circumstances). Section 324 BGB triggers when a party breaches an ancillary or protective duty under Section 241 II BGB — a duty collateral to the main performance — and the breach makes upholding the contract unreasonable for the other party; no Nachfrist is required because the breach is not something a second chance would cure.

Contrast example: a machine that simply never arrives is a Section 323 case; a repair technician who insults or injures the customer while performing an otherwise proper repair is a Section 324 case.

Exam trap: do not reach for Section 324 just because a breach "feels serious" — if the breach concerns the primary performance itself (even a badly performed one), it is Section 323, not Section 324.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 5 (Case)

**Facts:** Anna, a bakery owner, wants to order flour for her shop. She emails her regular supplier, Korn GmbH, intending to type "100 kg" but mistakes the number pad and types "1,000 kg" instead. Korn GmbH accepts the order the same afternoon. The next morning, Anna sees the confirmation email, immediately calls Korn GmbH, and explains she meant to order only 100 kg.

**Question:** Can Anna rescind her order?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Issue sentence.** Anna's order for 1,000 kg could be void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB if she effectively rescinds it under Section 119 BGB.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. A ground for rescission with causality.
   II. A declaration of rescission to the correct recipient (Section 143 BGB).
   III. Compliance with the time limit (Section 121 BGB).
   IV. No exclusion under Section 144 BGB.
   V. Legal consequence under Section 142 I BGB.

III. **Application.**
   Requirement I: Questionable is whether Anna's mistake is an error of content (Section 119 I Alt. 1) or an error of declaration (Section 119 I Alt. 2). This is supported by the fact that Anna knew exactly what "100 kg" and "1,000 kg" mean — she did not misunderstand the meaning of the words, she physically typed the wrong sign. Against this, one could argue any numerical slip looks like a content misunderstanding, but however, the mistake occurred in producing the declaration itself, not in Anna's understanding of it. Consequently, this is an error of declaration under Section 119 I Alt. 2 BGB. Causality is met: without the typing slip, Anna would not have declared an order for 1,000 kg.
   Requirement II: Anna's phone call to Korn GmbH the next morning, explaining she meant a different quantity, is a declaration of rescission addressed to the correct contracting partner, satisfying Section 143 I, II BGB even without any fixed wording, since layperson declarations are interpreted broadly under Sections 133, 157 BGB.
   Requirement III: Anna acted the morning after seeing the confirmation — this is without culpable delay, satisfying Section 121 I BGB.
   Requirement IV: Nothing in the facts suggests Anna confirmed the 1,000 kg order after discovering the error, so Section 144 BGB does not exclude rescission.

IV. **Interim results.** All four requirements are met.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, Anna's rescission is effective, and her order for 1,000 kg of flour is treated as void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB.

### Question 6 (Case)

**Facts:** Ben wants to sell his used car to Clara. To get a better price, Ben falsely tells Clara that another buyer already offered EUR 12,000, when in truth no such offer exists. Believing this, Clara agrees to pay EUR 11,500, higher than she originally intended. Two months later, Clara learns from a mutual friend that Ben invented the rival offer.

**Question:** Can Clara escape the EUR 11,500 purchase contract?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Issue sentence.** Clara's purchase contract at EUR 11,500 could be treated as void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB if she effectively rescinds it under Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. Deceit: an intentional false representation of fact.
   II. Double causality: the deceit caused Clara's error, and the error caused her declaration.
   III. Declaration of rescission to Ben (Section 143 BGB).
   IV. Compliance with the one-year time limit (Section 124 I BGB).
   V. No exclusion under Section 144 BGB.

III. **Application.**
   Requirement I: Questionable is whether Ben's invented rival offer counts as deceit. This is supported by the fact that Ben knowingly stated a fact — the existence of a EUR 12,000 offer — that was false, intending Clara to rely on it. Consequently, this is an intentional false representation satisfying Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB.
   Requirement II: Clara's belief in the fictitious offer caused her to misjudge the market value of the car, and that misjudgment caused her to agree to a higher price than intended. Both links of causality are present.
   Requirement III: Once Clara raises the issue with Ben (declares rescission), this satisfies Section 143 BGB; no particular form is required.
   Requirement IV: Clara has up to one year from discovering the deceit to rescind (Section 124 I BGB) — she is still within time two months after learning the truth.
   Requirement V: Nothing indicates Clara confirmed the contract after learning of Ben's lie, so Section 144 BGB does not bar rescission.

IV. **Interim results.** All requirements are met once Clara actually declares rescission to Ben.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, if Clara declares rescission within the one-year period, the EUR 11,500 purchase contract is treated as void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB, and any exchanged performance is unwound; Clara cannot additionally claim Section 122 reliance damages, since that protection does not extend to a deceiver like Ben.

### Question 7 (Case)

**Facts:** Café owner Dana orders a custom espresso machine from Roast Technik for EUR 8,000, due for delivery by June 1 so the café can open on schedule. June 1 passes with no delivery and no communication from Roast Technik. On June 3, Dana calls, gives Roast Technik until June 10 to deliver, and states clearly that she has no further use for the machine after that. June 10 passes; the machine still has not arrived.

**Question:** Can Dana get out of the contract and recover any payment already made?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Issue sentence.** Dana could have a right to revoke the purchase contract under Section 323 I BGB, entitling her to restitution under Sections 346-348 BGB.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. A reciprocal contract.
   II. A due and enforceable performance obligation.
   III. Non-performance or improper performance.
   IV. An additional period for performance, unless dispensable.
   V. A declaration of revocation (Section 349 BGB) and its effects (Sections 346-348 BGB).

III. **Application.**
   Requirement I: The contract for the custom espresso machine is a reciprocal purchase-type agreement — Roast Technik owes the machine, Dana owes the price — satisfying the "I give, you give" structure.
   Requirement II: Delivery was due by June 1, a specific agreed date, so the obligation was due and enforceable from that point.
   Requirement III: Roast Technik did not deliver by June 1 or at any point afterward — non-performance is established.
   Requirement IV: Dana set an additional period running to June 10 on June 3, which is a reasonable further chance to perform. Even though an additional period was actually given here (so dispensability under Section 323 II BGB does not need to be argued), the facts would also support dispensability given the fixed date tied to the café's opening.
   Requirement V: Dana's statement that she "has no further use for the machine" after June 10, once that date passes without delivery, functions as a valid declaration of revocation under Section 349 BGB.

IV. **Interim results.** All requirements for revocation under Section 323 I BGB are met, and revocation was validly declared.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, Dana can revoke the contract; her payment obligation falls away, and any amount already paid must be returned to her under Sections 346-348 BGB, with return duties performed concurrently under Section 348 BGB.

### Question 8 (Case — integrates Week 04 + Week 06)

**Facts:** Merchant Petra orders a packaging machine from manufacturer Fassel GmbH. During the sales visit, Fassel's agent verbally assures Petra the machine processes "up to 500 units per hour," and this throughput figure is the main reason Petra chooses this machine over a competitor's. The written supply contract Fassel presents — its standard pre-printed 20-page form used for every customer — contains a clause buried in Section 14, in the same small font as the rest of the document, stating: "Any dispute regarding technical specifications communicated verbally during the sales process is excluded from this contract." After delivery, Petra discovers the machine only processes 300 units per hour, and it later emerges that Fassel's agent knew the true figure was 300 and quoted 500 anyway to close the sale. Petra wants out of the contract entirely.

**Question:** Can Petra escape the contract, and does Fassel's Section 14 clause block her from raising the throughput issue?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Issue sentence.** Petra's purchase contract could be void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB if she effectively rescinds under Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB; separately, Fassel's Section 14 clause could fail to become part of the contract, or could be ineffective, under the Standard Business Terms regime (Sections 305 ff. BGB).

II. **Requirements.**
   I. Rescission for deceit: an intentional false representation and double causality.
   II. Whether the Section 14 clause qualifies as SBT (Section 305 I BGB).
   III. Whether the clause is incorporated, or excluded as surprising (Section 305c I BGB).
   IV. Whether the clause survives content control (Sections 307-309 BGB) if incorporated.
   V. Final routing: which claim/defense actually helps Petra.

III. **Application.**
   Requirement I: Questionable is whether Fassel's throughput statement is deceit. This is supported by the fact that Fassel's agent knew the true figure was 300 units per hour and stated 500 anyway, specifically to induce Petra's purchase — an intentional false representation of fact. Double causality is met: the false figure caused Petra's belief that the machine met her needs, and that belief caused her decision to buy. Consequently, Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB is satisfied, independent of any contract clause.
   Requirement II: Fassel's 20-page form is used for every customer, was drafted in advance, and was presented unilaterally by Fassel — this satisfies the Section 305 I BGB definition of Standard Business Terms; nothing suggests Petra individually negotiated the clause, so Section 305 I sentence 3 does not remove SBT status.
   Requirement III: Questionable is whether the clause was properly incorporated or excluded as surprising. Against incorporation, one could argue the clause is buried in Section 14 of a 20-page document in ordinary font, disclaiming exactly the kind of pre-contractual assurance that drove Petra's purchase decision — an unusual placement and content that Petra need not have expected. However, if Fassel can show the clause was clearly flagged and reviewable, incorporation under Section 305 II BGB might still succeed; on these facts, the buried placement combined with its content (waiving liability for representations central to the sale) supports treating it as a surprising clause under Section 305c I BGB, meaning it does not become part of the contract at all.
   Requirement IV: Even if the clause were incorporated, a clause attempting to exclude liability for the seller's own deceit conflicts with the same public-policy logic that bars excluding liability for fraud elsewhere in the code (compare Section 444 BGB's fraud override in warranty law) — such a clause unreasonably disadvantages the other party under Section 307 BGB and would fail content control regardless.
   Requirement V: Because the clause fails on either route (non-incorporation or content control), it cannot block Petra's rescission claim; the clause, if it fell away, would leave the rest of the contract in place under Section 306 BGB — but that is irrelevant here because Petra is not merely attacking the clause, she is attacking the entire declaration via rescission.

IV. **Interim results.** Rescission for deceit is independently established; the SBT clause is either not incorporated or fails content control, and in neither case can it shield Fassel from the deceit-based rescission.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, Petra can rescind the entire contract under Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB within one year of discovering the deceit (Section 124 I BGB), and Fassel's Section 14 clause does not prevent her from raising the throughput misrepresentation — the clause cannot immunize a party against its own deceit, whether analyzed as a failure of incorporation or a failure of content control.

### Question 9

Explain the three main error categories under Section 119 BGB and give an example distinguishing all three.

**Model Answer:**

1. Section 119 BGB provides three distinct rescission grounds for a mistaken declaration of intent, each addressing a different point where the mistake can occur.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 119 I Alt. 1 BGB (error of content), Section 119 I Alt. 2 BGB (error of declaration), Section 119 II BGB (error about essential characteristics).
3. Requirements/features: (1) error of content — the declaror says exactly what they intended but misunderstands the legal or factual meaning of their own words; (2) error of declaration — the declaror uses a different sign, number, word, or click than intended, i.e., a slip in producing the declaration itself; (3) error about essential characteristics — the declaror's will and declaration match, but the declaror is mistaken about an essential attribute of a person or thing central to the transaction (e.g., authorship of a painting).
4. Example: believing "a dozen" means 6 rather than 12 is an error of content; typing "1,000" while meaning "100" is an error of declaration; buying a painting believing it is by a famous painter when it is by a student is an error about an essential characteristic.
5. Trap: do not default to error of content whenever a mistake "feels like a misunderstanding" — if the wrong sign was physically produced (typed, clicked, spoken), it is always Section 119 I Alt. 2, not Alt. 1; a mere miscalculation of value or a disappointed expectation about future events is not an essential characteristic under Section 119 II.

### Question 10

List the requirements for revocation under Section 324 BGB and explain why no additional period (Nachfrist) is needed.

**Model Answer:**

The requirements are:
1. A reciprocal contract between the parties.
2. A breach of a duty under Section 241 II BGB — a protective or cooperation duty collateral to the main performance, not the main performance itself.
3. As a result of that breach, upholding the contract can no longer reasonably be expected of the obligee (unreasonableness/intolerability).
4. A causal relation between the breach and that unreasonableness.

No additional period is required because Section 324 targets a breach of trust or a protective duty (e.g., damaging the creditor's belongings, insulting or injuring the creditor) rather than a correctable performance shortfall — giving the debtor "a second chance" makes sense for a late or defective delivery (Section 323), but not for conduct that has already destroyed the basis for continuing the relationship. The rule exists because forcing a party to keep dealing with someone who breached trust in this way, merely to give them a chance to "try again," would not restore what was actually damaged.

### Question 11

Explain what Section 122 BGB compensates, who can claim it, and its two key limits.

**Model Answer:**

1. Section 122 BGB is the reliance-damages provision that follows certain rescissions, protecting the other party's trust in the validity of a declaration that turned out to be voidable.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 122 I BGB (the claim), Section 122 II BGB (the main exclusion).
3. Requirements/features: (1) it compensates negative interest — the position the claimant would have been in had they never trusted the declaration — not positive interest (the benefit of the bargain); (2) it is available to the recipient of a rescinded declaration in error cases under Sections 119 and 120 BGB; (3) Limit one: it is excluded entirely for rescission under Section 123 BGB (deceit/duress) — the deceiver or threatener gets no reliance protection; (4) Limit two: even where available, it is capped at the positive interest, so the claimant can never recover more via Section 122 than they would have gained from a valid contract.
4. Example: a truck-rental cost incurred by a recipient who relied on a now-rescinded declaration (a Section 119 I Alt. 2 typo case) is recoverable negative interest; if a seller deceives a buyer and the buyer rescinds under Section 123, the deceiving seller cannot claim Section 122 damages for their own lost preparations.
5. Trap: do not assume Section 122 automatically follows every successful rescission — check first whether the ground was an error (119/120, where 122 is available) or deceit/duress (123, where 122 is barred).

### Question 12

Compare the default rule and the exception for the additional period (Nachfrist) requirement under Section 323 BGB.

**Model Answer (Compare Schema):**

As a default rule, Section 323 I BGB requires the creditor to set the debtor an additional period (Nachfrist) for performance or cure before revoking — the debtor gets a genuine second chance, since revocation is a serious remedy that unwinds the contract. As an exception, Section 323 II BGB dispenses with this requirement in three situations: a serious and definitive refusal to perform, a fixed-date (absolute) transaction where the date was essential to the deal, or other special circumstances that, weighed against both parties' interests, justify immediate revocation. Contrast example: a buyer who simply hasn't paid by an ordinary due date must be given a further deadline before the seller can revoke; a wedding planner who ordered a custom cake for a specific Saturday wedding, and who is told two days before that the baker refuses outright to deliver, need not set a further deadline because the serious-refusal and fixed-date exceptions both apply. Exam trap: do not treat Section 323 II as optional window-dressing — if none of the three exceptions is argued and supported by the facts, a revocation declared without ever having set an additional period is invalid, regardless of how obviously flawed the debtor's performance was.

### Question 13

Explain the Section 326 V BGB revocation route and why it requires no additional period.

**Model Answer:**

1. Section 326 V BGB is the third revocation ground, alongside Sections 323 and 324 BGB, and applies when the debtor's duty to perform is excluded altogether.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 326 V BGB, generally triggered together with Section 275 I-III BGB (impossibility or its equivalents).
3. Requirements/features: (1) a reciprocal contract; (2) the debtor's performance duty is excluded under Section 275 I-III BGB — most commonly genuine impossibility, but also the disproportionate-effort and personal-unconscionability variants of Section 275 II-III; (3) once performance is excluded, the creditor may revoke without first setting an additional period.
4. Example: if the unique item a seller promised to deliver is destroyed before delivery, the seller's performance duty is excluded under Section 275 I BGB, and the buyer may revoke immediately under Section 326 V BGB.
5. Trap: do not ask whether the debtor was given "a fair second chance" in a Section 326 V case — an additional period is conceptually pointless once performance is genuinely impossible, so no Nachfrist analysis belongs in this route at all, unlike Section 323.

### Question 14 (Case)

**Facts:** Retailer Theo asks his new employee, Milo, to phone in an order to supplier Solvent AG for "200 boxes" of packaging tape. Milo, misreading his own handwritten note, tells Solvent AG's order line "2,000 boxes." Solvent AG confirms the order for 2,000 boxes the same day. Theo discovers the mistake three days later when he receives the shipping notice, and calls Solvent AG immediately to say the order should have been for 200 boxes.

**Question:** Can Theo rescind the order for 2,000 boxes?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Issue sentence.** Theo's order for 2,000 boxes could be void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB if he effectively rescinds it under Section 120 BGB.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. A transmission error and causality.
   II. A declaration of rescission to the correct recipient (Section 143 BGB).
   III. Compliance with the time limit (Section 121 BGB).
   IV. No exclusion under Section 144 BGB.
   V. Legal consequence under Section 142 I BGB.

III. **Application.**
   Requirement I: Questionable is whether Milo's misreading of Theo's note counts as a transmission error under Section 120 BGB rather than an error attributable to Theo himself. This is supported by the fact that Theo correctly formed and recorded his own intent — "200 boxes" — and the change occurred only when Milo, acting as the transmitting person/channel between Theo's intent and Solvent AG, mis-relayed that content. Against this, one could argue Milo is simply an employee whose mistakes are Theo's own responsibility; however, Section 120 BGB is specifically designed to cover exactly this situation — a person or channel entrusted with passing on a declaration who unintentionally changes it in transit — and an employee tasked purely with relaying an order to a third party functions as such a transmission channel, not as the person forming the declaration. Consequently, this is a transmission error under Section 120 BGB. Causality is met: without Milo's misreading, the order transmitted to Solvent AG would have stated 200 boxes.
   Requirement II: Theo's phone call to Solvent AG three days later, explaining the correct quantity, is a declaration of rescission addressed to the correct contracting partner, satisfying Section 143 I, II BGB without any required form.
   Requirement III: Theo acted as soon as he discovered the mistake via the shipping notice — this is without culpable delay, satisfying Section 121 I BGB.
   Requirement IV: Nothing in the facts suggests Theo confirmed the 2,000-box order after learning of the error, so Section 144 BGB does not exclude rescission.

IV. **Interim results.** All four requirements are met.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, Theo's rescission is effective, and the order for 2,000 boxes is treated as void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB; Solvent AG may have a Section 122 BGB reliance-damages claim for any costs incurred trusting the 2,000-box order, since Section 120 is an error ground (not a Section 123 case) and Section 122 protection is not excluded here.

### Question 15 (Case)

**Facts:** Delivery driver Igor works for a moving company hired by homeowner Nadia to move her furniture between two apartments. During the move, Igor properly transports and reassembles all of Nadia's furniture without any damage or delay — the primary performance is complete and correct. However, while in Nadia's apartment, Igor repeatedly makes hostile, insulting remarks about Nadia's belongings and, after Nadia asks him to stop, deliberately shoves a lamp off a table, breaking it. Nadia refuses to pay the final invoice and tells the moving company she wants nothing further to do with them.

**Question:** Can Nadia validly withhold payment via revocation, even though the move itself was completed properly?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Issue sentence.** Nadia could have a right to revoke the moving contract under Section 324 BGB, releasing her from the payment obligation despite the move itself having been performed correctly.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. A reciprocal contract.
   II. A breach of an ancillary/protective duty under Section 241 II BGB.
   III. Upholding the contract is no longer reasonable for Nadia (unreasonableness).
   IV. Causal relation between the breach and that unreasonableness.
   V. A declaration of revocation (Section 349 BGB) and its effects (Sections 346-348 BGB, adapted for services already rendered).

III. **Application.**
   Requirement I: The moving contract, under which the company owes the move and Nadia owes payment, is a reciprocal contract.
   Requirement II: Questionable is whether Igor's conduct is a Section 324 ancillary-duty breach rather than a Section 323 primary-duty problem. This is supported by the fact that the primary performance — transporting and reassembling the furniture — was completed without damage or delay; the problem lies entirely in Igor's hostile remarks and deliberate destruction of the lamp, which are protective/cooperation-duty violations under Section 241 II BGB collateral to the move itself. Against this, one could argue any property damage during a move is simply bad primary performance; however, the lamp-breaking was not a mishap in the course of moving furniture but a deliberate, hostile act unconnected to performing the move correctly. Consequently, this is a Section 324 ancillary-duty breach, not a Section 323 case.
   Requirement III: Igor's continued insults after being asked to stop, followed by deliberately damaging Nadia's property, mean the basis of trust needed to accept services from this company (through this employee) in Nadia's own home has been destroyed — upholding the contract, including payment, can no longer reasonably be expected of her.
   Requirement IV: The unreasonableness flows directly from Igor's conduct — there is no intervening cause.
   Requirement V: Nadia's statement that she wants "nothing further to do with them," combined with her refusal to pay, functions as a valid declaration of revocation under Section 349 BGB.

IV. **Interim results.** All requirements for revocation under Section 324 BGB are met, and revocation was validly declared.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, Nadia can revoke the moving contract under Section 324 BGB despite the furniture having been moved correctly; her payment obligation falls away under the resulting restitution relationship, though she would need to compensate the moving company for the objective value of services she cannot practically "return," while separately, Igor's deliberate lamp-breaking may support a damages claim against the company under Section 325 BGB alongside the revocation.

### Question 16 (Case — integrates Week 04 + Week 05)

**Facts:** Consumer Lena, browsing an online furniture shop from her home, intends to click "Add to Cart" on the standard "ErgoMesh" office chair (EUR 180) but accidentally clicks the adjacent "Leather Executive" chair (EUR 650) instead, and completes checkout without noticing. The Leather Executive chair is a standard, mass-produced model sold to any customer — nothing about it is custom-made. It arrives five days later; Lena immediately realizes her mistake upon opening the box and wants to send it back and get her money back. The shop's order confirmation email included a properly worded 14-day withdrawal notice as required by law.

**Question:** What routes are available to Lena, and which should she use?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Issue sentence.** Lena could be released from the Leather Executive purchase either by rescinding under Section 119 I Alt. 2 BGB or by withdrawing under Sections 355, 312c BGB, and the two routes need to be assessed separately since neither doctrine displaces the other on these facts.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. Rescission: a ground under Section 119 I Alt. 2 BGB with causality, a timely declaration, and no exclusion.
   II. Withdrawal: personal scope (Section 13 BGB consumer), material scope (Section 312c BGB distance contract), no exclusion under Section 312g II BGB, and a timely declaration under Section 355 BGB.
   III. Relationship between the two available routes and practical recommendation.

III. **Application.**
   Requirement I: Questionable is whether Lena's mis-click is an error of declaration. This is supported by the fact that Lena knew exactly what she wanted — the EUR 180 ErgoMesh chair — but used the wrong sign (clicking the adjacent product) when producing her declaration, exactly the pattern of Section 119 I Alt. 2 BGB rather than a content misunderstanding. Causality is met: without the mis-click, she would not have declared an order for the Leather Executive chair. However, rescission also demands a timely declaration "without undue delay" (Section 121 I BGB) once she discovers the error — she must act now, upon opening the box, or risk losing this route.
   Requirement II: Lena is a consumer acting outside any trade or profession (Section 13 BGB) buying from a business (Section 14 BGB) through means of distance communication with no simultaneous physical presence of both parties (Section 312c BGB) — the distance-contract withdrawal right applies. The chair is a standard, mass-produced item, not custom-made, and nothing suggests it is quickly perishable, so no Section 312g II BGB exclusion applies. Since the shop gave proper withdrawal instructions, Lena's 14-day period runs from receipt of the goods (Section 355 II BGB) and has not yet expired five days in.
   Requirement III: Nothing in either doctrine excludes the other — a consumer is not barred from rescinding merely because a withdrawal right also exists, and exercising withdrawal does not require proving any error at all. Withdrawal is procedurally the safer and simpler route: it needs no causality analysis, no proof of what Lena "really" meant to click, and no justification whatsoever (Section 355 I BGB), whereas rescission would require Lena to establish and timely assert an error of declaration, with any delay risking exclusion under Section 121 I BGB. Consequently, while both routes are technically open to her right now, withdrawal is the more reliable path.

IV. **Interim results.** Both a Section 119 I Alt. 2 rescission ground and a Section 355/312c withdrawal right are currently available and unexcluded; they coexist rather than displace one another.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, Lena can get out of the Leather Executive purchase and recover her payment either way, but she should exercise the Section 355 withdrawal right within the 14-day period, since it requires no proof of error or causality and cannot be defeated by an "undue delay" argument the way rescission could.

### Question 17 (Case — integrates Week 04 + Week 09)

**Facts:** Used-car dealer Voss sells a car to buyer Petros, telling him the odometer reading of 40,000 km is accurate. In truth, Voss knows the car has actually been driven 120,000 km and had the odometer rolled back before the sale. Petros buys the car for EUR 18,000. Three months later, a routine service reveals the true mileage. Petros wants to know his options.

**Question:** Can Petros rescind the purchase, claim warranty damages, or both — and how do the two routes interact?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Issue sentence.** Petros could rescind the purchase under Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB, or pursue warranty damages under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III BGB, and the two routes must be evaluated as independent options arising from the same deceit.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. Rescission for deceit: an intentional false representation and double causality.
   II. Warranty damages: a defect at transfer of risk (Section 434 BGB, referenced via Section 437), seller responsibility under Section 280 I BGB, and routing between damages in addition or instead of performance.
   III. Interaction between the two routes.

III. **Application.**
   Requirement I: Questionable is whether Voss's odometer statement is deceit. This is supported by the fact that Voss knew the true mileage was 120,000 km and stated 40,000 km anyway, specifically to induce Petros's purchase — an intentional false representation of fact. Double causality is met: the false mileage caused Petros's belief that the car was low-mileage, and that belief caused his decision to buy at EUR 18,000. Consequently, Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB is satisfied.
   Requirement II: A car with a rolled-back odometer does not have the mileage the parties assumed, which is a defect from the transfer of risk onward. Voss's responsibility under Section 280 I BGB is not merely presumed here but affirmatively established, since Voss knowingly misrepresented the mileage — intent under Section 276 BGB. Applying the hypothetical-performance test, the loss (paying EUR 18,000 for a car actually worth less at 120,000 km) would not exist if Voss had performed correctly by delivering a genuinely 40,000 km car in the first place — this is a non-fulfilment-type loss rather than a consequential loss surviving correct performance, so it routes toward damages instead of performance (big damages, giving up the car and claiming the non-fulfilment loss, or small damages, keeping the car and claiming the value deficit), both under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III, 281 BGB — though since curing "the true mileage" is not realistically possible for a car already driven 120,000 km, an additional period would likely be dispensable, or the claim proceeds via Section 283 if cure is treated as impossible.
   Requirement III: Nothing in either doctrine forces Petros to choose only one route in the abstract — rescission and warranty damages rest on different mechanics (voiding the declaration entirely versus a money claim within a still-standing contract), but Petros cannot recover twice for the same loss. If he rescinds under Section 123, the contract is void ab initio and the warranty regime, which presupposes a standing sale, no longer has a contract to operate on; if instead he keeps the contract and claims warranty damages, rescission becomes moot for the aspects covered by the damages award.

IV. **Interim results.** Both a Section 123 I Alt. 1 rescission ground and a Section 437/280 warranty-damages claim are independently established on these facts; they are alternative routes to relief, not cumulative ones for the same loss.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, Petros may choose either to rescind the purchase under Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB within one year of discovering the deceit (Section 124 I BGB), unwinding the sale entirely, or to keep the car and claim warranty damages under Sections 437 No. 3, 280 I, III BGB for the value deficit or non-fulfilment loss — but he cannot do both for the same loss, and rescission is likely the cleaner route here since it was Voss's own intentional deceit that caused the entire transaction.

### Question 18 (Case)

**Facts:** Farmer Otto orders 50 tons of a specific fertilizer blend from AgroChem for his spring planting, but due to a data-entry slip on AgroChem's order form (filled in by Otto's assistant based on Otto's instructions), the order as submitted specifies "500 tons" instead. AgroChem confirms and begins preparing the shipment. A week later, Otto notices the confirmation states 500 tons, immediately recognizes the error, but decides not to say anything yet because he wants to see if he can resell any surplus fertilizer to a neighboring farm at a profit. Two weeks after that, unable to find a buyer for the surplus, Otto calls AgroChem and tries to rescind the order for 500 tons, saying he only ever wanted 50.

**Question:** Can Otto still rescind?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Issue sentence.** Otto's order for 500 tons could be void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB if he effectively rescinds under Section 119 I Alt. 2 BGB, unless his rescission is barred.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. A ground for rescission with causality.
   II. A declaration of rescission to the correct recipient (Section 143 BGB).
   III. Compliance with the time limit (Section 121 BGB).
   IV. No exclusion under Section 144 BGB.
   V. Legal consequence under Section 142 I BGB.

III. **Application.**
   Requirement I: The data-entry slip that produced "500 tons" instead of Otto's intended "50 tons" is an error of declaration under Section 119 I Alt. 2 BGB — Otto knew exactly what quantity he wanted, but the wrong sign was entered when the declaration was produced. Causality is met: without the slip, the order would have stated 50 tons.
   Requirement II: Otto's phone call to AgroChem attempting to rescind, once made, is a declaration addressed to the correct contracting partner, satisfying Section 143 BGB in form.
   Requirement III: Questionable is whether Otto rescinded without undue delay under Section 121 I BGB. This is supported by the fact that undue delay is measured from when the entitled person becomes aware of the ground for rescission, and Otto became aware a week after the order was confirmed. Against this, one could argue three weeks is not obviously excessive on its own; however, Otto did not merely deliberate briefly before acting — he consciously delayed for two additional weeks specifically to explore reselling the erroneous surplus for profit, which is not the kind of reasonable reflection period Section 121 I BGB tolerates. Consequently, by the time Otto called AgroChem, the delay was no longer "without undue delay."
   Requirement IV: Separately, and even more directly, Otto's attempt to resell the surplus fertilizer, once he already knew about the mistaken 500-ton quantity, is conduct treating the mistaken transaction as one he intends to keep and exploit — this supports finding a confirmation of the transaction under Section 144 BGB, which independently excludes rescission regardless of the Section 121 timing question.

IV. **Interim results.** Requirement I is met, but Requirements III and IV both fail: the rescission was not declared without undue delay, and Otto's conduct amounts to a confirmation excluding rescission under Section 144 BGB.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, Otto cannot rescind the order for 500 tons — his own delay while attempting to profit from the surplus both exceeds the "without undue delay" standard of Section 121 I BGB and independently confirms the transaction under Section 144 BGB, leaving him bound to the 500-ton order.

### Question 19

Explain the difference between a void transaction and a voidable transaction, and place rescission on the correct side of that line.

**Model Answer:**

1. Void and voidable are two different ways a legal transaction can fail to bind the parties as intended, and rescission law only makes sense once the two are kept separate.
2. Statutory anchor: Sections 125, 134, 138 BGB (void from the start); Sections 119, 120, 123, 142 I BGB (voidable, void only once rescinded).
3. Requirements/features: (1) a void transaction never had legal effect at any point — it fails automatically the moment the defect exists, without anyone needing to do anything; (2) a voidable transaction is fully valid and binding unless and until the entitled party actually declares rescission under Section 143 BGB; (3) once rescission is validly declared, Section 142 I BGB deems the transaction void from the beginning (ex tunc) — but that retroactive voidness is a *consequence* of exercising the right, not the transaction's status beforehand.
4. Example: a contract violating a statutory prohibition under Section 134 BGB is void the instant it is concluded, with no declaration needed; a contract concluded because of a typing slip under Section 119 I Alt. 2 BGB is fully valid and enforceable right up until the mistaken party actually declares rescission.
5. Trap: if case facts show a party performing under the contract, invoicing, or otherwise acting on it for a period of time before anyone raises an objection, that pattern only makes sense for a voidable transaction — a genuinely void one was never alive to be acted on in the first place, so do not describe a Section 119/123 transaction as "void" before rescission has actually been declared.

### Question 20 (Case)

**Facts:** Bookseller Rita orders what she believes is a first-edition copy of a rare novel from dealer Kessler for EUR 4,000, relying on Kessler's assurance that it is a first edition. In truth, neither Rita nor Kessler knew at the time that the book was actually a later reprint — Kessler was not lying, he genuinely believed it too. Three weeks after the sale, Rita finds out from an appraiser that the book is a reprint. Believing that any "mistake about a purchase" gives her a full year to act, as she vaguely recalls from a law class, Rita waits another five months before calling Kessler to rescind.

**Question:** Can Rita still rescind?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Issue sentence.** Rita's purchase could be void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB if she effectively rescinds under Section 119 II BGB, but her rescission may be time-barred.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. A ground for rescission with causality (Section 119 II BGB).
   II. A declaration of rescission to the correct recipient (Section 143 BGB).
   III. Compliance with the applicable time limit.
   IV. No exclusion under Section 144 BGB.
   V. Legal consequence under Section 142 I BGB.

III. **Application.**
   Requirement I: Authorship/edition status of a rare book is an essential characteristic of the item in business dealings under Section 119 II BGB, since it directly drives the book's value and the buyer's decision to purchase; the reprint status was mistaken by both Rita and Kessler, and Rita would not have agreed to EUR 4,000 for a reprint. This is an essential-characteristic error, not deceit — Kessler made no false statement he knew to be false.
   Requirement II: Rita's phone call to Kessler, once made, is a declaration to the correct contracting partner, satisfying Section 143 BGB in form.
   Requirement III: Questionable is which time limit governs. This is supported by the fact that Rita's ground is an error under Section 119 II BGB, not deceit or duress under Section 123 BGB — the one-year period of Section 124 I BGB applies only to Section 123 grounds. Against Rita's assumption, one could point to a general sense that "mistake cases get a year to think it over"; however, Section 121 I BGB governs Section 119 (and 120) grounds specifically, and requires rescission without culpable delay from discovery, not within a year. Consequently, Rita's applicable clock is the short Section 121 I BGB standard, not the one-year Section 124 I BGB standard she was thinking of.
   Requirement IV: Rita waited roughly five and a half months after learning of the reprint before contacting Kessler — even without a fixed day count, that gap far exceeds "without culpable delay" under any reading of Section 121 I BGB, so her rescission is untimely regardless of any Section 144 confirmation issue.

IV. **Interim results.** Requirement I is met, but Requirement III fails: Rita applied the wrong statutory clock, and by the correct Section 121 I BGB standard her declaration came far too late.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, Rita cannot rescind — she mistakenly imported Section 124 I BGB's one-year deceit/duress deadline into what is actually a Section 119 II BGB error case governed by Section 121 I BGB's much shorter "without undue delay" standard, and her five-month wait exceeds that standard by any measure.

### Question 21 (Case)

**Facts:** Antique dealer Willem sells a clock to buyer Fenna for EUR 2,200, describing it verbally as "a genuine 19th-century mantel clock." Fenna, an antique-clock collector herself, examines the clock closely before buying and privately recognizes from its movement and markings that it is actually a 20th-century reproduction — she says nothing and buys it anyway at the discounted price, hoping to resell it later at a profit by relying on Willem's mistaken description in the sale documentation. Willem, still believing the clock is 19th-century, later realizes his own error after consulting a specialist, and rescinds the sale under Section 119 II BGB within days of finding out. Willem had already spent EUR 300 restoring a display cabinet specifically to showcase what he thought was a valuable 19th-century piece, money he would not have spent otherwise.

**Question:** Is Willem's rescission effective, and can he recover the EUR 300 from Fenna under Section 122 BGB?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Issue sentence.** Willem's rescission under Section 119 II BGB could be effective, but his claim for EUR 300 under Section 122 I BGB could be excluded if Fenna already knew of his mistake.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. Effective rescission: a ground under Section 119 II BGB, a timely declaration, no exclusion under Section 144 BGB.
   II. Section 122 I BGB reliance damages: recipient's reliance on the declaration's validity, loss within the negative interest, capped at the positive interest.
   III. Section 122 II BGB exclusion: recipient's knowledge (or should-have-known) of the ground for rescission.

III. **Application.**
   Requirement I: The clock's century of manufacture is an essential characteristic under Section 119 II BGB, and Willem's belief that it was genuinely 19th-century directly caused his declaration to sell at the price he set; he rescinded within days of learning the truth, satisfying Section 121 I BGB, and nothing suggests he confirmed the transaction beforehand, so Section 144 BGB does not exclude rescission. Willem's rescission is effective, and the sale is void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB.
   Requirement II: The EUR 300 restoration cost is a classic negative-interest loss — an expense Willem would not have incurred but for trusting his own mistaken declaration was accurate — and it plainly does not exceed the positive interest (the EUR 2,200 sale value), so the cap under Section 122 I BGB is not an obstacle in principle.
   Requirement III: Questionable is whether Fenna's own private knowledge of the reproduction defeats Willem's claim. This is supported by the fact that Fenna, as an experienced collector, actually recognized the clock as a reproduction before buying and said nothing, intending to exploit Willem's mistake — this is precisely the kind of recipient bad faith Section 122 II BGB is designed to catch, since reliance damages exist to protect a recipient's *trust* in the declaration's validity, and a recipient who already knows the declaration rests on a mistake has no such trust to protect. Against this, one could argue Fenna never said anything and so technically "relied" on the paperwork; however, Section 122 II BGB asks about knowledge of the *ground* for rescission, not about outward conduct, and Fenna's silent exploitation of a mistake she recognized is exactly that knowledge. Consequently, Section 122 II BGB excludes Willem's liability to Fenna.

IV. **Interim results.** Rescission is effective under Section 119 II BGB, but the Section 122 I BGB reliance-damages claim is excluded under Section 122 II BGB because Fenna knew of Willem's mistake.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, Willem's rescission is effective and the sale is void from the beginning, but he cannot recover the EUR 300 restoration cost from Fenna under Section 122 BGB, because Fenna's own knowledge of the true reproduction status at the time of sale excludes reliance-damages liability under Section 122 II BGB.

### Question 22 (Case — integrates Week 04 + Week 07)

**Facts:** Wholesaler Corbin grants his purchasing manager, Yusuf, a Prokura to buy inventory on Corbin's behalf. Negotiating a bulk textile order with supplier Lindqvist, Yusuf is shown a fabric sample and told by Lindqvist's sales rep — falsely, and knowingly so — that the fabric is "100% organic cotton," when it is actually a cotton-polyester blend. Relying on that false statement, Yusuf signs the order in Corbin's name for EUR 30,000. Corbin, who never saw the fabric sample or spoke with Lindqvist at any point, later discovers the true fabric composition and wants to rescind, arguing that he personally was never deceived by anyone.

**Question:** Can Corbin rescind the order for deceit, even though he personally never had any contact with Lindqvist?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Issue sentence.** Corbin's order could be void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB if the contract can be rescinded under Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB, which turns on whether Yusuf's deception — not Corbin's own — counts for the rescission analysis.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. Whose knowledge/deception counts where a representative concluded the contract (Section 166 I BGB).
   II. Deceit: an intentional false representation and double causality, tested at the representative's level.
   III. Declaration of rescission to the correct recipient (Section 143 BGB).
   IV. Compliance with the one-year time limit (Section 124 I BGB).
   V. No exclusion under Section 144 BGB.

III. **Application.**
   Requirement I: Questionable is whether Corbin's own lack of personal contact with Lindqvist defeats a deceit-based rescission. This is supported by the fact that Yusuf, holding Prokura under Sections 48-50 HGB, concluded the order in Corbin's name and within his representative authority — under Section 166 I BGB, where a declaration's legal effect depends on knowledge of certain circumstances, or on being affected by a defect of intent (such as deceit), it is the representative's — not the person represented's — knowledge or defect of intent that counts. Against this, one could argue rescission for deceit should require the represented party personally to have been misled; however, Section 166 I BGB exists precisely so that a principal cannot dodge attribution of a defect merely by acting through an agent, nor should the principal need direct contact with the deceiving party to be entitled to relief for a deceit that infected the negotiation. Consequently, Yusuf's exposure to Lindqvist's deceit is attributed to the transaction as if Corbin himself had been deceived.
   Requirement II: Lindqvist's sales rep knew the fabric was a cotton-polyester blend and stated "100% organic cotton" anyway, specifically to induce the order — an intentional false representation. Double causality is met at Yusuf's level: the false statement caused Yusuf's error about the fabric, and that error caused him to sign the EUR 30,000 order.
   Requirement III: Once Corbin (as the represented party entitled to rescind through his own declaration once the ground is attributed to the transaction) raises the issue with Lindqvist, this satisfies Section 143 BGB.
   Requirement IV: Corbin has one year from the point the deceit was discovered — by either Corbin or, per the same attribution logic, Yusuf — to declare rescission under Section 124 I BGB.
   Requirement V: Nothing in the facts suggests the order was confirmed after the deceit was discovered, so Section 144 BGB does not bar rescission.

IV. **Interim results.** Section 166 I BGB attributes Yusuf's exposure to the deceit to the transaction; all further rescission requirements are met once Corbin timely declares rescission.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, Corbin can rescind the textile order under Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB despite never having personally spoken with Lindqvist — Section 166 I BGB attributes Yusuf's deception at the negotiation to the transaction, so it makes no difference that the deceit reached the principal only through his authorized representative.
