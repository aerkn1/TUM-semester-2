## Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 1

Compare rescission and revocation: what triggers each, what section anchors each, and what consequence follows?

**Model Answer:**

Rescission attacks a declaration of intent that was flawed at the moment it was made, anchored in Sections 119, 120, or 123 BGB, and leads to voidness from the beginning (ex tunc) once effectively declared under Section 142 I BGB. Revocation reacts to a performance problem in an otherwise valid reciprocal contract, anchored in Sections 323, 324, or 326 V BGB, and transforms the original obligations into restitution duties under Sections 346-348 BGB rather than erasing the contract retroactively.

Contrast example: a buyer who typed the wrong quantity in an order (Section 119 I Alt. 2) rescinds the declaration itself; a buyer who never receives the goods on time (Section 323 I) revokes a contract that was validly formed.

Exam trap: do not use revocation for a flawed declaration or rescission for a late/defective performance — the two routes answer different questions (was the declaration itself broken, or did a valid deal go wrong later) and mixing them up misroutes the entire answer.

### Question 2

Explain deceit and duress under Section 123 BGB.

**Model Answer:**

1. Deceit (Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB) is the intentional creation or maintenance of an error about facts in another person, done to induce a declaration of intent; duress (Section 123 I Alt. 2 BGB) is an unlawful threat of a future evil that the threatening party claims to influence, made to induce a declaration.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 123 I BGB, with the one-year time limit for rescission under Section 124 I BGB.
3. Requirements: (1) for deceit, an intentional false representation or concealment of a fact and double causality (the deceit causes the error, the error causes the declaration); (2) for duress, an unlawful threat, tested through the threatened evil, the desired success, and the means-purpose relationship between them; (3) for both, the deceived/coerced party must not yet have confirmed the transaction under Section 144 BGB.
4. Example: a seller falsely claiming a rival offer is lower to pressure a buyer into a discount is deceit; a supplier threatening physical harm unless a contract is signed is duress. Ordinary commercial pressure, such as threatening to end a business relationship over unattractive terms, is usually lawful and not duress.
5. Consequence/trap: unlike error-based rescission under Section 119/120, Section 122 reliance damages do not protect a deceiver or a party using duress — the exam trap is assuming Section 122 always follows a successful rescission.

### Question 3

State the five-step structure required to establish effective rescission.

**Model Answer:**

The requirements are:
1. A ground for rescission and causality between that ground and the declaration issued (Sections 119, 120, or 123 BGB).
2. A declaration of rescission addressed to the correct recipient, generally the contracting partner (Section 143 I, II BGB).
3. Compliance with the applicable time limit — without undue delay for Sections 119/120 (Section 121 I BGB), or within one year for Section 123 (Section 124 I BGB).
4. No exclusion, in particular no confirmation of the transaction after learning of the defect (Section 144 BGB).
5. The legal effect: the declaration is void from the beginning, ex tunc (Section 142 I BGB).

If these are met, the transaction is treated as if it never existed, subject only to possible restitution under Sections 812 ff. BGB and, in error cases, reliance damages under Section 122 BGB. The rule exists because private autonomy should not bind a party to a declaration they never actually intended, provided the flaw is proven and asserted promptly.

### Question 4

Compare revocation under Section 323 BGB and revocation under Section 324 BGB.

**Model Answer:**

Section 323 BGB triggers when a party fails to render, or improperly renders, a primary performance duty in a reciprocal contract (e.g., the seller does not deliver, the buyer does not pay); it generally requires the creditor to set an additional period (Nachfrist) for performance or cure, unless that period is dispensable under Section 323 II BGB (serious and definitive refusal, a fixed-date transaction, or special circumstances). Section 324 BGB triggers when a party breaches an ancillary or protective duty under Section 241 II BGB — a duty collateral to the main performance — and the breach makes upholding the contract unreasonable for the other party; no Nachfrist is required because the breach is not something a second chance would cure.

Contrast example: a machine that simply never arrives is a Section 323 case; a repair technician who insults or injures the customer while performing an otherwise proper repair is a Section 324 case.

Exam trap: do not reach for Section 324 just because a breach "feels serious" — if the breach concerns the primary performance itself (even a badly performed one), it is Section 323, not Section 324.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 5 (Case)

**Facts:** Anna, a bakery owner, wants to order flour for her shop. She emails her regular supplier, Korn GmbH, intending to type "100 kg" but mistakes the number pad and types "1,000 kg" instead. Korn GmbH accepts the order the same afternoon. The next morning, Anna sees the confirmation email, immediately calls Korn GmbH, and explains she meant to order only 100 kg.

**Question:** Can Anna rescind her order?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Issue sentence.** Anna's order for 1,000 kg could be void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB if she effectively rescinds it under Section 119 BGB.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. A ground for rescission with causality.
   II. A declaration of rescission to the correct recipient (Section 143 BGB).
   III. Compliance with the time limit (Section 121 BGB).
   IV. No exclusion under Section 144 BGB.
   V. Legal consequence under Section 142 I BGB.

III. **Application.**
   Requirement I: Questionable is whether Anna's mistake is an error of content (Section 119 I Alt. 1) or an error of declaration (Section 119 I Alt. 2). This is supported by the fact that Anna knew exactly what "100 kg" and "1,000 kg" mean — she did not misunderstand the meaning of the words, she physically typed the wrong sign. Against this, one could argue any numerical slip looks like a content misunderstanding, but however, the mistake occurred in producing the declaration itself, not in Anna's understanding of it. Consequently, this is an error of declaration under Section 119 I Alt. 2 BGB. Causality is met: without the typing slip, Anna would not have declared an order for 1,000 kg.
   Requirement II: Anna's phone call to Korn GmbH the next morning, explaining she meant a different quantity, is a declaration of rescission addressed to the correct contracting partner, satisfying Section 143 I, II BGB even without any fixed wording, since layperson declarations are interpreted broadly under Sections 133, 157 BGB.
   Requirement III: Anna acted the morning after seeing the confirmation — this is without culpable delay, satisfying Section 121 I BGB.
   Requirement IV: Nothing in the facts suggests Anna confirmed the 1,000 kg order after discovering the error, so Section 144 BGB does not exclude rescission.

IV. **Interim results.** All four requirements are met.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, Anna's rescission is effective, and her order for 1,000 kg of flour is treated as void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB.

### Question 6 (Case)

**Facts:** Ben wants to sell his used car to Clara. To get a better price, Ben falsely tells Clara that another buyer already offered EUR 12,000, when in truth no such offer exists. Believing this, Clara agrees to pay EUR 11,500, higher than she originally intended. Two months later, Clara learns from a mutual friend that Ben invented the rival offer.

**Question:** Can Clara escape the EUR 11,500 purchase contract?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Issue sentence.** Clara's purchase contract at EUR 11,500 could be treated as void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB if she effectively rescinds it under Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. Deceit: an intentional false representation of fact.
   II. Double causality: the deceit caused Clara's error, and the error caused her declaration.
   III. Declaration of rescission to Ben (Section 143 BGB).
   IV. Compliance with the one-year time limit (Section 124 I BGB).
   V. No exclusion under Section 144 BGB.

III. **Application.**
   Requirement I: Questionable is whether Ben's invented rival offer counts as deceit. This is supported by the fact that Ben knowingly stated a fact — the existence of a EUR 12,000 offer — that was false, intending Clara to rely on it. Consequently, this is an intentional false representation satisfying Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB.
   Requirement II: Clara's belief in the fictitious offer caused her to misjudge the market value of the car, and that misjudgment caused her to agree to a higher price than intended. Both links of causality are present.
   Requirement III: Once Clara raises the issue with Ben (declares rescission), this satisfies Section 143 BGB; no particular form is required.
   Requirement IV: Clara has up to one year from discovering the deceit to rescind (Section 124 I BGB) — she is still within time two months after learning the truth.
   Requirement V: Nothing indicates Clara confirmed the contract after learning of Ben's lie, so Section 144 BGB does not bar rescission.

IV. **Interim results.** All requirements are met once Clara actually declares rescission to Ben.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, if Clara declares rescission within the one-year period, the EUR 11,500 purchase contract is treated as void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB, and any exchanged performance is unwound; Clara cannot additionally claim Section 122 reliance damages, since that protection does not extend to a deceiver like Ben.

### Question 7 (Case)

**Facts:** Café owner Dana orders a custom espresso machine from Roast Technik for EUR 8,000, due for delivery by June 1 so the café can open on schedule. June 1 passes with no delivery and no communication from Roast Technik. On June 3, Dana calls, gives Roast Technik until June 10 to deliver, and states clearly that she has no further use for the machine after that. June 10 passes; the machine still has not arrived.

**Question:** Can Dana get out of the contract and recover any payment already made?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Issue sentence.** Dana could have a right to revoke the purchase contract under Section 323 I BGB, entitling her to restitution under Sections 346-348 BGB.

II. **Requirements.**
   I. A reciprocal contract.
   II. A due and enforceable performance obligation.
   III. Non-performance or improper performance.
   IV. An additional period for performance, unless dispensable.
   V. A declaration of revocation (Section 349 BGB) and its effects (Sections 346-348 BGB).

III. **Application.**
   Requirement I: The contract for the custom espresso machine is a reciprocal purchase-type agreement — Roast Technik owes the machine, Dana owes the price — satisfying the "I give, you give" structure.
   Requirement II: Delivery was due by June 1, a specific agreed date, so the obligation was due and enforceable from that point.
   Requirement III: Roast Technik did not deliver by June 1 or at any point afterward — non-performance is established.
   Requirement IV: Dana set an additional period running to June 10 on June 3, which is a reasonable further chance to perform. Even though an additional period was actually given here (so dispensability under Section 323 II BGB does not need to be argued), the facts would also support dispensability given the fixed date tied to the café's opening.
   Requirement V: Dana's statement that she "has no further use for the machine" after June 10, once that date passes without delivery, functions as a valid declaration of revocation under Section 349 BGB.

IV. **Interim results.** All requirements for revocation under Section 323 I BGB are met, and revocation was validly declared.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, Dana can revoke the contract; her payment obligation falls away, and any amount already paid must be returned to her under Sections 346-348 BGB, with return duties performed concurrently under Section 348 BGB.

### Question 8 (Case — integrates Week 04 + Week 06)

**Facts:** Merchant Petra orders a packaging machine from manufacturer Fassel GmbH. During the sales visit, Fassel's agent verbally assures Petra the machine processes "up to 500 units per hour," and this throughput figure is the main reason Petra chooses this machine over a competitor's. The written supply contract Fassel presents — its standard pre-printed 20-page form used for every customer — contains a clause buried in Section 14, in the same small font as the rest of the document, stating: "Any dispute regarding technical specifications communicated verbally during the sales process is excluded from this contract." After delivery, Petra discovers the machine only processes 300 units per hour, and it later emerges that Fassel's agent knew the true figure was 300 and quoted 500 anyway to close the sale. Petra wants out of the contract entirely.

**Question:** Can Petra escape the contract, and does Fassel's Section 14 clause block her from raising the throughput issue?

**Model Answer:**

I. **Issue sentence.** Petra's purchase contract could be void from the beginning under Section 142 I BGB if she effectively rescinds under Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB; separately, Fassel's Section 14 clause could fail to become part of the contract, or could be ineffective, under the Standard Business Terms regime (Sections 305 ff. BGB).

II. **Requirements.**
   I. Rescission for deceit: an intentional false representation and double causality.
   II. Whether the Section 14 clause qualifies as SBT (Section 305 I BGB).
   III. Whether the clause is incorporated, or excluded as surprising (Section 305c I BGB).
   IV. Whether the clause survives content control (Sections 307-309 BGB) if incorporated.
   V. Final routing: which claim/defense actually helps Petra.

III. **Application.**
   Requirement I: Questionable is whether Fassel's throughput statement is deceit. This is supported by the fact that Fassel's agent knew the true figure was 300 units per hour and stated 500 anyway, specifically to induce Petra's purchase — an intentional false representation of fact. Double causality is met: the false figure caused Petra's belief that the machine met her needs, and that belief caused her decision to buy. Consequently, Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB is satisfied, independent of any contract clause.
   Requirement II: Fassel's 20-page form is used for every customer, was drafted in advance, and was presented unilaterally by Fassel — this satisfies the Section 305 I BGB definition of Standard Business Terms; nothing suggests Petra individually negotiated the clause, so Section 305 I sentence 3 does not remove SBT status.
   Requirement III: Questionable is whether the clause was properly incorporated or excluded as surprising. Against incorporation, one could argue the clause is buried in Section 14 of a 20-page document in ordinary font, disclaiming exactly the kind of pre-contractual assurance that drove Petra's purchase decision — an unusual placement and content that Petra need not have expected. However, if Fassel can show the clause was clearly flagged and reviewable, incorporation under Section 305 II BGB might still succeed; on these facts, the buried placement combined with its content (waiving liability for representations central to the sale) supports treating it as a surprising clause under Section 305c I BGB, meaning it does not become part of the contract at all.
   Requirement IV: Even if the clause were incorporated, a clause attempting to exclude liability for the seller's own deceit conflicts with the same public-policy logic that bars excluding liability for fraud elsewhere in the code (compare Section 444 BGB's fraud override in warranty law) — such a clause unreasonably disadvantages the other party under Section 307 BGB and would fail content control regardless.
   Requirement V: Because the clause fails on either route (non-incorporation or content control), it cannot block Petra's rescission claim; the clause, if it fell away, would leave the rest of the contract in place under Section 306 BGB — but that is irrelevant here because Petra is not merely attacking the clause, she is attacking the entire declaration via rescission.

IV. **Interim results.** Rescission for deceit is independently established; the SBT clause is either not incorporated or fails content control, and in neither case can it shield Fassel from the deceit-based rescission.

V. **Final answer.** As a result, Petra can rescind the entire contract under Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB within one year of discovering the deceit (Section 124 I BGB), and Fassel's Section 14 clause does not prevent her from raising the throughput misrepresentation — the clause cannot immunize a party against its own deceit, whether analyzed as a failure of incorporation or a failure of content control.

## Quick Answer Route Summary

| Question # | Primary Route/Section | One-line takeaway |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Rescission vs. revocation (142 I vs. 346-348) | Flawed formation vs. failed performance — different questions entirely. |
| 2 | Section 123 I Alt. 1/2 BGB | Deceit and duress both need causality; ordinary commercial pressure is not duress. |
| 3 | Sections 119/120/123, 143, 121/124, 144, 142 I BGB | Five-step rescission structure: ground, declare, deadline, no exclusion, ex tunc effect. |
| 4 | Sections 323 vs. 324 BGB | Primary-duty failure vs. ancillary-duty breach — Nachfrist only for 323 by default. |
| 5 | Section 119 I Alt. 2 BGB | Typo/slip = declaration error, not content error. |
| 6 | Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB | Knowingly false statement inducing a purchase is deceit, one-year deadline. |
| 7 | Section 323 I BGB | Missed fixed delivery date + Nachfrist + clear "no longer interested" = valid revocation. |
| 8 | Section 123 I Alt. 1 BGB + Sections 305c I / 307 BGB | Deceit-based rescission survives regardless of an SBT clause trying to disclaim the same misrepresentation. |
