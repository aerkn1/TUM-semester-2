## Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 1

State and apply the three requirements for effective agency under Section 164 I BGB.

**Model Answer:**
1. Effective agency is the legal institute that lets an agent's own declaration of intent bind a principal directly toward a third party, defined in Section 164 I BGB.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 164 I BGB.
3. Requirements: (1) an own declaration of intent by the agent, (2) publicity — the declaration is recognizably made in the principal's name, (3) power of representation.
4. Example: an authorized sales employee negotiates and closes a sale in the shop owner's name.
5. Consequence/trap: if all three are met, the principal and third party become the contracting parties directly and the agent drops out of the contract; missing any single element defeats agency entirely — it does not partially bind the principal.

### Question 2

Compare an agent and a messenger.

**Model Answer:**
1. An agent forms and declares their own legal will within their authority (Section 164 I BGB); a messenger merely transmits another person's already-formed, fixed declaration without adding any will of their own.
2. Trigger: agent status is tested under Section 164 I BGB (own declaration, publicity, authority); messenger status is tested under the rules on transmission of a declaration (Section 120 BGB governs transmission errors).
3. Consequence: an effective agent's declaration binds the principal directly; a messenger's transmitted declaration is legally the principal's own declaration, so its validity turns on whether it was transmitted correctly, not on any authority analysis.
4. Contrast example: an employee negotiating price and terms with a customer is an agent; an employee reading a pre-written letter from the owner word-for-word to a customer is a messenger.
5. Exam warning: do not run the Section 164 I agency test on a messenger — analyze transmission (Section 120) instead, since a messenger never formed an "own will" to test for publicity or authority.

### Question 3

Compare agency by estoppel and ostensible agency.

**Model Answer:**
1. Agency by estoppel exists where the principal knows a person appears to act as their agent and tolerates it; ostensible agency exists where the principal does not know but should have known and prevented the appearance through due care.
2. Trigger: both rest on a third party's good-faith reliance on an appearance of authority created or tolerated by the principal, rather than on an actual declaration under Section 167 I BGB.
3. Consequence: in both cases the law treats the agent as having real power of representation, protecting the good-faith third party's reliance despite the absence of actual authorization.
4. Contrast example: a principal who watches an unauthorized helper repeatedly close sales in the shop and does nothing is bound by estoppel; a principal who never checks and would have discovered repeated unauthorized use of company letterhead and email with ordinary diligence is bound by ostensible agency.
5. Exam warning: a single isolated unauthorized act is normally not enough for either route — the facts must show a pattern or appearance attributable to the principal plus reasonable reliance; do not conflate general negligence with either specific doctrine.

### Question 4

List the requirements (exceptions) that allow a self-dealing transaction to stand despite Section 181 BGB.

**Model Answer:**
The exceptions are:
1. Prior permission — the principal authorized the self-dealing transaction in advance.
2. Later ratification — the principal ratifies the transaction after the fact under Section 177 BGB.
3. Mere performance of an existing obligation — the transaction only fulfills a debt that already exists, so no fresh conflict of interest arises.
4. Legal advantage only for the principal — the transaction brings the principal solely a legal benefit with no corresponding burden.

If none of these applies, the self-dealing or multiple-representation transaction is provisionally invalid and cannot bind the principal. Section 181 exists because an agent who is also the counterparty (or who represents both sides) has a structural conflict of interest, since there is no independent party bargaining against the principal's interest.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 5 (Case)

**Facts:** Retailer R owns a bookstore. R's cousin K sometimes helps out unpaid during busy weekends but has no formal employment contract or job title. One Saturday, a regular customer W asks K to order a rare first-edition book. K agrees to source it and quotes a price of EUR 400, without mentioning R or the bookstore at any point during the conversation — W has only ever met K as "R's cousin who sometimes hangs around" and has no idea K is acting on the bookstore's behalf. K genuinely intends the transaction to be for the bookstore. Three weeks later, R obtains the book, but W refuses to pay the EUR 400, denying she ever agreed to buy anything from R's bookstore.

**Question:** Does R have a claim against W for payment of EUR 400 under Section 433 II BGB?

**Model Answer:**

I. R could have a claim against W for payment of EUR 400 under Section 433 II BGB if R and W concluded a valid purchase contract, which in turn depends on whether K effectively represented R under Section 164 I BGB when negotiating with W.

II. Requirements:
- Contract requires two matching declarations of intent (offer and acceptance).
- If K, not R, made the relevant declaration, effective representation requires: (1) own declaration of intent, (2) publicity, (3) power of representation.
- Legal consequence if representation fails.

III. Application:
- Own declaration: K forms and expresses her own will to sell the book for EUR 400 to W — this is not a mere transmission of R's pre-fixed words, so K is a candidate agent, not a messenger.
- Publicity: Questionable is whether K acted recognizably in R's name. This is supported by K's genuine intent to act for the bookstore and her habit of helping out there. Against this, one could argue W had no way to recognize this from the conversation — K never mentioned R or the bookstore, and W only knew K informally, not as someone acting for the bookstore in this transaction. However, publicity requires that the third party can recognize from the circumstances that the declaration is made for someone else; a private, undisclosed intention is not enough under Section 164 II BGB. Consequently, publicity is not met.
- Power of representation: becomes irrelevant once publicity fails, since all three elements of Section 164 I BGB are cumulative.

IV. Interim result: K did not effectively represent R under Section 164 I BGB; Section 164 II BGB applies instead, and K's hidden intent to act for R is disregarded.

V. Final answer: As a result, no contract was concluded between R and W; K herself is likely treated as the contracting party, so R has no claim against W for the EUR 400 under Section 433 II BGB.

### Question 6 (Case)

**Facts:** Bakery owner B employs C exclusively as a delivery driver; C has no sales authority under her employment contract. While delivering an order, C is approached by a passing café owner, D, who urgently wants to buy B's entire remaining stock of pastries for a same-day event. C, eager to help, agrees to sell D the pastries for EUR 300, explicitly telling D "I'm selling these on behalf of the bakery." When B learns of the sale, B is furious because the pastries were reserved for a different regular customer, and B refuses to honor the deal. D, having already promised the pastries to his own event guests, wants to know his options.

**Question:** Can D demand performance (delivery of the pastries) from C personally under Section 179 I BGB?

**Model Answer:**

I. D could have a claim against C for performance under Section 179 I BGB if C acted as an unauthorized agent for B and B has refused to ratify the transaction.

II. Requirements:
- C made an own declaration of intent in B's name (candidate agency under Section 164 I BGB).
- C lacked power of representation for this type of transaction.
- B refused ratification under Section 177 I BGB.
- D chooses performance (rather than damages) under Section 179 I BGB.

III. Application:
- Own declaration and publicity: C forms her own declaration to sell the pastries and explicitly states she is selling "on behalf of the bakery," so both own declaration and publicity are met.
- Power of representation: Questionable is whether C's authority as a delivery driver extends to sales. This is supported by the fact that C's employment contract covers delivery only. Against this, one could argue any employee visibly representing the business might appear to carry some authority. However, C's role and the circumstances (an improvised curbside sale of the entire remaining stock) give no objective indication of sales authority, and there are no facts suggesting a pattern of C making sales that B tolerated or should have noticed, so no reliance-based authority arises either. Consequently, C lacked power of representation.
- Ratification: B refuses to ratify because the pastries were reserved for someone else, satisfying Section 177 I BGB's refusal condition.
- Section 179 I choice: D may choose between performance and damages; the facts show D wants the pastries themselves.

IV. Interim result: C acted as an unauthorized agent; B's refusal to ratify triggers Section 179 I BGB liability against C personally.

V. Final answer: As a result, D can demand performance (delivery of the pastries) from C personally under Section 179 I BGB, since C, not B, is now treated as bound by the sale.

### Question 7 (Case)

**Facts:** GmbH managing director M has broad, unrestricted power of representation for TechParts GmbH, including authority to buy and sell company equipment. M personally owns an old delivery van that the company genuinely needs. Without informing the other shareholders or seeking any prior approval, M signs a purchase contract "on behalf of TechParts GmbH" to buy his own van for EUR 15,000 — a fair market price. When the other shareholders discover the deal, they argue the contract cannot bind the company because M contracted with himself.

**Question:** Is the purchase contract between M (as seller) and TechParts GmbH (as buyer, represented by M) valid?

**Model Answer:**

I. The contract's validity depends on whether M could effectively represent TechParts GmbH under Section 164 I BGB despite Section 181 BGB's restriction on self-dealing.

II. Requirements:
- M acted as TechParts GmbH's representative (organ representation under Section 35 GmbHG).
- M is personally the counterparty to the transaction (self-contracting).
- Section 181 BGB is triggered.
- Check whether an exception applies: prior permission, later ratification, mere performance of an existing obligation, or legal advantage only for the principal.

III. Application:
- Representative capacity: M has organ representation power for TechParts GmbH under Section 35 GmbHG, so M can, in principle, bind the company.
- Self-contracting: M personally sells the van while simultaneously representing the buyer (TechParts GmbH), so M stands on both sides of the same transaction — this is self-contracting, not merely an internal-instruction issue.
- Exceptions: Questionable is whether any Section 181 exception applies. This is supported by the fact that the price was fair and the company genuinely needed the van. Against this, one could argue a fair price does not by itself amount to prior permission or later ratification, and the facts show no advance authorization from the shareholders and no subsequent approval — they only object after discovering the deal. The transaction is also not the mere performance of an existing obligation, and it is not legally advantageous only for TechParts GmbH, since the company must pay EUR 15,000. However, with no exception on the facts, Section 181 BGB applies in full.
- Consequence: because none of the four exceptions is met, M's self-dealing falls outside his power of representation for this specific transaction.

IV. Interim result: M lacked effective power of representation for this transaction due to Section 181 BGB, notwithstanding his general organ authority.

V. Final answer: As a result, the purchase contract is provisionally invalid; it cannot bind TechParts GmbH unless the company later ratifies it under Section 177 BGB.

### Question 8 (Case — integrates Week 07 + Week 11)

**Facts:** HandelsWaren GmbH is a mid-size wholesale trading company (a merchant by legal form under Section 6 I HGB in conjunction with Section 13 III GmbHG). The managing director grants employee N formal Prokura, duly registered in the commercial register. Internally, the shareholders instruct N never to place single orders above EUR 50,000 without board sign-off — but this limit is never communicated to any customer or supplier. Acting under her Prokura, N signs an EUR 80,000 order with supplier Q for raw materials; Q has no knowledge of the internal EUR 50,000 limit and has dealt with N as HandelsWaren's authorized signatory before. When the shareholders discover the order, they refuse to accept the delivery, arguing N exceeded her authority.

**Question:** Is HandelsWaren GmbH bound to accept and pay for the EUR 80,000 order?

**Model Answer:**

I. HandelsWaren GmbH is bound to Q under the purchase contract if N effectively represented the company, which requires testing both ordinary agency under Section 164 I BGB and the HGB-specific Prokura rules, since HandelsWaren GmbH is a merchant.

II. Requirements:
- HandelsWaren GmbH qualifies as a merchant, triggering the HGB layer on top of the BGB baseline.
- N made an own declaration of intent in the company's name (Section 164 I BGB elements).
- N held Prokura, and the scope of Prokura under Sections 48-50 HGB determines whether the internal EUR 50,000 limit defeats her external power.
- Legal consequence for the EUR 80,000 order.

III. Application:
- Merchant status: HandelsWaren GmbH is a GmbH, so it is a merchant by legal form under Section 6 I HGB in conjunction with Section 13 III GmbHG, without needing to run the Section 1 HGB scale test. This triggers HGB rules, including Prokura, on top of the ordinary BGB agency framework.
- Own declaration and publicity: N forms her own declaration to place the order and signs as HandelsWaren's registered Prokurist, so both elements of Section 164 I BGB are satisfied. This could also have been analyzed as ordinary Handlungsvollmacht under Section 54 HGB, but N's status here is the broader, registered Prokura.
- Power of representation — Prokura scope: Questionable is whether the internal EUR 50,000 cap limits N's external authority. This is supported by the shareholders' clear internal instruction. Against this, Section 50 HGB provides that internal restrictions on Prokura are generally ineffective against third parties — the near-unrestrictable statutory scope of Prokura under Section 49 HGB exists precisely so that third parties like Q can rely on a registered Prokurist's authority without investigating internal limits. However, this protection can fail if the third party knew of the limit or the abuse was obvious; here, Q had no knowledge of the EUR 50,000 cap and had previously dealt with N as an authorized signatory, so nothing on the facts should have put Q on notice. Consequently, the internal limit does not defeat N's external power of representation.

IV. Interim result: N's Prokura gave her full external authority to place the EUR 80,000 order despite the internal cap, because Section 50 HGB protects Q's good-faith reliance.

V. Final answer: As a result, HandelsWaren GmbH is bound to accept and pay for the EUR 80,000 order under the contract with Q; the shareholders' remedy for N's breach of the internal instruction lies only in the internal employment/mandate relationship against N, not against Q.

### Question 9

List the three statutory sources of power of representation and give one example of each.

**Model Answer:**
The three sources are:
1. Statutory authority — power of representation given directly by law, independent of any individual declaration. Example: Section 35 GmbHG gives a GmbH's directors representation power simply by virtue of holding the office.
2. Authority by declaration (Section 167 I BGB) — the principal grants authority through a declaration, either internally to the agent or externally to the third party. Example: P orally tells A, "You may sell furniture for me" (internal authorization), or P tells key buyers "A is authorized to sell up to EUR 10,000" (external authorization).
3. Reliance-based authority — power of representation arising from the principal's own responsible creation or toleration of an appearance of agency, protecting a third party's good-faith reliance; it splits into agency by estoppel (principal knows and tolerates) and ostensible agency (principal negligently fails to notice and prevent). Example: a principal who watches an unauthorized helper repeatedly close sales and does nothing is bound by estoppel.

If none of these three sources is present, the acting person has no power of representation, and the principal is not bound absent ratification under Section 177 BGB — the three sources are independent routes to the same legal consequence, not cumulative requirements.

### Question 10

Explain what happens when a principal revokes an agent's authority but only tells the agent, not the third party.

**Model Answer:**
1. This is a third-party-protection issue arising after termination of authority, governed by Section 168 BGB together with Sections 170 and 173 BGB.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 168 BGB (authority usually ends with the underlying relationship and is freely revocable), read with Sections 170, 173 BGB (protection of a good-faith third party after withdrawal).
3. Requirements/features: (1) authority has been effectively revoked internally; (2) the revocation was communicated only to the agent, not to the third party; (3) the third party neither knew nor should have known that authority had ended.
4. Example: P internally revokes A's authority but never tells regular client Z; A, still holding old business materials, confirms an order with Z, who has dealt with A before and has no reason to suspect anything changed — Z remains protected and P is bound.
5. Trap: ending the underlying relationship (e.g., terminating A's employment) does not by itself solve the external third-party problem — always ask specifically what the third party was told and whether the third party was in good faith, rather than assuming termination alone cuts off external effect.

### Question 11

Compare "lack of authority" and "abuse of existing authority" as agency-law problems.

**Model Answer (Compare Schema):** Lack of authority exists where the agent never had power of representation for the transaction at all, tested through Sections 177 and 179 BGB: the contract is pending until the principal ratifies, and if the principal refuses, the third party may claim against the unauthorized agent personally under Section 179 BGB. Abuse of existing authority exists where the agent does hold valid external power of representation but violates an internal instruction on how to use it (the "may do" boundary), while the "can do" boundary toward the third party remains intact. Contrast example: a warehouse worker with no sales authority who sells furniture is a lack-of-authority case (pending ratification); a sales manager told internally never to sell below EUR 10,000 who nonetheless sells to an unaware buyer for EUR 8,000 is an abuse-of-authority case (principal is bound externally, remedy is internal against the agent). Exam trap: do not lump every "bad" agent action into "no authority" — abuse of existing authority keeps the principal bound externally, and the correct remedy lies in the internal relationship (employment, mandate, service duties), not in Sections 177/179 or in Section 166 BGB.

### Question 12

List the four hidden information problems in the principal-agent problem, with the corresponding agency-cost response for each.

**Model Answer:**
1. Hidden characteristics (adverse selection) — the agent is unsuitable for the role before hiring; addressed by signaling and screening (e.g., certificates, assessment centers).
2. Hidden action (moral hazard) — the agent behaves badly after being hired, where the principal cannot fully observe conduct; addressed by incentives (e.g., performance-linked bonuses).
3. Hidden information (moral hazard) — the agent knows more than the principal can assess about the situation; addressed by surveillance (e.g., dual-control or joint-representation requirements).
4. Hidden intention — the agent's underlying motivation is not aligned with the principal's interest; addressed by limitation of authority, particularly external authorization that tells third parties the boundary of what the agent may do.

Trap: these are information/motivation problems from economics, not separate legal doctrines — the legal agency rules (Section 164 I BGB requirements, Section 181 BGB, ratification) determine who is bound, while the principal-agent framework explains why businesses design authority limits, bonus systems, and monitoring in the first place; don't present the four categories as if they were statutory requirements.

### Question 13 (Case)

**Facts:** Print shop owner O never explicitly authorizes intern I to sign contracts. Over several months, I repeatedly uses O's letterhead and O's business email account to confirm print orders with regular client X, signing "on behalf of PrintShop O." O has easy access to the outgoing email folder and multiple opportunities to notice this but never checks it. X, relying on this established pattern, places a large order with I for a batch of prints worth EUR 2,000. O refuses to fulfill it, claiming I was never authorized to sign anything.

**Question:** Is O bound to X for the EUR 2,000 order?

**Model Answer:**

I. O could be bound to X under a purchase/service contract for EUR 2,000 if I effectively represented O, which turns on whether ostensible agency supplies the power of representation I otherwise lacks.

II. Requirements:
- I made an own declaration of intent in O's name (publicity via letterhead/email).
- I lacked actual statutory or declared authority.
- Ostensible agency requires: an appearance of authority attributable to O (pattern/negligent failure to control) plus X's good-faith reasonable reliance.

III. Application:
- Own declaration and publicity: I forms and signs order confirmations "on behalf of PrintShop O" using O's own letterhead and email account — clearly recognizable to X as acting for O.
- Actual authority: no facts show O ever declared authority to I under Section 167 I BGB, and no statutory route applies, so actual power of representation is absent.
- Ostensible agency: Questionable is whether O's negligent failure to monitor creates reliance-based authority. This is supported by the repeated, months-long pattern of I using O's official channels — not an isolated act — combined with O's easy ability to check outgoing correspondence and consistent failure to do so. Against this, one could argue O never affirmatively created the appearance and merely stayed passive. However, ostensible agency's essence is exactly this kind of negligent failure to prevent an appearance the principal could and should have discovered with due care; O's access to the account and the repeated conduct meet that bar. Consequently, the appearance is attributable to O.
- X's reliance: X is a regular client who has observed this pattern and had no reason to doubt I's authority; nothing on the facts suggests X knew or should have known I lacked authority, so X's reliance is in good faith.

IV. Interim result: Ostensible agency supplies I with real power of representation for this transaction.

V. Final answer: As a result, O is bound to X for the EUR 2,000 order; O's remedy, if any, lies internally against I.

### Question 14 (Case)

**Facts:** Boutique owner B never assigns anyone a formal cashier or sales title. Sales assistant J, hired under a contract covering stock organizing only (no customer-facing sales duties), steps behind the register during a rush and sells customer Y a jacket at the marked price, processing payment through the store's standard till and handing over a receipt. B later argues that J had no authority to sell anything, since her job was stock-keeping only.

**Question:** Did the boutique conclude a valid sale to Y?

**Model Answer:**

I. B's boutique could be bound to Y under Section 433 BGB if J effectively represented B, which requires testing Section 164 I BGB together with whether Section 56 HGB's shop-authority rule supplies the needed power of representation despite J's contractually limited role.

II. Requirements:
- J's own declaration and publicity (acting within the store, at the register).
- Power of representation: check actual/declared authority first, then Section 56 HGB customary-shop-sale authority.
- Any facts cutting against Y's good-faith reliance.

III. Application:
- Own declaration and publicity: J rings up the sale and hands over the jacket at the register inside B's boutique — an ordinary retail transaction recognizably made for the shop, satisfying own declaration and publicity.
- Actual/declared authority: J's employment contract covers stock organizing only, so no explicit declared authority under Section 167 I BGB covers sales.
- Section 56 HGB: Questionable is whether Section 56 HGB nonetheless supplies authority because J was employed and acting within a commercial shop for a customary, everyday retail-type transaction. This is supported by the boutique being a typical retail business where a staff member processing a routine register sale is the customary appearance presented to any customer, and Y has no way to know J's internal job title is stock-only. Against this, one could argue J was never assigned register duties and B never intended her to sell. However, Section 56 HGB authority attaches to the objective appearance of an employee acting in a shop for a customary sale, not to the employee's internal job description, and Y could not be expected to inquire about internal titles before buying a jacket at a marked price. Consequently, Section 56 HGB authority applies.

IV. Interim result: J had power of representation under Section 56 HGB despite lacking sales duties under her employment contract.

V. Final answer: As a result, the boutique concluded a valid sale to Y; B cannot rely on J's internal job limitation to defeat the contract, though B may address the situation internally with J.

### Question 15 (Case)

**Facts:** P revokes A's authority to negotiate deals after A leaves the company but only tells A internally, never informing longtime client Z, who has ordered goods through A many times before. Two weeks after leaving, A — still holding old business cards and P's former letterhead — visits Z and "confirms" a new order in P's name for EUR 5,000. Z, unaware anything changed, agrees. P refuses to honor the order, arguing A's authority had already ended.

**Question:** Is P bound to Z for the EUR 5,000 order?

**Model Answer:**

I. P could be bound to Z if A still had effective power of representation at the time of the order despite the internal revocation, which turns on third-party protection under Sections 168, 170, 173 BGB.

II. Requirements:
- A's own declaration and publicity.
- Authority formally ended (Section 168 BGB revocation).
- Whether Z is nonetheless protected because withdrawal was only declared to A, not to Z, and Z neither knew nor should have known (Sections 170, 173 BGB).

III. Application:
- Own declaration/publicity: A confirms the order in P's name using P's old letterhead, so both elements are met.
- Revocation: P validly revoked A's authority under Section 168 BGB; internally, A no longer has authority.
- Third-party protection: Questionable is whether Z is protected despite the revocation. This is supported by the fact that P only communicated the withdrawal internally to A and never informed Z, a longtime client who had dealt with A many times before and had every reason to expect A still represented P. Against this, one could argue A's departure and the passage of two weeks should have prompted Z to double-check. However, Sections 170 and 173 BGB place the burden on the principal to notify third parties who relied on a previously-exercised authority; Z had no actual knowledge of the revocation and nothing on the facts suggests Z should have known, since P made no external announcement. Consequently, Z remains protected.

IV. Interim result: A's authority, though internally revoked, remains effective against P in relation to Z under Sections 170, 173 BGB.

V. Final answer: As a result, P is bound to Z for the EUR 5,000 order; P's recourse is only against A internally for acting after authority had ended.

### Question 16 (Case — integrates Week 07 + Week 12-13)

**Facts:** Bau-Tech GmbH's managing director L has statutory representation power under Section 35 GmbHG. The articles of association internally cap L's authority to sign purchase contracts at EUR 20,000 without shareholder approval — this cap is not publicly disclosed and is unknown to the company's regular steel supplier, ST GmbH. Separately, L signs a contract "on behalf of Bau-Tech GmbH" to buy a used company car from himself personally for EUR 12,000, a fair price, without first asking the other shareholders. When the shareholders find out, they object to both: (a) the car purchase, and (b) a EUR 35,000 steel order L signed with ST GmbH that exceeds the internal EUR 20,000 cap.

**Question:** Is Bau-Tech GmbH bound to (a) buy L's car, and (b) pay ST GmbH for the steel order?

**Model Answer:**

I. Bau-Tech GmbH's liability for each transaction depends on whether L had effective power of representation under Section 164 I BGB and, for organ representation, Sections 35, 37 GmbHG, with transaction (a) additionally engaging Section 181 BGB self-dealing.

II. Requirements:
- L acts as organ representative under Section 35 GmbHG (statutory power).
- Section 37 GmbHG: internal limits generally do not bind externally.
- Transaction (a): Section 181 BGB self-dealing and its four exceptions.
- Transaction (b): whether the EUR 20,000 internal cap defeats ST GmbH's reliance.

III. Application:
- (a) Car purchase: L represents Bau-Tech GmbH under Section 35 GmbHG while also being personally the seller — this is self-contracting, triggering Section 181 BGB. Questionable is whether an exception applies. This is supported only by the fair market price of EUR 12,000. Against this, there is no prior shareholder permission, no later ratification (the shareholders are objecting, not approving), the transaction is not the mere performance of an existing obligation, and it is not legally advantageous to Bau-Tech GmbH alone since the GmbH must pay EUR 12,000. However, a fair price alone does not satisfy any of the four Section 181 exceptions. Consequently, L lacked effective power of representation for the car purchase.
- (b) Steel order: L represents Bau-Tech GmbH under Section 35 GmbHG for an ordinary transaction with ST GmbH, an independent counterparty — no self-dealing issue arises here. Questionable is whether the internal EUR 20,000 cap defeats L's authority toward ST GmbH. This is supported by the shareholders' clear internal restriction in the articles. Against this, Section 37 GmbHG provides that internal limitations on a director's representation power generally do not affect its external effect — mirroring the agency-law can-do/may-do split under Section 164 I BGB — and nothing on the facts shows ST GmbH knew or should have known of the unpublished cap. However, since ST GmbH is an ordinary independent third party dealing at arm's length with no facts suggesting bad faith, Section 37 GmbHG protects the transaction's external validity despite the internal breach. Consequently, the internal cap does not defeat L's authority toward ST GmbH.

IV. Interim results: (a) L's self-dealing car purchase fell outside his power of representation absent any Section 181 exception; (b) L's steel order remains within his external power of representation despite breaching the internal cap.

V. Final answer: As a result, Bau-Tech GmbH is not bound to buy L's car (provisionally invalid unless later ratified under Section 177 BGB), but Bau-Tech GmbH is bound to pay ST GmbH for the EUR 35,000 steel order; the shareholders' remedy against L for exceeding the internal cap lies only internally, e.g. under Section 43 I GmbHG duty-of-care/loyalty liability, not against ST GmbH.

### Question 17 (Case — integrates Week 07 + Week 03)

**Facts:** Furniture retailer R authorizes sales agent A, by declaration under Section 167 I BGB, to accept offers for the "Nordic" sofa line strictly at the listed catalog price of EUR 900, with no discretion over delivery terms. Customer C sends R's shop a written offer to buy a Nordic sofa for EUR 900 "with free delivery included." A, eager to close the sale and clearly stating "I accept on behalf of R," replies "Agreed, EUR 900, but delivery costs EUR 50 extra." C never responds further. Weeks later, C arrives expecting a concluded contract with free delivery; R claims no contract was ever formed, and separately argues that even if A's reply could be seen as forming something, A had no authority to change the delivery terms.

**Question:** Was a contract concluded between R and C, and is R bound by A's reply?

**Model Answer:**

I. Whether R and C concluded a contract depends first on ordinary offer/acceptance analysis under Sections 145, 150 II BGB, and second — since A, not R, made the relevant declaration — on whether A effectively represented R for that declaration under Section 164 I BGB.

II. Requirements:
- C's written offer must meet essentialia negotii (Section 145 BGB).
- A's reply must be tested under Section 150 II BGB (modified acceptance = rejection + new offer).
- A's reply, if a new offer, is attributed to R only if A had own declaration, publicity, and power of representation (Section 164 I BGB).
- Power of representation is scoped by A's actual declared authority (accept at catalog price only).

III. Application:
- C's offer: naming the sofa, the EUR 900 price, and free delivery gives sufficiently definite essentialia negotii, so C made a valid offer under Section 145 BGB.
- A's reply as modified acceptance: A's reply changes a term of C's offer (adding EUR 50 for delivery instead of free delivery). Under Section 150 II BGB, any change to the offer's terms counts as rejection of the original offer and a new counteroffer, regardless of A using the word "agreed." So no contract formed on C's original terms.
- Attribution of A's counteroffer to R: A makes an own declaration and clearly states she acts on R's behalf, satisfying own declaration and publicity. Questionable is whether A had power of representation to make this specific counteroffer. This is supported by A's actual declared authority extending only to accepting the EUR 900 catalog price, with no discretion over delivery terms. Against this, one could argue closing sales generally implies some flexibility on ancillary terms like delivery. However, the facts expressly limit A's authority to accepting at the listed price, and altering delivery terms goes beyond that scope. Consequently, A acted without authority in making the counteroffer.
- Consequence: R is not automatically bound by A's unauthorized counteroffer; R could ratify it under Section 177 I BGB, but nothing on the facts shows ratification, and in any event C never accepted A's counteroffer, so no contract exists on those terms either.

IV. Interim result: C's original offer was rejected by A's modified reply under Section 150 II BGB; A's own counteroffer exceeded her authority and never bound R, and C never accepted it in any event.

V. Final answer: As a result, no contract was concluded between R and C — not on C's original free-delivery terms (rejected via modified acceptance) and not on A's EUR 50-delivery counteroffer (unauthorized, and never accepted by C); R is not bound to sell C the sofa on the facts as they stand.

### Question 18 (Case)

**Facts:** Art broker K holds declared authority (Section 167 I BGB) from gallery owner G to sell paintings on G's behalf, and separately holds declared authority from collector H to buy paintings on H's behalf — two independent agency relationships, with the dual role disclosed to neither principal. K arranges the sale of one of G's paintings to H for EUR 25,000, signing the sale-purchase contract "on behalf of G" as seller's representative and "on behalf of H" as buyer's representative in the same document. When G later learns K also represented H, G refuses to deliver the painting, arguing the contract cannot bind her.

**Question:** Is the sale-purchase contract between G and H valid?

**Model Answer:**

I. The contract's validity depends on whether K could effectively represent both G and H under Section 164 I BGB despite Section 181 BGB's restriction on multiple representation.

II. Requirements:
- K acts as representative for both sides of the same transaction (multiple representation).
- Section 181 BGB is triggered.
- Check the four exceptions: prior permission, later ratification, mere performance of an existing obligation, legal advantage only for the principal.

III. Application:
- Multiple representation: K signs the same contract as representative for both seller G and buyer H, so K represents both sides of one transaction — the second form of Section 181 BGB, distinct from self-contracting.
- Exceptions: Questionable is whether any exception rescues the transaction. This is supported by nothing specific on the facts beyond the sale appearing ordinary. Against this, there is no indication G gave K prior permission to also act for H, no later ratification (G refuses once she learns of the dual role), the transaction is not the mere performance of a pre-existing obligation, and it is not legally advantageous only to G since G must actually deliver the painting. However, with none of the four exceptions met, Section 181 BGB applies in full.

IV. Interim result: K lacked effective power of representation for the sale due to unresolved multiple representation under Section 181 BGB.

V. Final answer: As a result, the sale-purchase contract is provisionally invalid; it cannot bind G (or H) unless one or both principals later ratify it under Section 177 BGB.

### Question 19

Compare Section 164 II BGB and Section 179 BGB as agency-law problems.

**Model Answer (Compare Schema):** Section 164 II BGB is a publicity failure: the acting person never showed, expressly or by circumstances, that they intended to act in someone else's name, so the law disregards any private intent to represent another and treats the actor as bound personally to the transaction — the analysis never reaches the question of authority at all. Section 179 BGB is an authority failure: the acting person did disclose that they were acting for a principal (publicity is satisfied), but lacked power of representation for the transaction, so the contract is provisionally ineffective pending the principal's ratification under Section 177 I BGB, and if the principal refuses, the third party may claim performance or damages from the unauthorized agent personally under Section 179 I BGB. Contrast example: an employee who quietly intends to buy fuel "for the boss" without ever saying so is a Section 164 II case — the employee is bound personally regardless of the missing authority question. An employee who explicitly says "I'm buying this for the boss" but was only ever authorized to buy snacks, not fuel, is a Section 179 case — the disclosed representative capacity is not in doubt, only the scope of authority. Exam trap: do not skip straight to a Section 179 authority analysis without first confirming publicity is even met — if publicity fails, the case ends at Section 164 II and Section 179 liability is never reached, because Section 179 presupposes the agent's disclosed representative capacity was already established.

### Question 20

Explain what Section 166 BGB actually governs, and why it is not the answer to an internal-instruction-breach question.

**Model Answer:**
1. Section 166 BGB is the knowledge-attribution rule in agency law: where the legal effect of a declaration of intent depends on the presence, absence, or particular knowledge of certain circumstances, it is the agent's — not the principal's — knowledge or culpable ignorance that counts, since the agent is the one who actually forms and expresses the relevant declaration.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 166 BGB.
3. Requirements/features: (1) a declaration of intent was made through an agent under effective representation; (2) the legal consequence of that declaration turns on knowledge of some fact or a defect of intent (e.g., knowing about a defect, acting in bad faith); (3) it is the agent's actual knowledge or the agent's negligent ignorance that is imputed to the principal for that purpose, not the principal's own state of mind — subject to the exception that a principal who gave specific instructions cannot hide behind an agent who carried them out in ignorance of facts the principal actually knew.
4. Example: a principal who has no idea a car has a hidden defect authorizes an agent to sell it; if the agent independently knows about the defect and stays silent, the agent's knowledge is imputed to the principal for purposes of a fraudulent-concealment analysis, even though the principal personally never knew.
5. Trap: Section 166 BGB is not the anchor for an ordinary internal-instruction breach (e.g., an agent selling below an internally set minimum price) — that scenario stays inside the agency "can do" (external, binds the principal) versus "may do" (internal, recourse against the agent through the underlying employment/mandate/service relationship) framework; reaching for Section 166 there is a common misrouting error.

### Question 21 (Case)

**Facts:** Fresh-GmbH has two managing directors, X and Y, jointly registered in the commercial register with joint representation as the default rule in the articles of association — no provision grants either director sole signing power. Supplier V, unaware of the joint-representation requirement, negotiates a EUR 40,000 delivery contract only with X, who signs "on behalf of Fresh-GmbH" alone, without consulting Y. When Y discovers the signed contract, Y refuses to approve it, believing the terms are unfavorable.

**Question:** Is Fresh-GmbH bound to V under the EUR 40,000 contract?

**Model Answer:**

I. Fresh-GmbH's liability to V depends on whether X alone had effective power of representation under Section 164 I BGB in conjunction with Section 35 GmbHG's organ-representation rule.

II. Requirements:
- X made an own declaration of intent in Fresh-GmbH's name (publicity satisfied by signing "on behalf of" the company).
- Power of representation for a GmbH organ is governed by Section 35 GmbHG; with several directors, joint representation is the statutory default unless the articles of association provide otherwise.
- Consequence if X's sole signature does not meet the applicable representation rule.

III. Application:
- Own declaration and publicity: X forms and signs a declaration explicitly for Fresh-GmbH, so both elements are met without difficulty.
- Power of representation: Questionable is whether X alone could bind Fresh-GmbH. This is supported by X's status as a duly registered managing director with organ representation power in principle. Against this, the articles of association set joint representation as the default, and nothing in the facts shows any provision granting X sole signing power. However, Section 35 GmbHG's joint-representation default means a single director's signature does not, by itself, satisfy the power-of-representation element unless the articles depart from that default — and the facts state no such departure exists. Consequently, X lacked power of representation to bind Fresh-GmbH alone.

IV. Interim result: X's sole signature did not meet Fresh-GmbH's joint-representation requirement, so X lacked effective power of representation for this transaction.

V. Final answer: As a result, Fresh-GmbH is not bound to V under the EUR 40,000 contract unless Y (or both directors jointly) later ratifies it under Section 177 I BGB; since Y refuses, the contract remains provisionally ineffective, and V's recourse, if any, lies against X personally under Section 179 BGB.

### Question 22 (Case)

**Facts:** Procurement manager J holds broad, externally communicated authority to sign supply contracts for MetalWorks GmbH up to EUR 100,000. Supplier K secretly agrees to pay J a personal EUR 5,000 kickback if J signs an inflated EUR 90,000 order for goods actually worth EUR 60,000, and J does so, signing clearly "on behalf of MetalWorks GmbH." MetalWorks GmbH later discovers the kickback arrangement and refuses to pay the EUR 90,000, arguing the contract cannot bind it despite J's apparent authority.

**Question:** Is MetalWorks GmbH bound to pay K the EUR 90,000?

**Model Answer:**

I. MetalWorks GmbH's liability depends on whether J's formal power of representation under Section 164 I BGB is defeated by the collusive arrangement with K under Section 138 BGB.

II. Requirements:
- J made an own declaration of intent in MetalWorks GmbH's name, with externally communicated power of representation covering this transaction.
- Ordinary "can do vs. may do" analysis would treat J's authority as intact even if J's conduct also breaches internal duties.
- Collusion between the agent and the third party can override formal authority and void the transaction under Section 138 BGB (violation of public policy/good morals).

III. Application:
- Own declaration, publicity, and formal authority: J signs explicitly for MetalWorks GmbH and holds externally communicated authority up to EUR 100,000, a limit the EUR 90,000 order does not exceed, so on an ordinary agency analysis J's declaration would bind MetalWorks GmbH.
- Collusion: Questionable is whether the secret kickback arrangement defeats this otherwise-valid authority. This is supported by K's active participation in a scheme where J deliberately signs an inflated order in exchange for a personal payment — this is not a unilateral breach of an internal instruction that K is merely unaware of, but a two-sided arrangement where K knowingly helps J betray MetalWorks GmbH's interest. Against this, one could argue the price still falls within J's externally communicated authority limit, so the "can do" boundary is nominally intact. However, ordinary internal/external protection exists to protect good-faith third parties, not third parties who actively collude with the agent against the principal's interest; where agent and third party collude to harm the principal, the transaction offends public policy and falls under Section 138 BGB regardless of formal authority. Consequently, the collusion defeats J's otherwise-valid power of representation for this transaction.

IV. Interim result: The collusive kickback arrangement between J and K voids the transaction under Section 138 BGB, notwithstanding J's formal, externally communicated authority.

V. Final answer: As a result, MetalWorks GmbH is not bound to pay K the EUR 90,000; the contract is void, and MetalWorks GmbH's remedies against J (internally) and potentially against K remain separate from any claim under the void supply contract.

### Question 23 (Case)

**Facts:** At a self-service gas station, driver N fills up a company van registered to Logistics-Plus GmbH and pays cash at the counter without saying a word about who owns the van or on whose behalf she is buying the fuel. Attendant P sees the company livery on the van but has no idea whether N is buying for herself or for her employer. Two days later, N's employer disputes the fuel bill, claiming N had no authority to buy fuel and that Logistics-Plus GmbH never contracted with the gas station at all.

**Question:** Did Logistics-Plus GmbH conclude the fuel purchase contract with the gas station, or is N personally bound?

**Model Answer:**

I. Whether Logistics-Plus GmbH or N is the contracting party for the fuel purchase depends on whether the publicity requirement of Section 164 I BGB is met or excused for this transaction.

II. Requirements:
- N made an own declaration of intent to buy the fuel.
- Publicity: P must be able to recognize, expressly or by circumstances, that N acts for Logistics-Plus GmbH rather than personally.
- Possible substitute for publicity in an immediate, low-stakes cash transaction where the buyer's identity is economically irrelevant to the other side ("transaction for whom it concerns").

III. Application:
- Own declaration: N forms her own will to buy the fuel and pay for it, so she is a candidate agent, not a mere messenger.
- Publicity: Questionable is whether P could recognize N was acting for Logistics-Plus GmbH. This is supported by the company livery visibly displayed on the van, which gives some circumstantial indication of a business context. Against this, N says nothing at all about acting for her employer, and P has no actual way of knowing whether N is buying fuel for herself, for a personal reason, or for the company — company-liveried vehicles can still be fueled by an employee for mixed or unclear purposes. However, this is exactly the kind of immediate, fully-settled cash transaction where the identity of the contracting party is arguably economically irrelevant to the gas station, which only cares that the pump price gets paid on the spot — a point on which course materials treat the "transaction for whom it concerns" substitute for publicity as arguable rather than automatic. Consequently, whether publicity is satisfied or excused here is a genuinely close call that should be argued both ways rather than asserted outright.

IV. Interim result: If the "transaction for whom it concerns" exception applies, Logistics-Plus GmbH is bound; if not, publicity fails and N is bound personally under Section 164 II BGB.

V. Final answer: As a result, the outcome turns on which side of this arguable publicity question is taken: on the stronger reading (immediate cash transaction, identity economically irrelevant), Logistics-Plus GmbH concluded the fuel purchase; on the stricter reading (no actual words or unambiguous circumstances showing agency), N is bound personally and Logistics-Plus GmbH owes nothing to the gas station.

### Question 24 (Case)

**Facts:** Sales manager R has externally communicated authority to sell inventory for Home-Deco GmbH, subject to an internal instruction never to sell below EUR 15,000 per unit for the "Alpine" cabinet line — an instruction R once mentioned out loud to buyer T during an earlier, unrelated negotiation where T happened to be present. Months later, R sells T an Alpine cabinet for EUR 11,000, and T, remembering the EUR 15,000 floor R had mentioned before, accepts the discounted price without asking any questions. Home-Deco GmbH refuses to honor the sale.

**Question:** Is Home-Deco GmbH bound to sell T the cabinet for EUR 11,000?

**Model Answer:**

I. Home-Deco GmbH's liability depends on whether R's power of representation, though formally intact under the ordinary internal/external split, is defeated here because T knew of the internal EUR 15,000 floor.

II. Requirements:
- R made an own declaration of intent in Home-Deco GmbH's name with externally communicated authority to sell inventory.
- Ordinarily, an internal-instruction breach (abuse of existing authority) still binds the principal externally, leaving only an internal remedy against the agent.
- Exception: if the third party actually knows the agent is exceeding the internal limit, the third party's usual protection weakens.

III. Application:
- Own declaration, publicity, and formal external authority: R sells the cabinet explicitly for Home-Deco GmbH and holds general external authority to sell inventory, so on the ordinary "can do" analysis R's declaration would bind Home-Deco GmbH.
- Internal-limit breach: R's EUR 11,000 price breaches the internal EUR 15,000 floor for the Alpine line, which is ordinarily just an internal "may do" problem that does not affect the external validity of the sale.
- T's knowledge: Questionable is whether T's actual awareness of the EUR 15,000 floor changes the result. This is supported by the facts stating R had personally told T about the internal instruction on an earlier occasion, and T explicitly recalls it at the time of this sale. Against this, one could argue the internal/external split exists precisely to protect third parties from having to investigate internal limits, and T did not affirmatively investigate anything here. However, that protective purpose only extends to third parties who neither knew nor should have known of the limit; a third party with actual knowledge of the specific internal restriction cannot claim the benefit of a rule designed to protect good-faith reliance, because there is no good faith to protect. Consequently, T's actual knowledge defeats reliance on R's ordinarily-intact external authority for this specific transaction.

IV. Interim result: R's external authority, though formally unaffected by an ordinary internal-instruction breach, does not protect T here because T actually knew of the EUR 15,000 floor.

V. Final answer: As a result, Home-Deco GmbH is not bound to sell T the cabinet for EUR 11,000; Home-Deco GmbH may refuse to honor the below-floor sale, and any consequence for R's breach remains an internal matter between R and Home-Deco GmbH.

### Question 25 (Case)

**Facts:** Antique collector Q instructs agent M, by declaration under Section 167 I BGB, to buy a specific 18th-century clock at an upcoming auction, up to EUR 8,000. At the auction, that clock is withdrawn, but M — without asking Q first — buys a different, comparable 18th-century clock instead for EUR 7,500, explaining the substitution to auctioneer S at the time of purchase. Q says nothing when M delivers the clock, displays it prominently in Q's collection room, and shows it to visiting friends over the following weeks without ever objecting to M or S.

**Question:** Is Q bound to pay S the EUR 7,500 for the substitute clock?

**Model Answer:**

I. Q could be bound to S for the EUR 7,500 clock if M's initially unauthorized purchase became effective through Q's later ratification under Section 177 I BGB.

II. Requirements:
- M made an own declaration of intent in Q's name (publicity satisfied via M's explanation to S).
- M's declared authority was limited to the specific withdrawn clock, so the substitute purchase exceeded that authority.
- Ratification under Section 177 I BGB can retroactively validate an unauthorized agent's transaction through the principal's approval, which can be given expressly or through unambiguous conduct.

III. Application:
- Own declaration and publicity: M forms his own declaration to buy the substitute clock and explains the situation to S, so both elements are satisfied.
- Power of representation: Q's declared authority under Section 167 I BGB was limited to the specific withdrawn clock up to EUR 8,000; a different, merely comparable clock falls outside that specific instruction, so M acted without authority for the substitute purchase.
- Ratification: Questionable is whether Q's conduct amounts to ratification. This is supported by Q accepting delivery of the clock, prominently displaying it in the collection room, and showing it to visitors over an extended period without any objection to M or S. Against this, one could argue Q never said a single word approving the purchase, so there is no express ratification. However, ratification under Section 177 I BGB does not require an express statement — unambiguous conduct consistent only with approval is sufficient, and openly integrating the clock into the collection and showcasing it to others for weeks is conduct incompatible with any intent to reject the purchase. Consequently, Q's conduct constitutes implicit ratification.

IV. Interim result: M's initially unauthorized substitute purchase was retroactively validated by Q's implicit ratification.

V. Final answer: As a result, Q is bound to pay S the EUR 7,500 for the substitute clock; the ratification cures M's lack of authority from the outset.

### Question 26 (Case — integrates Week 07 + Week 10)

**Facts:** Warehouse operator W GmbH authorizes employee F, by declaration under Section 167 I BGB, to sell a specific forklift stored in the warehouse to buyer Z and to hand over possession on the company's behalf. F meets Z, signs the sale agreement clearly "on behalf of W GmbH," and separately walks Z to the warehouse bay and physically hands over the forklift's keys. Later, W GmbH argues that F's authority under Section 164 I BGB only covers declarations of intent, so the physical handover of the forklift needs a separate, additional showing of agency authority that the facts do not establish, meaning ownership never passed to Z.

**Question:** Did ownership of the forklift pass from W GmbH to Z?

**Model Answer:**

I. Ownership passes to Z under Section 929 sentence 1 BGB if the agreement, delivery, continued agreement at delivery, and authorization elements are met, which requires separately analyzing how agency law applies to the agreement element versus the delivery element.

II. Requirements:
- Section 929 sentence 1 BGB: (1) agreement between owner and acquirer that ownership shall pass, (2) delivery of the thing, (3) the agreement still existing at the time of delivery, (4) the transferor's authorization to dispose.
- The agreement is a declaration of intent, so Section 164 I BGB agency rules (own declaration, publicity, power of representation) apply to test whether F's agreement bound W GmbH.
- Delivery is a real/factual act, not a declaration of intent, so Section 164 ff. does not apply directly to the handover itself; a representative's physical handover on the principal's behalf is instead a question of possession through an agent in possession under Section 855 BGB, not a second agency test.

III. Application:
- Agreement element: F, authorized under Section 167 I BGB to sell the forklift, makes his own declaration that ownership shall pass to Z and signs clearly on W GmbH's behalf — own declaration, publicity, and power of representation are all met, so the disposition agreement is effectively attributed to W GmbH under Section 164 I BGB.
- Delivery element: Questionable is whether F additionally needs separate agency authority to perform the physical handover. This is supported by W GmbH's argument that Section 164 I is agency's only gateway and nothing else should count without it. Against this, delivery under Section 929 sentence 1 BGB is a real act of physically transferring control over the thing, not a further declaration of intent — the course materials are explicit that agency rules govern the agreement, not the handover itself, and a person handing over an item on the owner's instruction is analyzed as holding or transferring possession on the owner's behalf under Section 855 BGB, not as needing a fresh Section 164 I test. However, since F was already validly authorized to sell and hand over the forklift by W GmbH's instruction, and F's physical handover of the keys is exactly the kind of factual act Section 855 BGB accommodates without requiring separate agency formalities, the delivery element is satisfied once F physically hands the forklift over to Z. Consequently, W GmbH's argument that a second agency showing is needed for delivery fails.
- Remaining elements: nothing in the facts suggests the agreement lapsed before delivery or that W GmbH lacked authorization to dispose of its own forklift, so those elements are met without further issue.

IV. Interim result: All four elements of Section 929 sentence 1 BGB are satisfied — the agreement was validly attributed to W GmbH via ordinary Section 164 I agency analysis, and the delivery was validly completed as a real act without needing a separate agency test.

V. Final answer: As a result, ownership of the forklift passed from W GmbH to Z; W GmbH's objection that delivery needed independent agency authority misapplies Section 164 I BGB to a real act it does not govern.
