# Week 07 Agency Practice Questions And Model Answers

Course: Business Law
Exam date: 2026-07-28
Source: week-07-agency.md, week-07-agency-cheatsheet-and-tricks.md

## Short-Answer / Theory Questions

### Question 1

State and apply the three requirements for effective agency under Section 164 I BGB.

**Model Answer:**
1. Effective agency is the legal institute that lets an agent's own declaration of intent bind a principal directly toward a third party, defined in Section 164 I BGB.
2. Statutory anchor: Section 164 I BGB.
3. Requirements: (1) an own declaration of intent by the agent, (2) publicity — the declaration is recognizably made in the principal's name, (3) power of representation.
4. Example: an authorized sales employee negotiates and closes a sale in the shop owner's name.
5. Consequence/trap: if all three are met, the principal and third party become the contracting parties directly and the agent drops out of the contract; missing any single element defeats agency entirely — it does not partially bind the principal.

### Question 2

Compare an agent and a messenger.

**Model Answer:**
1. An agent forms and declares their own legal will within their authority (Section 164 I BGB); a messenger merely transmits another person's already-formed, fixed declaration without adding any will of their own.
2. Trigger: agent status is tested under Section 164 I BGB (own declaration, publicity, authority); messenger status is tested under the rules on transmission of a declaration (Section 120 BGB governs transmission errors).
3. Consequence: an effective agent's declaration binds the principal directly; a messenger's transmitted declaration is legally the principal's own declaration, so its validity turns on whether it was transmitted correctly, not on any authority analysis.
4. Contrast example: an employee negotiating price and terms with a customer is an agent; an employee reading a pre-written letter from the owner word-for-word to a customer is a messenger.
5. Exam warning: do not run the Section 164 I agency test on a messenger — analyze transmission (Section 120) instead, since a messenger never formed an "own will" to test for publicity or authority.

### Question 3

Compare agency by estoppel and ostensible agency.

**Model Answer:**
1. Agency by estoppel exists where the principal knows a person appears to act as their agent and tolerates it; ostensible agency exists where the principal does not know but should have known and prevented the appearance through due care.
2. Trigger: both rest on a third party's good-faith reliance on an appearance of authority created or tolerated by the principal, rather than on an actual declaration under Section 167 I BGB.
3. Consequence: in both cases the law treats the agent as having real power of representation, protecting the good-faith third party's reliance despite the absence of actual authorization.
4. Contrast example: a principal who watches an unauthorized helper repeatedly close sales in the shop and does nothing is bound by estoppel; a principal who never checks and would have discovered repeated unauthorized use of company letterhead and email with ordinary diligence is bound by ostensible agency.
5. Exam warning: a single isolated unauthorized act is normally not enough for either route — the facts must show a pattern or appearance attributable to the principal plus reasonable reliance; do not conflate general negligence with either specific doctrine.

### Question 4

List the requirements (exceptions) that allow a self-dealing transaction to stand despite Section 181 BGB.

**Model Answer:**
The exceptions are:
1. Prior permission — the principal authorized the self-dealing transaction in advance.
2. Later ratification — the principal ratifies the transaction after the fact under Section 177 BGB.
3. Mere performance of an existing obligation — the transaction only fulfills a debt that already exists, so no fresh conflict of interest arises.
4. Legal advantage only for the principal — the transaction brings the principal solely a legal benefit with no corresponding burden.

If none of these applies, the self-dealing or multiple-representation transaction is provisionally invalid and cannot bind the principal. Section 181 exists because an agent who is also the counterparty (or who represents both sides) has a structural conflict of interest, since there is no independent party bargaining against the principal's interest.

## Case / Legal-Opinion Questions

### Question 5 (Case)

**Facts:** Retailer R owns a bookstore. R's cousin K sometimes helps out unpaid during busy weekends but has no formal employment contract or job title. One Saturday, a regular customer W asks K to order a rare first-edition book. K agrees to source it and quotes a price of EUR 400, without mentioning R or the bookstore at any point during the conversation — W has only ever met K as "R's cousin who sometimes hangs around" and has no idea K is acting on the bookstore's behalf. K genuinely intends the transaction to be for the bookstore. Three weeks later, R obtains the book, but W refuses to pay the EUR 400, denying she ever agreed to buy anything from R's bookstore.

**Question:** Does R have a claim against W for payment of EUR 400 under Section 433 II BGB?

**Model Answer:**

I. R could have a claim against W for payment of EUR 400 under Section 433 II BGB if R and W concluded a valid purchase contract, which in turn depends on whether K effectively represented R under Section 164 I BGB when negotiating with W.

II. Requirements:
- Contract requires two matching declarations of intent (offer and acceptance).
- If K, not R, made the relevant declaration, effective representation requires: (1) own declaration of intent, (2) publicity, (3) power of representation.
- Legal consequence if representation fails.

III. Application:
- Own declaration: K forms and expresses her own will to sell the book for EUR 400 to W — this is not a mere transmission of R's pre-fixed words, so K is a candidate agent, not a messenger.
- Publicity: Questionable is whether K acted recognizably in R's name. This is supported by K's genuine intent to act for the bookstore and her habit of helping out there. Against this, one could argue W had no way to recognize this from the conversation — K never mentioned R or the bookstore, and W only knew K informally, not as someone acting for the bookstore in this transaction. However, publicity requires that the third party can recognize from the circumstances that the declaration is made for someone else; a private, undisclosed intention is not enough under Section 164 II BGB. Consequently, publicity is not met.
- Power of representation: becomes irrelevant once publicity fails, since all three elements of Section 164 I BGB are cumulative.

IV. Interim result: K did not effectively represent R under Section 164 I BGB; Section 164 II BGB applies instead, and K's hidden intent to act for R is disregarded.

V. Final answer: As a result, no contract was concluded between R and W; K herself is likely treated as the contracting party, so R has no claim against W for the EUR 400 under Section 433 II BGB.

### Question 6 (Case)

**Facts:** Bakery owner B employs C exclusively as a delivery driver; C has no sales authority under her employment contract. While delivering an order, C is approached by a passing café owner, D, who urgently wants to buy B's entire remaining stock of pastries for a same-day event. C, eager to help, agrees to sell D the pastries for EUR 300, explicitly telling D "I'm selling these on behalf of the bakery." When B learns of the sale, B is furious because the pastries were reserved for a different regular customer, and B refuses to honor the deal. D, having already promised the pastries to his own event guests, wants to know his options.

**Question:** Can D demand performance (delivery of the pastries) from C personally under Section 179 I BGB?

**Model Answer:**

I. D could have a claim against C for performance under Section 179 I BGB if C acted as an unauthorized agent for B and B has refused to ratify the transaction.

II. Requirements:
- C made an own declaration of intent in B's name (candidate agency under Section 164 I BGB).
- C lacked power of representation for this type of transaction.
- B refused ratification under Section 177 I BGB.
- D chooses performance (rather than damages) under Section 179 I BGB.

III. Application:
- Own declaration and publicity: C forms her own declaration to sell the pastries and explicitly states she is selling "on behalf of the bakery," so both own declaration and publicity are met.
- Power of representation: Questionable is whether C's authority as a delivery driver extends to sales. This is supported by the fact that C's employment contract covers delivery only. Against this, one could argue any employee visibly representing the business might appear to carry some authority. However, C's role and the circumstances (an improvised curbside sale of the entire remaining stock) give no objective indication of sales authority, and there are no facts suggesting a pattern of C making sales that B tolerated or should have noticed, so no reliance-based authority arises either. Consequently, C lacked power of representation.
- Ratification: B refuses to ratify because the pastries were reserved for someone else, satisfying Section 177 I BGB's refusal condition.
- Section 179 I choice: D may choose between performance and damages; the facts show D wants the pastries themselves.

IV. Interim result: C acted as an unauthorized agent; B's refusal to ratify triggers Section 179 I BGB liability against C personally.

V. Final answer: As a result, D can demand performance (delivery of the pastries) from C personally under Section 179 I BGB, since C, not B, is now treated as bound by the sale.

### Question 7 (Case)

**Facts:** GmbH managing director M has broad, unrestricted power of representation for TechParts GmbH, including authority to buy and sell company equipment. M personally owns an old delivery van that the company genuinely needs. Without informing the other shareholders or seeking any prior approval, M signs a purchase contract "on behalf of TechParts GmbH" to buy his own van for EUR 15,000 — a fair market price. When the other shareholders discover the deal, they argue the contract cannot bind the company because M contracted with himself.

**Question:** Is the purchase contract between M (as seller) and TechParts GmbH (as buyer, represented by M) valid?

**Model Answer:**

I. The contract's validity depends on whether M could effectively represent TechParts GmbH under Section 164 I BGB despite Section 181 BGB's restriction on self-dealing.

II. Requirements:
- M acted as TechParts GmbH's representative (organ representation under Section 35 GmbHG).
- M is personally the counterparty to the transaction (self-contracting).
- Section 181 BGB is triggered.
- Check whether an exception applies: prior permission, later ratification, mere performance of an existing obligation, or legal advantage only for the principal.

III. Application:
- Representative capacity: M has organ representation power for TechParts GmbH under Section 35 GmbHG, so M can, in principle, bind the company.
- Self-contracting: M personally sells the van while simultaneously representing the buyer (TechParts GmbH), so M stands on both sides of the same transaction — this is self-contracting, not merely an internal-instruction issue.
- Exceptions: Questionable is whether any Section 181 exception applies. This is supported by the fact that the price was fair and the company genuinely needed the van. Against this, one could argue a fair price does not by itself amount to prior permission or later ratification, and the facts show no advance authorization from the shareholders and no subsequent approval — they only object after discovering the deal. The transaction is also not the mere performance of an existing obligation, and it is not legally advantageous only for TechParts GmbH, since the company must pay EUR 15,000. However, with no exception on the facts, Section 181 BGB applies in full.
- Consequence: because none of the four exceptions is met, M's self-dealing falls outside his power of representation for this specific transaction.

IV. Interim result: M lacked effective power of representation for this transaction due to Section 181 BGB, notwithstanding his general organ authority.

V. Final answer: As a result, the purchase contract is provisionally invalid; it cannot bind TechParts GmbH unless the company later ratifies it under Section 177 BGB.

### Question 8 (Case — integrates Week 07 + Week 11)

**Facts:** HandelsWaren GmbH is a mid-size wholesale trading company (a merchant by legal form under Section 6 I HGB in conjunction with Section 13 III GmbHG). The managing director grants employee N formal Prokura, duly registered in the commercial register. Internally, the shareholders instruct N never to place single orders above EUR 50,000 without board sign-off — but this limit is never communicated to any customer or supplier. Acting under her Prokura, N signs an EUR 80,000 order with supplier Q for raw materials; Q has no knowledge of the internal EUR 50,000 limit and has dealt with N as HandelsWaren's authorized signatory before. When the shareholders discover the order, they refuse to accept the delivery, arguing N exceeded her authority.

**Question:** Is HandelsWaren GmbH bound to accept and pay for the EUR 80,000 order?

**Model Answer:**

I. HandelsWaren GmbH is bound to Q under the purchase contract if N effectively represented the company, which requires testing both ordinary agency under Section 164 I BGB and the HGB-specific Prokura rules, since HandelsWaren GmbH is a merchant.

II. Requirements:
- HandelsWaren GmbH qualifies as a merchant, triggering the HGB layer on top of the BGB baseline.
- N made an own declaration of intent in the company's name (Section 164 I BGB elements).
- N held Prokura, and the scope of Prokura under Sections 48-50 HGB determines whether the internal EUR 50,000 limit defeats her external power.
- Legal consequence for the EUR 80,000 order.

III. Application:
- Merchant status: HandelsWaren GmbH is a GmbH, so it is a merchant by legal form under Section 6 I HGB in conjunction with Section 13 III GmbHG, without needing to run the Section 1 HGB scale test. This triggers HGB rules, including Prokura, on top of the ordinary BGB agency framework.
- Own declaration and publicity: N forms her own declaration to place the order and signs as HandelsWaren's registered Prokurist, so both elements of Section 164 I BGB are satisfied. This could also have been analyzed as ordinary Handlungsvollmacht under Section 54 HGB, but N's status here is the broader, registered Prokura.
- Power of representation — Prokura scope: Questionable is whether the internal EUR 50,000 cap limits N's external authority. This is supported by the shareholders' clear internal instruction. Against this, Section 50 HGB provides that internal restrictions on Prokura are generally ineffective against third parties — the near-unrestrictable statutory scope of Prokura under Section 49 HGB exists precisely so that third parties like Q can rely on a registered Prokurist's authority without investigating internal limits. However, this protection can fail if the third party knew of the limit or the abuse was obvious; here, Q had no knowledge of the EUR 50,000 cap and had previously dealt with N as an authorized signatory, so nothing on the facts should have put Q on notice. Consequently, the internal limit does not defeat N's external power of representation.

IV. Interim result: N's Prokura gave her full external authority to place the EUR 80,000 order despite the internal cap, because Section 50 HGB protects Q's good-faith reliance.

V. Final answer: As a result, HandelsWaren GmbH is bound to accept and pay for the EUR 80,000 order under the contract with Q; the shareholders' remedy for N's breach of the internal instruction lies only in the internal employment/mandate relationship against N, not against Q.
