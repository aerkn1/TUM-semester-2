## Scope

This quick recall tested the core Agency exam route:

- agency triangle: principal, agent, third party;
- internal `may do` versus external `can do`;
- publicity versus authority;
- no-authority route through Sections 177 and 179 BGB;
- reliance-based authority, including agency by estoppel and ostensible agency;
- contracting with oneself under Section 181 BGB.

The main remaining weak spot is the no-publicity consequence: authority cannot rescue missing publicity.

## Prompt 1: Internal Limit

Coach prompt:

> P owns a furniture company. P tells A internally: "Do not sell any desk below EUR 10,000." P publicly presents A as sales manager. A tells customer T: "I sell you this desk on behalf of P for EUR 8,000." T does not know about the internal limit and accepts.
>
> 1. Who are principal, agent, and third party?
> 2. Is publicity satisfied?
> 3. Is this a `can do` problem or a `may do` problem?
> 4. Who is bound externally?
> 5. What can P potentially claim internally?

User answer:

> 1-P= furntiure company, a = sales manager , t = customer
> 2-yes, A mentions the sales on behalf of P
> 3-may do? since its violation of internal instruciton 
> 4-P is bound externally
> 5-section 166 BGB?

Feedback:

- Actor identification: green.
- Publicity: green.
- `can do` versus `may do`: green/yellow. The correct classification is `may do`, because A violated an internal instruction while still apparently having external authority.
- External binding: green.
- Internal claim route: yellow. Section 166 BGB is not the correct anchor here. Section 166 concerns attribution of knowledge or defects of intent through the representative. Here P may have internal recourse against A based on the internal relationship, such as employment, mandate, or service duties.

Refined answer:

```text
A had external power of representation, so P is bound externally to T. A violated the internal price instruction, so P may have internal recourse against A. Section 179 BGB does not apply because A had authority; Section 166 BGB is not the main issue.
```

## Prompt 2: No Publicity

Coach prompt:

> A privately wants to buy a laptop for P. A goes to T and says only: "I buy this laptop for EUR 1,000." T thinks A is buying personally. A never mentions P and the circumstances do not show a company purchase.
>
> 1. Which Section 164 I requirement fails?
> 2. Is P bound?
> 3. Who is likely treated as the contracting party?
> 4. Is this an authority problem or a publicity problem?

User answer:

> 1- 164 II BGB, no publicity 
> 2-Yes, p is still bound under power of representation against the customer
> 3-P and customer 
> 4-publicity

Feedback:

- Identified no publicity: yellow/green. More precise: the Section 164 I requirement "acting in the principal's name" fails; Section 164 II BGB states the consequence.
- Binding consequence: red/yellow. P is not bound merely because A may have authority. Authority cannot cure missing publicity.
- Contracting party: red/yellow. A is likely treated as contracting personally with T.
- Problem category: green. This is a publicity problem, not an authority problem.

Refined answer:

```text
Because A did not act recognizably in P's name, the publicity requirement under Section 164 I BGB is missing. Under Section 164 II BGB, A's hidden intention to act for P is irrelevant. P is not bound; A is likely the contracting party.
```

## Prompt 3: No Authority

Coach prompt:

> Warehouse worker S works for P's furniture company. S has no sales authority. A customer T calls, and S says: "I sell you 10 chairs on behalf of P." T accepts. P later hears about the deal.
>
> 1. Is publicity satisfied?
> 2. Which Section 164 I requirement fails?
> 3. Is P immediately bound?
> 4. What can P do under Section 177 BGB?
> 5. If P refuses, which risk may S face?

User answer:

> 1- yes, S mentions the sales on behalf of P 
> 2-declaration of intent without power of representation
> 3-it depends, based on the declaration of intent, p has the right for ratification
> 4-can ratify the contract
> 5-can be claimed by third party/customer under 179 bgb

Feedback:

- Publicity: green.
- Failed element: green with wording refinement. The failed element is power of representation. S still made an own declaration of intent in P's name.
- Immediate binding: yellow. P is not immediately bound; the transaction is pending until P ratifies or refuses.
- Ratification under Section 177 BGB: green.
- Section 179 BGB liability risk: green.

Refined answer:

```text
S made an own declaration of intent in P's name, so publicity is satisfied. However, S lacked power of representation, so P is not immediately bound. Under Section 177 BGB, P may ratify the contract. If P refuses ratification, S may be liable to T under Section 179 BGB.
```

## Prompt 4: Agency By Estoppel

Coach prompt:

> S is formally only a warehouse employee. For several weeks, S uses P's company email and order forms to sell furniture to customers. P sees the customer confirmations and does nothing. Later, T buys from S in good faith.
>
> 1. Is this regular authorization or reliance-based authority?
> 2. Is it agency by estoppel or ostensible agency?
> 3. Why?
> 4. What is the effect if the requirements are met?

User answer:

> reliance-based authority under agency by estoppel, S is not authorized by P but aware of actions taken by S. ı think section 242 BGB applies with goo faith

Feedback:

- Reliance-based authority: green.
- Agency by estoppel: green.
- Reasoning: green/yellow. P's knowledge and tolerance are the decisive facts.
- Statutory/theory anchor: green/yellow. Section 242 BGB/good faith is the course's background principle, but the exam answer should state the reliance structure clearly.

Refined answer:

```text
This is reliance-based authority, not regular authorization, because P did not expressly authorize S by declaration. It is agency by estoppel because P knew S was appearing and acting as agent and tolerated it. If T relied in good faith and the requirements are met, S is treated as having power of representation, so P can be bound to T.
```

## Prompt 5: Ostensible Agency

Coach prompt:

> S is formally only a warehouse employee. S uses P's company email, company order forms, and company phone line to sell furniture for weeks. P does not actually notice this, but a normal careful business owner would have noticed from the records. T buys from S in good faith.
>
> 1. Regular authorization or reliance-based authority?
> 2. Agency by estoppel or ostensible agency?
> 3. Why?
> 4. If requirements are met, what happens externally?

User answer:

> 1-reliance-based authrotiy
> 2-ostensible agency
> 3-P would have noticed it if carefully reiewed the order records
> 4-P becomes bound to T due to the good faith and the requirements are met

Feedback:

- Reliance-based authority: green.
- Ostensible agency: green.
- Reasoning: green. P did not know, but should have known with due care.
- External result: green. Use the phrase "T's good-faith reliance" for precision.

Refined answer:

```text
This is reliance-based authority. It is ostensible agency because P did not actually know of S's conduct, but P could and should have known with due care from the records and controls. If T relied in good faith and the other agency requirements are met, S is treated as having power of representation and P is bound externally to T.
```

## Prompt 6: Contracting With Oneself

Coach prompt:

> P authorizes A to sell P's used company car. A signs a contract selling the car from P to A personally for a very low price. P did not give permission for A to buy it herself.
>
> 1. What is this issue called?
> 2. Which section do you check?
> 3. Why is the law concerned?
> 4. Is this just a normal internal-instruction violation?
> 5. What exceptions could allow it?

User answer:

> 1- self-contracting
> 2-it must be 181 BGB 
> 3-conflict of interest as agent representing both side seller and consumer 
> 4-no due to there is not counterparty bargaing as the agent is also a customer that acts as external 
> 5-if it is permitted by principal in advance or ratifies it

Feedback:

- Issue label: green.
- Section 181 BGB: green.
- Conflict of interest: green.
- Not a normal internal-instruction violation: green.
- Exceptions: green/yellow. Prior permission and ratification are correct; also remember mere performance of an existing obligation and legally advantageous-only transactions.

Refined answer:

```text
A is self-contracting because A represents P while personally being the buyer. Section 181 BGB applies because A sits on both sides of the transaction and has a structural conflict of interest. This is not merely a `may do` breach; Section 181 can restrict A's external power. The transaction may still work if P permitted it in advance, ratifies it later, it merely performs an existing obligation, or it is legally advantageous only for P.
```
